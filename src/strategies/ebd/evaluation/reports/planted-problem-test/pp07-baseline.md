# Estimates of 1918 Flu Deaths Span 17 to 100 Million

No single figure is settled. The most repeated public figure is that the 1918–1920 pandemic killed **at least 50 million people worldwide**, a number the CDC gives with about **675,000 deaths in the United States** ([CDC history page](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/flu/pandemic-resources/1918-commemoration/1918-pandemic-history.htm)). That figure traces largely to Johnson and Mueller (2002), who put the toll at about 50 million and treated 100 million as an upper limit ([PMC, Spreeuwenberg et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7314216/)). The most recent quantitative reassessment is lower. Spreeuwenberg, Kroneman and Paget (2018) estimated **17.4 million deaths** in 1918 and 1919 combined ([PMC, Spreeuwenberg et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7314216/)). The gap between these figures is mostly a disagreement about India, and the reassessment has been challenged in print. The defensible answer is therefore a range, with about 50 million as the conventional central value and roughly 17 to 100 million as the published bounds. Records from individual countries confirm that official counts understate deaths, which argues against the lowest figures.

## The 50 million figure and its origins

The 50 million figure has been repeated for two decades. Taubenberger and Morens (2006) state that the pandemic "caused ≈50 million deaths worldwide," with total deaths "arguably as high as 100 million" ([CDC EID](https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/eid/article/12/1/05-0979_article)). Burnet, the Nobel-winning immunologist, also gave probably 50 million and possibly as high as 100 million, though the sources do not give the date of his estimate ([NCBI Bookshelf](https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sites/books/NBK22148/)). Johnson and Mueller themselves concluded that the figure was probably understated, and they stated that a truly accurate figure seems unlikely ever to be found ([ResearchGate, Johnson and Mueller 2002](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/11487892_Updating_the_Accounts_Global_Mortality_of_the_1918-1920_Spanish_Influenza_Pandemic)).

Earlier estimates were lower. A 1920s calculation put global mortality near 21.5 million, and Patterson and Pyle (1991) gave a range of 24.7 to 39.3 million, with about 30 million as their best estimate ([AJE, Reassessing the Global Mortality Burden](https://dx.doi.org/10.1093/aje/kwy191); [FactCheck.org](https://www.factcheck.org/2020/03/trumps-suspect-spanish-flu-claims/)). Johnson and Mueller describe a consistent upward revision of estimates over the decades ([Bull Hist Med, Johnson and Mueller 2002](https://www.soa.org/globalassets/assets/files/sections/johnson-mueller.pdf)).

| Estimate | Year | Figure | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Patterson and Pyle | 1991 | 24.7–39.3 million (best ≈30 million) | Geographic mortality tabulation ([AJE](https://dx.doi.org/10.1093/aje/kwy191)) |
| Johnson and Mueller | 2002 | ≈50 million; 100 million upper limit | Compiled registration, excess and regional data ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7314216/)) |
| Barro, Ursúa and Weng | 2020 | ≈39 million | City-level excess mortality ([NBER WP 27673](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w27673/w27673.pdf)); the NBER digest says ≈40 million ([NBER digest](https://www.nber.org/digest/may20/social-and-economic-impacts-1918-influenza-epidemic)) |
| Spreeuwenberg, Kroneman and Paget | 2018 | 17.4 million (15 million in 1918, 2.5 million in 1919) | Indirect excess mortality, 13 countries plus British India ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7314216/)) |
| Murray et al. (projection) | 2006 | 51–81 million (median 62 million) | Model for a 2004-sized population, not a historical count ([Medical News Today](https://www.medicalnewstoday.com/articles/59714)) |

Murray et al. are not competing measurements of the 1918 toll. Their figure applies 1918–20 death rates to today's population to describe a hypothetical future pandemic, and they describe it as a plausible upper limit ([Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/warning-a-flu-pandemic-to/)).

## The 2018 reassessment rests on India

Spreeuwenberg et al. used an indirect approach based on the Human Mortality Database and the Statistical Abstract for British India. Their central estimate was controlled for war and the underlying mortality trend ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7314216/)). They argued that totals of 25 million or more imply average death rates above those observed, and that 100 million is highly unlikely ([AJE](https://dx.doi.org/10.1093/aje/kwy191)). Their India value of 6.17 million is the crux. Chandra et al. argue in a 2019 letter that this figure relies uncritically on official statistics, which underreport deaths by **35–55% depending on region** ([OUP, Chandra letter](https://academic.oup.com/aje/article-abstract/188/7/1404/5368300)). The authors replied that they did not intend to assess India's burden, which they considered very challenging ([OUP, authors' reply](https://academic.oup.com/aje/article-abstract/188/7/1405/5368302?redirectedFrom=fulltext)).

Other Indian estimates are higher. Johnson and Mueller list a published toll of **18.5 million** for India ([Johnson & Mueller 2002](https://www.soa.org/globalassets/assets/files/sections/johnson-mueller.pdf)). Census-based estimates of total deaths reached 12–13 million ([Nezine, Census Report 1921](https://nezine.com/info/MlJSazlwRUt5cS9EU1hVWHBJMGJqdz09/census-data-on-epidemics:-an-estimated-1,11,340-died-in-spanish-flu-that-struck-assam-a-century-ago.html)). Mills' study of registered deaths gives 15–18.5 million ([Gateway House](https://www.gatewayhouse.in/1918-flu-india/)). Chandra et al. (2012) found at most 13.88 million deaths in the sampled districts, against 17.21 million under Davis's 1951 assumptions, and concluded Davis's figure was overstated by at least 24% ([Demography, Chandra et al. 2012](https://scholar.google.com/scholar_lookup?title=Mortality+from+the+influenza+pandemic+of+1918%E2%80%931919%3A+The+case+of+India&amp=&author=S.+Chandra&amp=&author=G.+Kuljanin&amp=&author=J.+Wray&amp=&publication_year=2012&amp=&journal=Demography&amp=&pages=857-865&amp=&doi=10.1007%2Fs13524-012-0116-x&amp=&pmid=22661303)). Because India's figure can shift the global total by several million, the 17.4 million estimate should be read as contingent on this single input.

## Country records show undercounting

Recorded counts in high-income countries came close to excess-mortality estimates, but the US data show incomplete coverage. Crosby's estimate is 675,000 deaths, of which about 550,000 were excess ([Richmond Fed](https://www.richmondfed.org/publications/research/econ_focus/2017/q2/economic_history)). Census Bureau tables record about 445,000 deaths in the last four months of 1918, with about 346,000 in the registration area ([US Census Bureau](https://census.gov/library/publications/1920/demo/1918-mortality-special-tables.html)). Only about three quarters of US states and territories reported mortality data at the time ([ScienceDirect](https://sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0042682218303313?via=ihub%3D)).

Low- and middle-income countries show larger gaps. Japan's official record of **257,363** deaths was revised to 257,000–481,000 in one study ([EID, Japan 1918–19](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3647405/)). Brazil has a recent reassessment of 266,000–587,000 deaths, well above an earlier estimate of about 35,240 that is now considered an underestimate ([Redalyc](https://www.redalyc.org/journal/3861/386173774008/html/); [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0264410X11002829)). For Java, a panel-data study estimated a loss of 4.26–4.37 million, more than twice the established figure for all of Indonesia ([ResearchGate, Spreeuwenberg letter](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/331480689_REReassessing_the_global_mortality_burden_of_the_1918_influenza_pandemic)). Johnson and Mueller give China 4–9.5 million and Africa about 2.375 million ([Johnson & Mueller 2002](https://www.soa.org/globalassets/assets/files/sections/johnson-mueller.pdf)). Western Samoa lost roughly 22% of its population, about 8,500 people ([SS Talune, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SS_Talune)), while mortality ranged from under 0.1% in Tasmania to 22% in Western Samoa ([Lancet Infectious Diseases](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1473309918301786)).

The regional totals in Johnson and Mueller's tables are also not fully consistent with their headline figure. Africa (about 2.375 million), the Americas (1.54 million), Asia (26–36 million), Europe (2.3 million) and Oceania (85,000) sum to roughly 32–42 million, below the stated global total of more than 48.8 million ([Johnson & Mueller 2002](https://www.soa.org/globalassets/assets/files/sections/johnson-mueller.pdf)). This is an inference from the published tables, and it shows how sensitive the 50 million figure is to its inputs.

## Why the estimates diverge

Three methodological issues explain most of the spread. First, excess-mortality estimates depend on the baseline for "normal" deaths. Spreeuwenberg et al. used the average of years before and after the pandemic, while Jedwab describes a regression on logged deaths from non-pandemic years ([GWU IIEP, Jedwab](https://www2.gwu.edu/~iiep/assets/docs/papers/2020WP/JedwabIIEP2020-15.pdf)). Second, influenza and pneumonia are hard to separate. Death certificates in the US did not reliably distinguish influenza from pneumonia ([PMC, US PHS canvass](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7893349/)), which is why many studies prefer all-cause mortality ([NBER WP 27673](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w27673/w27673.pdf)). Third, wartime and colonial reporting excluded rural and indigenous populations. Undercounting is the most consistent direction of bias across the sources. Corrections for individual countries, such as Japan and Java, have generally raised estimates rather than lowered them.

Age structure also matters. Taubenberger and Morens report that more than 99% of excess deaths were in people under 65 ([CDC EID](https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/eid/article/12/1/05-0979_article)). This is a descriptive finding, not a total, but it explains why the pandemic's toll was concentrated in young adults.

## Conclusion

The honest answer to "how many died" is a range: roughly **17 to 100 million**, with the commonly cited central value of about **50 million**. Readers should treat 50 million as the conventional figure, not a measured total. The 17.4 million reassessment is a serious challenge that is still disputed, especially on India, and no public-health body has formally adopted it. The 100 million figure is an upper bound that most authors treat as unlikely.

For a practical citation, the safest wording is "at least 50 million according to the CDC, with published estimates ranging from about 17 million to 100 million." Anyone who needs a single number should state the method and year, because the figures differ by more than fivefold depending on how India and undercounted regions are handled. The lesson for future pandemic planning is the same as for the historical record: recorded counts understate the toll, and modelled totals depend heavily on assumptions about places with poor data.
