# Most countries require prescriptions for systemic antibiotics

For oral and injectable antibiotics, the answer is yes in every major jurisdiction the research covers. A prescription is legally required in the United States, Canada, the UK, Australia, New Zealand, Germany, Italy, Spain, India, China, Japan, Brazil, Mexico, South Africa, the UAE and Saudi Arabia. The main exceptions are narrower than they first appear. Some **topical** products are sold over the counter, such as triple antibiotic ointments in the US ([DailyMed](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=28fff253-ddd4-4669-aa90-b4caf48c905f&audience=consumer)) and Polysporin ointment in Canada ([Health Canada DPD](https://health-products.canada.ca/dpd-bdpp/info?lang=eng&code=60546)). Pharmacists in England can supply specific antibiotics for **seven defined conditions** under Pharmacy First, which launched on 31 January 2024 ([UK government health blog](https://healthmedia.blog.gov.uk/2024/02/01/pharmacy-first-what-you-need-to-know/)), and France has allowed certain antibiotics to be dispensed without a prescription since 19 June 2024 for two narrow conditions ([Ramsay Services](https://www.ramsayservices.fr/symptomes/antibiotiques-sans-ordonnance-en-pharmacie-cest-desormais-possible-dans-certains-cas)). Where the law is clear, enforcement is often weak: studies from China, India and Saudi Arabia document widespread non-prescription sales. Confidence varies. Brazil and Mexico rest on primary or near-primary texts, while Japan, the UAE and Saudi Arabia rest mainly on secondary sources.

## The legal rule rests on prescription-only classification

In the United States, the rule comes from the statute rather than from a list naming each antibiotic. Prescription drugs must carry an "Rx only" label, and a drug subject to the prescription requirement is **misbranded** if it lacks that label before dispensing ([21 U.S.C. § 353, Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/21/353)). No FDA page was found that names every systemic antibiotic as Rx-only, so the classification is inferred from the statute and secondary sources. Canada works through its Prescription Drug List, which covers drugs outside the Controlled Drugs and Substances Act schedules ([Drug and Health Product Register](https://hpr-rps.hres.ca/pdl-phu.php)). In the EU, Directive 2001/83/EC lets member states set prescription-only sub-categories, so national rules are the operative source ([legislation.gov.uk copy of Directive 2001/83/EC](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/cy/eudr/2001/83/title/VI)). Australia lists antibiotics in Schedule 4, "Prescription only medicines," and the February 2026 Poisons Standard names amoxicillin in that entry ([Poisons Standard, February 2026](https://www.parliament.wa.gov.au/publications/tabledpapers.nsf/displaypaper/4211024c34f86183284c670448258db700043038/$file/tp-1024.pdf)). In India, the CDSCO states that antibiotics fall under Schedule H and H1 and may be sold only on a Registered Medical Practitioner's prescription ([CDSCO notice](https://cdsco.gov.in/opencms/resources/UploadCDSCOWeb/2018/UploadPublic_NoticesFiles/Combine.pdf)). China divides drugs into prescription-only and OTC categories, and prescription-only drugs may be sold only against a valid prescription ([IBA Healthcare Survey 2026](https://www.ibanet.org/document?id=Healthcare-Survey-2026-China)).

Some sources disagree on the detail. In New Zealand, Pharmac says "most" antibiotics are prescription-only ([Pharmac](https://www.pharmac.govt.nz/pharmaceutical-schedule/about-the-schedule)), while Healthify says all antibiotics taken by mouth need a prescription ([Healthify](https://healthify.nz/medicines-a-z/a/antibiotics)). For oral products the two agree. In South Africa, sources disagree on whether antibiotics are Schedule 3 or Schedule 4, though both are prescription-only ([Lancet Global Health, 2025](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/langlo/article/PIIS2214-109X(25)00394-8/fulltext); [MedxDRG](https://medxdrg.com/understanding-if-you-need-a-prescription-for-medication-in-south-africa)). The Indian Schedule H1 date is also contested: a CDSCO notice gives 30 August 2013 ([CDSCO notice](https://cdsco.gov.in/opencms/resources/UploadCDSCOWeb/2018/UploadPublic_NoticesFiles/Combine.pdf)), while a Kerala study says 2014 ([PMC9480811](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9480811/)). The CDSCO date is the more authoritative source.

## Topical and pharmacist-led routes are the real exceptions

The clearest over-the-counter exceptions are topical products, and they depend on product-specific status rather than a general antibiotic exemption. In the US, bacitracin, neomycin and polymyxin B combinations are marketed OTC under an FDA monograph, and a product outside the monograph would be treated as a prescription drug ([DailyMed, Padagis label](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=28fff253-ddd4-4669-aa90-b4caf48c905f&audience=consumer)). Canada's Polysporin Antibiotic Ointment is listed in the Drug Product Database as a non-prescription drug ([Health Canada DPD](https://health-products.canada.ca/dpd-bdpp/info?lang=eng&code=60546)). One Canadian online pharmacy and one commercial site conflict on whether Polysporin needs a prescription. The DPD entry is the more authoritative source and should be relied on.

Pharmacist-led supply is the more significant exception. In England, Pharmacy First lets community pharmacists supply antibiotics for seven conditions without a GP visit ([UK government health blog](https://healthmedia.blog.gov.uk/2024/02/01/pharmacy-first-what-you-need-to-know/)). The sources disagree on cost: Healthwatch says a pharmacy-supplied antibiotic is free, but a prescription-only medicine would attract usual charges unless the patient is exempt ([Healthwatch](https://www.healthwatch.co.uk/advice-and-information/2026-09-21/what-can-pharmacy-first-scheme-do-me)), while PharmSee says Pharmacy First supply carries no prescription charge ([PharmSee](https://pharmsee.co.uk/blog/post/antibiotics-from-pharmacy-without-gp-pharmacy-first-2026)). In France, the legal basis is Article 52 of the LFSS 2024 and decree no. 2024-550 of 17 June 2024 ([Pharmacie des Marelles](https://pharmaciedesmarelles.fr/est-ce-qu-un-pharmacien-peut-donner-des-antibiotiques/)). Strep throat is limited to patients aged 10 and over, and simple cystitis to women aged 16 to 65 ([Pharmacie des Marelles](https://pharmaciedesmarelles.fr/est-ce-qu-un-pharmacien-peut-donner-des-antibiotiques/)). In South Africa, pharmacists are legally authorised to prescribe Schedule 3 and 4 drugs, which makes them the only market in this set with a formal pharmacist prescribing role for antibiotics ([Lancet Global Health, 2025](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/langlo/article/PIIS2214-109X(25)00394-8/fulltext)). In the US, state laws add pharmacist-prescribing routes under collaborative practice rules, but these are prescriptions issued by pharmacists, not over-the-counter sales ([Florida House analysis, CS/HB 389](https://www.flsenate.gov/Session/Bill/2020/389/Analyses/h0389e.HHS.PDF)). The state-by-state picture was not fully established.

## Enforcement lags the law in several markets

The gap between legal rule and practice is most visible where studies have measured it. In China, a simulated-patient study across 13 provinces found antibiotics obtained without prescription in **83.6%** of pharmacies visited ([Antimicrobial Resistance and Infection Control, 2020](https://doaj.org/article/ff6ca2763c9b4c81b9f486e82f44f42f)). A second study found that a prescription-only sign made no measurable difference to antibiotic offers ([PMC6961359](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6961359/)). In Saudi Arabia, a Riyadh study found that **77.6%** of pharmacies dispensed antibiotics without prescription in 2011, falling to **12.5%** of 327 pharmacies after the 2018 regulations ([Springer, Int J Clin Pharm, 2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11096-020-01033-0)). In India, a South Indian study found antibiotics, including Schedule H1 drugs, still sold without prescription after an education programme ([PMC8092171](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8092171)), and a Goa study found pharmacists knew the rules but did not follow record-keeping requirements ([Porter et al., 2021](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/09685332211020786)). In Brazil, a city-level study found that only 7.5% of refusals were due to lack of a prescription ([SciELO Brazil](http://www.scielo.br/j/bjid/a/YRcTQ3vKDPtfVdyTzT6zLrK/?lang=en)). These studies are small and local, so they should not be generalised nationally.

Formal penalties are less clear. Under US law, a prescription drug without the Rx-only label is misbranded ([21 U.S.C. § 353, Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/21/353)), but no official source found sets out fines or imprisonment for selling or buying antibiotics without a prescription in the US or Canada. Brazil's ANVISA has warned that non-compliant pharmacies face fines and possible licence suspension, but that warning concerned GLP-1 drugs, not antibiotics ([Demarest, 2025](https://www.demarest.com.br/en/anvisa-aprova-obrigatoriedade-de-retencao-de-receita-medica-para-dispensacao-dos-medicamentos-semaglutida-liraglutida-dulaglutida-exenatida-tirzepatida-e-lixisenatida/)). Mexico has a complaint channel, with irregular antibiotic sales reportable anonymously to COFEPRIS by portal or phone ([Ateneа Pharma Revista](https://www.ateneapharmarevista.com/post/riesgos-y-sanciones-a-las-farmacias-por-vender-antibi%C3%B3ticos-sin-receta-en-m%C3%A9xico)). A 2026 Mexican study reports that **96.7%** of rural pharmacies in Hidalgo sold antibiotics irregularly ([Crónica, Feb 2026](https://www.cronica.com.mx/academia/2026/02/23/antibioticos-sin-control-una-problematica-persistente/)).

## Online sales and travel imports do not bypass the prescription

Online and cross-border routes mostly lead back to the prescription requirement. In the US, FDA says it is generally illegal to import an unapproved drug for personal use ([FDA](https://www.fda.gov/AboutFDA/Transparency/Basics/ucm194904.htm)). FDA's personal importation policy is discretionary and typically covers no more than a three-month supply, and FDA says it does not give individuals a licence to import unapproved drugs ([FDA PIP FAQ](https://fda.report/media/83411/PIP-Faqs.pdf)). Sources conflict on whether a valid US prescription is required under that policy ([Everyone.org](https://everyone.org/guides/country/index?code=US)). Canada allows personal importation of up to a three-month supply of unapproved products, but Controlled Drugs cannot be imported for personal use ([House of Commons HEAL Committee Report 2](https://www.ourcommons.ca/DocumentViewer/en/36-1/HEAL/report-2/page-78)). Health Canada's guidance on personal-use health products covers prescription drugs ([Health Canada GUI-0116](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/drugs-health-products/compliance-enforcement/importation-exportation/personal-use-health-products-guidance/document.html)). A carrier summary says Canadian residents importing prescription drugs by courier face restrictions ([FedEx Canada](https://www.fedex.com/en-ca/shipping-services/international/regulatory/prescription-reminder.html)). Medsafe in New Zealand warns that importing a prescription medicine without reasonable excuse is illegal ([Medsafe FAQ](https://www.medsafe.govt.nz/compliance/faqs.asp)).

Online sellers are a known risk. A US study found that **36.2%** of antibiotics sold online were sold without a prescription ([PMC7470343](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7470343)). China's National Medical Products Administration issued online prescription-drug sales guidelines on 28 May 2026, requiring prescription sales only on a valid prescription with pharmacist review, though some platforms still displayed prescription-drug indications ([Caixin Global, 2026](https://www.caixinglobal.com/2026-05-28/china-tightens-rules-on-online-prescription-drug-sales-102448412.html)). Japan's commercial guides say prescription medicines cannot be bought online without a valid Japanese prescription ([Expat Focus, Japan](https://www.expatfocus.com/guide/japan-prescriptions-and-medications)). Telehealth can produce a legal prescription from a locally licensed clinician, which is a route to the prescription requirement rather than an exception to it.

## Conclusion

The practical answer is that a prescription is needed for systemic antibiotics almost everywhere, and buyers who want an oral or injectable antibiotic should assume they need one. The over-the-counter options are limited to specific topical products, such as triple antibiotic ointments in the US and Polysporin ointment in Canada, and to pharmacist-supplied routes in England and France. Travellers and online buyers should not treat foreign or unregulated sources as an exception, since import policies in the US, Canada and New Zealand are discretionary or restricted.

Several gaps remain and should be checked against primary sources before reliance. The US and Canadian classifications of individual antibiotics were not confirmed in regulator lists. The reported German reform restricting pharmacist supply was not confirmed as enacted. Primary texts were not retrieved for Japan, the UAE and Saudi Arabia. Penalties for individual buyers are not documented in any official source found. The 2026 US customs changes also conflict on their effective date, with one source giving 22 October and another 20 January 2027 ([Appendicure](https://appendicure.com/2026/10/08/prescription-drug-importation/); [American Action Forum](https://www.americanactionforum.org/insight/drug-compounding-may-increase-under-new-customs-policy/)). For a specific purchase, the product's own regulatory status in the country of purchase is the decisive check.
