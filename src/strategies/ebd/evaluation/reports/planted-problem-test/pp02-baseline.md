# Galileo Turned a Dutch Spyglass into Cosmic Evidence

Galileo did not invent the telescope. Dutch spectacle-makers in 1608 produced the first documented instruments, and Galileo's contribution was to raise their magnification from about three-fold to about twenty-fold within months and to point the result at the sky. Between late 1609 and 1610 his observations of the Moon, the Milky Way, Jupiter's moons, Venus and sunspots gave Copernicanism its first telescopic evidence and undermined the idea of an unchanging heaven. The observations were confirmed by Jesuit astronomers, yet they did not settle the debate, because the key disputes concerned how to interpret them, and the Church condemned Copernicanism as a physical claim in 1616 and again in 1633. Over the following centuries the telescope split into refractor and reflector lines, gave astronomy its precise measuring tools, and, once combined with photography and spectroscopy, underpinned the rise of astrophysics. Historians still disagree about how much of Galileo's success rested on new optical theory and how much on his patronage and reputation.

## Galileo improved a Dutch invention rather than inventing it

The earliest known patent application for a telescope-type instrument was filed by **Hans Lippershey, a Middelburg spectacle-maker, on 2 October 1608** with the States General, describing an instrument "for seeing things far away as if they were near" ([AAS](https://aas.org/posts/story/2017/10/month-astronomical-history-invention-telescope)). **Jacob Metius, a lens maker from Alkmaar, filed a similar application a few weeks later** ([AAS](https://aas.org/posts/story/2017/10/month-astronomical-history-invention-telescope); [Wikipedia, Jacob Metius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacob_Metius)). Both were refused, because the instrument was judged too easy to copy to be kept secret, though the government rewarded Metius with a small award and employed Lippershey to make binocular versions ([AIP](https://history.aip.org/exhibits/cosmology/tools/tools-first-telescopes.htm)). These early instruments combined a convex and a concave lens in a tube and magnified about three or four times ([AIP](https://history.aip.org/exhibits/cosmology/tools/tools-first-telescopes.htm)). The exact design of the 1608 patent instrument is not documented; the Linda Hall Library notes that it may have used two convex lenses or one convex and one concave ([Linda Hall Library](https://www.lindahall.org/about/news/scientist-of-the-day/hans-lipperhey/)). Van Helden's 1977 study, the standard reference, gives Lippershey the strongest claim mainly because his application is the earliest record of a physical telescope ([Sky at Night Magazine](https://www.skyatnightmagazine.com/space-science/who-invented-the-telescope)). A claim by Zacharias Janssen, advanced by his son in 1655, remains contested ([Wikipedia, History of the telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_telescope)). One online source places Lippershey's application with a "Belgian government," which conflicts with the States General cited elsewhere and is best treated as an error ([Molecular Expressions](https://micro.magnet.fsu.edu/optics/timeline/people/lippershey.html)).

Galileo's own dates and figures vary by source. One account has him hearing of the spyglass in Venice in late July 1609 and obtaining one in Padua in early August ([Gish Bar Times](http://www.gishbartimes.org/2010/01/io400-part-1-copernius-galileo-and.html)). The Rice Galileo Project instead dates a three-power instrument to June or July 1609 ([Galileo Project, Rice](https://galileo.library.rice.edu/sci/instruments/telescope.html)). A *Nature* article reports rumours of a Flemish spyglass a few weeks before late August and then an improved instrument ([Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-019-02848-2)). The sequence most sources agree on is shown below.

| Stage | Magnification | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Dutch instruments, 1608 | About 3–4x | [AIP](https://history.aip.org/exhibits/cosmology/tools/tools-first-telescopes.htm) |
| Galileo's first instrument, mid-1609 | 3x | [Galileo Project, Rice](https://galileo.library.rice.edu/sci/instruments/telescope.html) |
| Venetian Senate demonstration, August 1609 | About 8–9x | [Galileo Project, Rice](https://galileo.library.rice.edu/sci/instruments/telescope.html); [Designworld](https://www.designworldonline.com/today-in-engineering-history-galileo-demonstrates-telescope-to-venetian-senate/) |
| Instrument pointed at the sky, late 1609 | About 20x (sources also report 21x and up to 30x) | [Galileo Project, Rice](https://galileo.library.rice.edu/sci/instruments/telescope.html); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/225716816_Galileo%27s_telescope_History_scientific_analysis_and_replicated_observations) |

Galileo worked out that magnification is the ratio of the eyepiece's power to the objective's power. Opticians offered only a narrow range of lens strengths, so he taught himself to grind lenses ([Designworld](https://www.designworldonline.com/today-in-engineering-history-galileo-demonstrates-telescope-to-venetian-senate/)). The Venetian demonstrations took place on 21 August 1609 ([arXiv 1307.4963](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1307.4963)). The Museo Galileo's surviving telescope has a plano-convex objective of 37 mm diameter, 15 mm aperture and 980 mm focal length, dated late 1609 to early 1610; its original eyepiece was replaced in the 19th century ([Museo Galileo](https://catalogue.museogalileo.it/object/GalileosTelescope_n01.html)). Wikipedia gives the objective as 38 mm ([Wikipedia, Sidereus Nuncius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidereus_Nuncius)). Galileo stopped the aperture down to reduce blur at the lens rim ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/225716816_Galileo%27s_telescope_History_scientific_analysis_and_replicated_observations)). A modern replica of a Florence telescope gives about 20x ([arXiv 2503.12543](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.12543)).

## Moon, Milky Way and stars revealed a rougher, richer cosmos

Galileo first turned his telescope on the Moon on **30 November 1609**, and his comparison of light and shadow near the terminator led him to argue for mountains and valleys ([NCAR HAO](https://www2.hao.ucar.edu/education/scientists/galileo-galilei-1564-1642)). He concluded that the Moon's surface resembles Earth's, with valleys, plains and mountains, and that its dark spots are shadows cast as sunlight changes ([Rice, Galileo's Moon](https://galileo.library.rice.edu/lib/student_work/astronomy95/moon.html)). He attributed the faint unlit glow to earthshine, which the Australia Telescope National Facility also notes ([ATNF](https://www.atnf.csiro.au/resources/education/senior-cosmicengine/history/galileo-newton/); [Cloudy Nights](https://www.cloudynights.com/articles/articles/the-discoveries-of-galileo-%E2%80%93-part-3-the-moon-r3287/)). His height estimates came from how far into the dark side illuminated peaks became visible, and some of his sample peaks exceeded four Italian miles ([University of Virginia](https://galileoandeinstein.phys.virginia.edu/lectures/galtel.htm); [Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Sidereal_Messenger_of_Galileo_Galilei/The_Sidereal_Messenger)). Modern review suggests these heights were overstated, with the Albategnius depression drawn far larger than modern photographs show, possibly because of earlier Pythagorean descriptions ([ETH Zurich Library](https://library.ethz.ch/en/collections-and-archives/platforms/virtual-exhibitions/galileo-galilei/galileos-works/the-moon-in-sidereus-nuncius-and-the-dialogue.html)). The sources I consulted do not establish a specific Galileo observation of libration.

Turning to the Milky Way, Galileo saw it resolved into thousands of stars ([ATNF](https://www.atnf.csiro.au/resources/education/senior-astrophysics/observing/galileo/)), and he described the galaxy as "nothing else than a congeries of innumerable stars distributed in clusters" ([SkyTonight](https://skytonight.org/galileo-1610)). He reported at least ten times more stars than the naked eye sees, and published charts of Orion's Belt and the Pleiades with newly seen stars ([Wikipedia, Sidereus Nuncius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidereus_Nuncius)). Even through the telescope, stars remained points of light, which he attributed to their distance ([ATNF](https://www.atnf.csiro.au/resources/education/senior-astrophysics/observing/galileo/)). A scholarly paper argues that Galileo measured stellar disks, but it also holds that the disks were diffraction patterns of the instrument, which no early telescope could resolve as true stellar sizes ([arXiv 0903.3429](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0903.3429)). The sources I consulted rely on secondary summaries rather than the original text of *Sidereus Nuncius*.

## Jupiter's moons and Venus's phases tested Copernicus, and sunspots moved the Sun

On **7 January 1610** Galileo saw three stars near Jupiter and noticed the next evening that they had shifted in a way that implied motion around the planet ([SolarViews](https://solarviews.com/eng/galdisc.htm)). By 15 January he concluded they orbited Jupiter ([Wikipedia, Galilean moons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galilean_moons)), and after about two months he had found four ([Science in School](https://scienceinschool.org/article/2012/galileo/)). The discovery mattered because it showed that bodies could circle a centre other than Earth, which answered the objection that Copernicus's system required two centres of motion ([Linda Hall Library](https://www.lindahall.org/about/news/scientist-of-the-day/galileo-galilei-2/)). Priority is disputed: Simon Marius claimed earlier observations, and some sources give his first observation as 8 January 1610 ([SolarViews](https://solarviews.com/eng/galdisc.htm); [Wikipedia, Exploration of Io](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exploration_of_Io)). Marius's names (Io, Europa, Ganymede, Callisto) became the standard ones ([Wikipedia, Ganymede](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ganymede_(moon))). Galileo's own names were the "Medicean" planets ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/science/Galilean-satellite)), and the discovery also served his search for Medici patronage ([Science in School](https://scienceinschool.org/article/2012/galileo/)).

Venus showed a full set of phases, which the Ptolemaic system could not produce, since its Venus stayed in a small epicycle and would appear only as a crescent ([IMSS Florence](https://brunelleschi.imss.fi.it/esplora/cannocchiale/dswmedia/simula/esimula1_3_st.html); [UNLV](https://www.physics.unlv.edu/~jeffery/astro/ptolemy/venus_phases.html)). Some sources call this the first conclusive observational proof for Copernicus ([NMSU](http://astronomy.nmsu.edu/aklypin/WebSite/galileo.htm)), but the phases were equally compatible with Tycho Brahe's geocentric model, in which Venus circles the Sun, so they weakened geocentrism without proving heliocentrism ([arXiv physics/0606255](https://arxiv.org/pdf/physics/0606255)). The timing is contested. Galileo did not report the phases in *Sidereus Nuncius*; he reported them later, in the 1613 *Letters on Sunspots* ([UNLV](https://www.physics.unlv.edu/~jeffery/astro/ptolemy/venus_phases.html)). A 2021 study dates his first written account to 30 December 1610, after Benedetto Castelli's letter of 5 December had suggested the test, and it argues he may not have known of the phases before that prompt ([arXiv 2102.06595](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2102.06595)).

Sunspots were the third challenge. Galileo began sunspot work in April 1612 ([Rice](https://galileo.library.rice.edu/sci/observations/sunspots.html)), and he concluded that the spots lay on or near the solar surface and that the Sun rotated about once every 28 days ([Cloudy Nights, Part 2](https://www.cloudynights.com/articles/articles/the-discoveries-of-galileo-%E2%80%93-part-2-sunspots-and-their-movements-r3281/); [Oxford](https://www.cabinet.ox.ac.uk/spots-sun-1612-13)). The rotation result is an inference from tracking spots across days, not a direct measurement ([Space.com](https://www.space.com/does-the-sun-rotate)). He took the spots as evidence that the heavens were not immutable ([Wikipedia, Letter to Benedetto Castelli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Letter_to_Benedetto_Castelli)). Christoph Scheiner, writing as "Apelles," argued the spots were small planets orbiting the Sun ([Oxford](https://www.cabinet.ox.ac.uk/spots-sun-1612-13); [Wikipedia, Mark Welser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Welser)). Galileo was partly wrong: the 2021 study says sunspots are depressions rather than clouds, and that his claim of one common period at all latitudes was mistaken, since equatorial spots move faster than polar ones ([arXiv 2102.06595](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2102.06595)).

## Jesuit confirmation did not end the debate, and the Church ruled on claims

Reception came in two stages. The Roman College Jesuits were first sceptical, but Clavius later became persuaded of the Jupiter satellites, the Venus phases and the lunar irregularities after further observation ([Coyne, Forum Italicum 2015](https://www.vaticanobservatory.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/Jesuits-and-Galileo.pdf)). In April 1611 Clavius and colleagues certified the phenomena, though they did not endorse Galileo's theory ([Rice, Clavius](https://galileo.library.rice.edu/sci/clavius.html); [Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/people/science-and-technology/astronomy-biographies/christopher-clavius)). Clavius's 1611 edition still told astronomers to find a way to arrange the celestial orbs to save the phenomena, which Coyne reads as a move from Tycho toward, but not into, Copernicanism ([Coyne 2015](https://www.vaticanobservatory.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/Jesuits-and-Galileo.pdf)). Jesuit philosophers murmured against the anti-Aristotelian implications, and a circular from Father General Acquaviva on 24 May 1611 stressed doctrinal uniformity ([Coyne 2015](https://www.vaticanobservatory.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/Jesuits-and-Galileo.pdf)). Kepler responded enthusiastically with his *Dissertatio cum Nuncio Sidereo* in 1610 ([Beinecke](https://beinecke.library.yale.edu/node/64938)), while Francesco Sizzi's 1611 *Dianoia* denied the satellites, largely on astrological grounds ([Wikipedia, Francesco Sizzi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francesco_Sizzi)). The sunspot quarrel with Scheiner cooled relations with the Jesuits ([Coyne 2015](https://www.vaticanobservatory.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/Jesuits-and-Galileo.pdf)). In 1619 Orazio Grassi used the 1618 comets to argue from the absence of observable parallax that comets lie beyond the Moon ([Wikipedia, The Assayer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Assayer)). Galileo's *Discourse on Comets* rebutted him ([Wikipedia, Discourse on Comets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discourse_on_Comets)), and Coyne says the disputes of 1619–23 permanently changed his relations with the Jesuits ([Coyne 2015](https://www.vaticanobservatory.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/Jesuits-and-Galileo.pdf)).

The Church's rulings targeted physical claims rather than the observations. In 1616 a committee of eleven consultants called the heliocentric theory "philosophically absurd and formally heretical" ([Vatican Observatory](https://www.vaticanobservatory.org/resources/galileo/historical-events/galileo-a-quick-summary/)). Bellarmine warned Galileo on 26 February 1616 not to hold, teach or defend Copernicanism, and the Index suspended *De revolutionibus* in March 1616 ([Famous Trials](https://www.famous-trials.com/galileotrial/1015-chronology)). A Church History review describes the first summons as a warning rather than an interrogation ([Cambridge, Church History](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/church-history/article/galileos-nontrial-1616-pretrial-16321633-and-trial-may-10-1633-a-review-of-procedure-featuring-routine-violations-of-the-forum-of-conscience/F5BB9632FA3F4C34B33C119A401EB3BC)). Historian Thomas Kelly, quoted by UCLA, calls 1616 a non-trial, and he says Galileo stretched the truth in 1633 about his post-1616 views ([UCLA Newsroom](https://newsroom.ucla.edu/releases/the-truth-about-galileo-and-his-conflict-with-the-catholic-church)). Sources disagree on whether the 1616 injunction was formally documented: one exhibit says the 1633 case rested on an unsigned notary record, and Galileo produced a Bellarmine letter stating he had not been asked to abjure ([Shareok](https://shareok.org/bitstreams/2cb8fc3a-eaa4-4e07-a30b-eee856bc0499/download)). In 1633 Galileo was found guilty of "vehement suspicion of heresy" on 22 June ([Vatican Observatory](https://www.vaticanobservatory.org/resources/galileo/historical-events/galileo-a-quick-summary/)). Coyne argues that Aristotle's physics was treated as so sacred that denying it looked like heresy ([Coyne 2015](https://www.vaticanobservatory.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/Jesuits-and-Galileo.pdf)). The Jesuit confirmation of the observations therefore did not protect Galileo, because the charge concerned what Copernicanism could be claimed to be.

The evidential status of the telescope was also contested. The Stanford Encyclopedia says Galileo used particular observations to confirm or reject theoretical proposals, and that a single experiment could in principle overturn a principle ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/galileo/)). Physics World reports that the telescope failed to show the annual stellar parallax that Copernicanism predicted ([Physics World](https://physicsworld.com/a/the-galileo-affair/)). Critics raised optical objections too: Scheiner noticed that the projected solar image took on the tint of its lens ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00016-026-00353-4)), and an arXiv paper argues Galileo did not disclose the role of the aperture stop, which Clavius had asked about ([arXiv 1307.4963](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1307.4963)). The *Dialogue* (1632) argued that parallax would be detectable in principle, but a letter to *Sky & Telescope* reports unpublished Mizar observations by Galileo showing no parallax ([arXiv 0712.4281](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0712.4281)). Historians differ on whether authority moved to instruments. The Galileo Project says the telescope shifted authority over observation from men to instruments ([Rice](https://galileo.library.rice.edu/sci/instruments/telescope.html)). Biagioli argues that instrument credibility stayed tied to personal and patronage authority, since Galileo sent telescopes to patrons and gave the book to a wider audience ([Biagioli 2000](http://ipbio.org/pdfs/papers/Biagioli%202000%20Replication%20or%20Monopoly%20Science%20in%20Context.pdf); [Wilding review](https://ircps.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/04/2007-09-01_Wilding.pdf)), and that Medici patronage gave his discoveries social legitimacy ([Chronicle of Higher Education](https://www.chronicle.com/article/galileo-said-to-present-his-discoveries-to-favor-medicis/)).

## Technical limits drove refractor and reflector design

Galileo's telescopes had narrow fields that shrank as magnification rose ([IMSS Florence](https://brunelleschi.imss.fi.it/esplora/cannocchiale/dswmedia/risorse/complete_texts.pdf)). Estimates of their apparent field range from about 15 to 20 arcminutes ([arXiv 2503.12543](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.12543); [arXiv 1307.4963](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1307.4963)). Their singlet lenses suffered chromatic aberration, which produced colour fringes at the field edge ([arXiv 1307.4963](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1307.4963)), and the glass contained bubbles and a greenish tint ([AIP](https://history.aip.org/exhibits/cosmology/tools/tools-first-telescopes.htm)). A study of two surviving Galileo telescopes finds optics approaching diffraction limits, with resolving powers of about 20 and 10 arcseconds, and identifies chromatic aberration as the main limit ([Academia](https://www.academia.edu/4696389/The_telescopes_of_Galileo)). Chromatic error falls as focal length grows relative to aperture, so the required focal length rises with the square of the diameter ([IMSS Florence](https://brunelleschi.imss.fi.it/esplora/cannocchiale/dswmedia/storia/estoria5_st.html)). Kepler's 1611 *Dioptrice* proposed a convex eyepiece, which gave a wider field and more magnification but produced inverted images ([Museo Galileo](https://brunelleschi.imss.fi.it/telescopiogalileo/etel.asp?c=50026)). Scholars disagree about the first Keplerian instrument: a 2022 study finds Kepler never built one and names Francesco Fontana as the most likely maker, a claim it treats as probable rather than proven ([ADS, JAHH 2022](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022JAHH...25..511M/abstract)). Kepler's design was not quickly accepted, and the AIP exhibit links its uptake to Scheiner's 1630 *Rosa Ursina* ([AIP](https://history.aip.org/exhibits/cosmology/tools/tools-first-telescopes.htm)). A study argues that the *Dioptrice* was influential but was overshadowed in the 1660s by work using geometrical optics ([Academia](https://www.academia.edu/2019199/Kepler_s_legacy_telescopes_and_geometrical_optics_1611_1669)).

Newton reasoned that refractors would always be defective and built his first reflector in 1668, using a copper-tin alloy mirror and a small flat secondary mirror ([AIP](https://history.aip.org/exhibits/cosmology/tools/tools-early-reflectors.htm)). He presented it to the Royal Society in January 1672 ([Aeon](https://aeon.co/ideas/how-many-great-minds-does-it-take-to-invent-a-telescope)). James Gregory had published a parabolic-mirror design in *Optica promota* (1663) ([Aeon](https://aeon.co/ideas/how-many-great-minds-does-it-take-to-invent-a-telescope)). Large reflectors were slow to follow, and John Hadley built a large Newtonian in 1721 ([Aeon](https://aeon.co/ideas/how-many-great-minds-does-it-take-to-invent-a-telescope)). Refractors dominated the second half of the 19th century, but research telescopes were later largely superseded by reflectors, which allow larger apertures ([Wikipedia, Refracting telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refracting_telescope)).

## Telescopes reshaped measurement, institutions and astrophysics

The Galilean telescope was a poor sighting device for classical astrometry, which measures large angles between stars ([arXiv 1104.4554](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1104.4554)). Measurement became practical when Gascoigne placed fine threads in the focal plane in the late 1630s, a method that fell into neglect after his death in 1644 ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-1-4614-3945-5_13); [arXiv 1104.4554](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1104.4554)). Huygens independently invented the filar micrometer around 1660 and used it to measure the Moon and planets ([arXiv 1104.4554](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1104.4554)). The micrometer remained a standard astrometric tool for more than 300 years ([arXiv 1104.4554](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1104.4554)), and set circles later sped measurement ([Wikipedia, Astrometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrometry)). Catalogue accuracy also drove institutions: Tycho's 1598 catalogue was not accurate enough for longitudes, and Flamsteed's first task at Greenwich was more accurate charts ([Wikipedia, Royal Observatory, Greenwich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Observatory,_Greenwich)).

State observatories followed. The Paris Observatory was established on 21 June 1667 ([Consulat général de France](https://hongkong.consulfrance.org/The-Paris-Observatory-at-the)) and was financed from the royal treasury for geodesy and mapmaking ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1631071315000462)). Greenwich was founded by order of Charles II in 1675 to rectify tables of the heavens for navigation and find longitude ([Royal Observatory Greenwich](https://www.royalobservatorygreenwich.org/articles.php?article=1)). The Royal Observatory began the *Nautical Almanac* in 1766, and the Greenwich meridian was adopted as prime in 1884 partly because navigators used the almanac ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Royal-Greenwich-Observatory)). Paris kept its own meridian until 1911 ([Armagh Planet](https://armaghplanet.com/paris-observatory.html)). Its calendar role was indirect: the sources document ephemerides and meridians, not calendar reform.

The telescope's path to physical astronomy ran through measurement. Bessel announced the first trigonometric stellar parallax, for 61 Cygni, in 1838, with a value of 0.31″ that later analysis matches to the Hipparcos figure of 0.287″ ([arXiv astro-ph/0502167](https://arxiv.org/pdf/astro-ph/0502167); [arXiv 1707.01020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1707.01020)). Spectroscopy and photography then changed the goal. Huggins fitted a slit spectrometer to his telescope ([arXiv 2410.11455](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.11455v4)), and Kirchhoff and Bunsen identified sodium in the solar spectrum in 1857 ([ATNF](https://www.atnf.csiro.au/resources/education/senior-astrophysics/spectroscopy/spectro-history/)). The Potsdam Astrophysical Observatory, founded in 1874, is cited as the first institute to use the term astrophysics ([arXiv 2304.07121](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.07121)). Max Wolf used long-exposure photography to search for asteroids in 1891 ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/science/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/new-view-universe-photography-and-spectroscopy-nineteenth-century-astronomy)). Hubble was launched on 24 April 1990 with a flawed primary mirror, and COSTAR was fitted in December 1993 ([NASA](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/hubble/overview/hubble-timeline/hubble-history-timeline-full-text/); [ESA](https://www.esa.int/Science_Exploration/Space_Science/Hubble_overview)). Sources give the mirror error variously as 2 microns and 3 microns ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/hubble/30-years-ago-hubble-launched-to-unlock-the-secrets-of-the-universe/)). One educational site credits Hubble with more than 19,000 peer-reviewed papers, though this is a secondary figure ([ASMR Education](https://asmr.education/faq/1990s/hubble-space-telescope-launch-year)).

## Conclusion

Galileo's lasting contribution was less an invention than a demonstration. He turned a three-power Dutch device into a tool that could test cosmological claims, and his observations gave Copernicanism evidence that could be checked by others. The Roman College Jesuits did check them and confirmed the main phenomena, which shows that observation could persuade experts. But observation alone did not resolve the debate: the parallax problem remained unanswered, and the Church's judgments turned on the physical reality of Earth's motion rather than on the observations. This is why the historical record shows the telescope changing what counted as evidence while leaving authority, patronage and theory in contention.

The longer history shows the telescope's value growing as it was paired with measurement, instruments and institutions. Micrometers, state observatories and the parallax measurement of 1838 turned it into a quantitative tool, and spectroscopy and photography turned it toward the physical nature of stars. Remaining uncertainties include the exact design of the 1608 instrument, the dating of Galileo's Venus claims, the magnitude of his Moon heights, and the priority of the Jupiter moons. Each is worth weighing in a careful reading of the primary texts.
