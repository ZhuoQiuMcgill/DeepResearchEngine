# Vesuvius, Not Etna, Buried Pompeii in 79 CE

The eruption that destroyed Pompeii was an eruption of Vesuvius, not Mount Etna. [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Pompeii) names Vesuvius as the volcano whose violent eruption destroyed the city on August 24, 79 CE, and [NASA Earth Observatory](https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/napoli-and-volcanism-vesuvius-and-mt-etna-1898/) describes Vesuvius as the cone that erupted to bury Pompeii and Herculaneum. Etna is a separate volcano on Sicily ([Smithsonian Magazine](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/mount-etnas-spectacular-monday-explosion-marks-the-volcanos-most-intense-activity-in-years-180986741/)), and no retrieved source links it to the 79 CE disaster. Pompeii died because it lay about 23 km southeast of Naples at Vesuvius's base ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Pompeii)), downwind of an eruption that first buried it under falling pumice and then under hot surges. Warning tremors and an earlier earthquake were recorded, but no one at the time linked them to a coming eruption. The exact date, the size of the eruption and the main cause of death remain disputed, and this report flags each point where the sources diverge.

## Vesuvius, not Etna, was the volcano behind the destruction

The notes support a single volcano for the disaster. The Frontiers study describes the 79 CE event as a Plinian eruption of Vesuvius ([Frontiers in Earth Science](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/earth-science/articles/10.3389/feart.2024.1386960/full)). In this usage, a Plinian eruption is one with a sustained eruption column, the "sustained column" phase in that study. Etna was active in Roman times: a basaltic Plinian eruption dated to 122 BC damaged the volcano's southern flank ([ResearchGate, Coltelli et al.](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/236671933_Discovery_of_a_Plinian_basaltic_eruption_of_Roman_age_at_Etna_volcano_Italy)), and Suetonius records Etna activity in 40 CE ([Livius](https://www.livius.org/articles/place/etna/)). These events are separate from 79 CE. The only Etna-Pompeii link in the notes is fiction: Robert Harris's novel *Pompeii* has a character recognise Vesuvian phenomena from an Etna eruption ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pompeii_(novel))).

Sources disagree on the eruption's size. [Magma Tribune](https://magmatribune.com/volcanoes/eruption-of-mount-vesuvius-79-ad/), summarising the Global Volcanism Program, rates it 5 on the Volcanic Explosivity Index, while a separate ResearchGate-indexed paper rates it 6 ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/282406903_The_eruption_of_Vesuvius_in_AD_79)). The notes do not explain the gap, and the rating is secondary for the question of cause. The date is also contested. [History.com](https://www.history.com/articles/pompeii) says August 24 is the traditional date, drawn from manuscripts of Pliny's letter, and that some scholars argue for October on archaeological grounds. [Magma Tribune](https://magmatribune.com/volcanoes/eruption-of-mount-vesuvius-79-ad/) reports that NOAA's database lists August 25. The notes contain no independent radiocarbon or dendrochronological date, so the calendar question stays open.

## The city was buried in two stages

The eruption came in phases, and the timing of each phase matters for understanding the death toll. The opening phase was a brief, water-driven blast whose ash did not reach Pompeii; the Plinian phase then produced a sustained column of falling pumice, and a final phase generated successive pyroclastic currents ([Frontiers in Earth Science](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/earth-science/articles/10.3389/feart.2024.1386960/full)). Pumice fall began around 1 p.m. on the first day, switched from white to grey pumice after about seven hours, and the Plinian phase lasted about 18 hours ([Frontiers in Earth Science](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/earth-science/articles/10.3389/feart.2024.1386960/full)). Pyroclastic currents are the hot, fast clouds of ash and gas that the sources describe as surges; one account says a giant cloud of hot ash and gas surged down the western flank and engulfed Herculaneum ([History.com](https://www.history.com/this-day-in-history/august-24/vesuvius-erupts)).

The thickness of the deposit is also contested. Frontiers gives a maximum pumice thickness of 2.8–2.9 m at Pompeii ([Frontiers in Earth Science](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/earth-science/articles/10.3389/feart.2024.1386960/full)), a 1985 reconstruction gives up to 2.8 m ([University of Chicago Journals, Sigurdsson et al.](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.2307/504292)), a Scientific Reports paper gives up to 3 m ([PMC, Scientific Reports](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7925681/)), and [Pompeii Italy](https://www.pompeiiitaly.org/en/about-pompeii/eruption/) gives 4 to 6 m. The most careful figure is about 3 m of pumice, with total deposit including surge ash reaching 6 m or more in places ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Pompeii)). Surge timing differs between studies. A stratigraphic study identifies 17 pyroclastic density currents and places the fourth surge at Pompeii at about 7 a.m. on the second day ([ResearchGate, Luongo et al.](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/222033714_Impact_of_the_AD_79_explosive_eruption_on_Pompeii_I_Relations_amongst_the_depositional_mechanisms_of_the_pyroclastic_products_the_framework_of_the_buildings_and_the_associated_destructive_events)), while Frontiers places the start of surge arrival from about 8 a.m. ([Frontiers in Earth Science](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/earth-science/articles/10.3389/feart.2024.1386960/full)). A difference of an hour may reflect how "arrival" is defined, but the sources do not say so. A newer study at a fresh excavation finds two short pauses in the second phase that it says contradict the picture drawn from Pliny's letters ([Geological Magazine, Scarpati et al.](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/geological-magazine/article/pompeian-hiatuses-new-stratigraphic-data-highlight-pauses-in-the-course-of-the-ad-79-eruption-at-pompeii/0D717E1C9BBDFD4C10AE34A0F2B86ADF)).

Roofs failed under the fall load. Most roof collapses happened a few hours after the Plinian phase began, under about 40–60 cm of pumice ([Frontiers in Earth Science](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/earth-science/articles/10.3389/feart.2024.1386960/full)). Herculaneum, on the western side, received only a few centimetres of ash, which caused little structural damage ([Wikipedia, Herculaneum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herculaneum)). Pompeii lay on the main axis of pumice fall, because high winds carried the plume south-southeast over it ([Michigan Tech, Vesuvio](https://www.geo.mtu.edu/volcanoes/boris/mirror/mirrored_html/VESUVIO_79.html)). Wind direction is itself disputed: one blog says the wind blew straight toward Pompeii from the northwest ([Arcfield Weather](https://arcfieldweather.com/blog/2016/8/24/1000-am-on-this-date-in-79-ad-mount-vesuvius-erupted-and-pompeii-italy-was-changed-forever)), which conflicts with the southeast direction in the other sources.

## Heat or asphyxiation: the cause of death is contested

Deaths fell into two broad groups: people killed by roof collapse under the pumice, and people killed by the surges. The Frontiers study counts 394 bodies in the pumice-fall deposit, 345 of them indoors and probably killed by collapse, and 650 bodies in the pyroclastic-current deposits, split almost evenly between indoor (334) and outdoor (316) areas ([Frontiers in Earth Science](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/earth-science/articles/10.3389/feart.2024.1386960/full)). These are excavation counts, not a total death toll, and the notes show no reliable total. A 2010 study combining volcanology and bioanthropology argued that heat from surges of at least 250°C, rather than ash suffocation, was the main cause of death, even for people sheltered inside buildings ([PMC, Mastrolorenzo et al.](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2886100/)). A 2023 study of six victims, using X-ray fluorescence, concluded they most likely died of asphyxiation before their bones were heated ([Interesting Engineering, reporting Alapont et al.](https://interestingengineering.com/science/pompeii-victims-died-peacefully)), and a critic argues that the thermal-shock study analysed remains from Herculaneum, not Pompeii ([Science](https://www.science.org/content/article/how-did-pompeii-s-victims-really-die)). The evidence does not decide between these accounts for Pompeii, so this report treats heat as a real lethal mechanism for those caught in surges, while accepting that the mechanism for the Pompeii victims is unresolved.

Pompeii's remains also show how the deaths varied. A 2010 analysis found the S4 surge caused most fatalities at Pompeii, even though its deposit at the city is only about 3 cm thick ([PLOS ONE, Mastrolorenzo et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2886100/)). An older review says people in the first phase were mainly killed by roof collapses and those in the second phase by physical trauma or suffocation ([ResearchGate, Giacomelli et al.](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/257758316_The_eruption_of_Vesuvius_of_79_AD_and_its_impact_on_human_environment_in_Pompeii)). A textbook summary reports that about 62% of remains found at Pompeii lay in surge deposits ([Lumen Learning](https://courses.lumenlearning.com/suny-worldhistory/chapter/eruptions-of-vesuvius-and-pompeii/)), though the notes give no primary study for that figure. The earthquake hypothesis is also thin: the authors who propose it base it on two men crushed by a wall in one insula, and they note no clear evidence of syn-eruptive earthquake damage had been reported at Pompeii before their study ([Frontiers in Earth Science](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/earth-science/articles/10.3389/feart.2024.1386960/full)).

## Warnings went unread, and few people left in time

The main warnings were earthquakes and tremors. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AD_62_Pompeii_earthquake) puts the 62 CE earthquake at an estimated magnitude of 5 to 6 and says it may have been a precursor to the renewed activity of Vesuvius in 79 CE, a hedged inference from timing and location. The date is itself disputed: Seneca places the event in 63 CE, and most experts favour 62 CE ([Kiddle](https://kids.kiddle.co/AD_62_Pompeii_earthquake)). Some damage was still unrepaired when the volcano erupted, and minor earthquakes were felt in the four days before the eruption, but the warnings were not recognised ([Wikipedia, Eruption of Mount Vesuvius in 79 AD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eruption_of_Mount_Vesuvius_in_79_AD)). Pliny the Younger treated the tremors as routine, noting they were "not particularly alarming because they are frequent in Campania" ([Wikipedia, Eruption of Mount Vesuvius in 79 AD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eruption_of_Mount_Vesuvius_in_79_AD)). Modern accounts differ: one tourism site says modern volcanologists attribute the 62 CE quake to magma moving beneath Vesuvius ([Pompeii City](https://pompeiicity.com/en/history/earthquake-62-ad)), while a history article argues that the later activity was not a result of the 62 CE quake ([History and Archaeology Online](https://historyandarchaeologyonline.com/did-the-earthquake-of-62ad-lead-to-pompeiis-decline-or-renewal/)). No ancient source ties the earthquake to Vesuvius, and the link is an interpretation.

Some people fled. In Pompeii, many rushed toward the harbour ([Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/sites/drsarahbond/2016/08/24/august-24-79-an-hour-by-hour-account-of-vesuvius-eruption-on-the-1937th-anniversary/)), and those who stayed died the next morning when toxic gas filled the city ([History.com](https://www.history.com/this-day-in-history/august-24/vesuvius-erupts)). Excavations outside the Porta Stabia found two men who had been trying to reach the coast, and the younger was probably struck by a pyroclastic flow ([La Brújula Verde](https://www.labrujulaverde.com/en/2026/04/archaeologists-reconstruct-the-escape-of-a-man-who-tried-to-protect-himself-from-the-eruption-of-vesuvius-with-a-mortar-in-pompeii/)). I found no source describing an official Roman evacuation order. Pliny's letters, the only surviving eyewitness account, describe people debating whether to stay indoors or go out as buildings shook ([WiredPen](https://wiredpen.com/resources/pliny-the-younger-letters-on-the-eruption-of-mount-vesuvius/)). Pliny also never names Pompeii ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eruption_of_Mount_Vesuvius_in_79_AD)), so the city-level sequence comes from excavations, not the letters.

## Forecasting then and now: the limits of warning

Scientists still cannot forecast the size and style of volcanic eruptions accurately ([ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2022/01/220120125422.htm)). One view holds that a large eruption like that of 79 CE would probably follow a quiet period of centuries ([Futurity](https://www.futurity.org/vesuvius-volcanoes-magma-2684962/)), while another holds that volcanologists could predict an eruption soon after reactivation signs appeared ([National Geographic](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/magazine/article/vesuvius-volcano-dormant-eruption-pompeii)). The 2006 Avellino work, which warned that a major eruption could threaten Naples, remains controversial ([National Geographic](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/magazine/article/vesuvius-volcano-dormant-eruption-pompeii)). Modern planning assumes warning time. Civil Protection says a reactivation's timing cannot be pinned down, and that the alert would rise through yellow and orange before evacuation ([Vesuvius Hike](https://vesuviushike.it/en/vulcano-attivo/)). The plan estimates **72 hours** for evacuation, and the red zone covers about **700,000 people** on the slopes ([MyItalia](https://myitalia.it/stories/seven-hundred-thousand-under-vesuvius)). The alert level was green when that page was checked in September 2026 ([MyItalia](https://myitalia.it/stories/seven-hundred-thousand-under-vesuvius)). The sources do not test whether the 79 CE warnings could have led to a timely evacuation, and the modern plan rests on monitoring that the ancient world lacked.

## Conclusion

The central answer is that Vesuvius destroyed Pompeii in 79 CE, and the disaster unfolded in stages. A sustained pumice fall of about 3 m collapsed roofs and buried the city, and then surges of hot gas and ash killed many of the people who remained, with the sources agreeing that the surges were the more lethal phase. The reasons for the city's exposure were its position on the main axis of the fall, and the warning tremors that people treated as routine. Several findings remain unsettled: the calendar date, the eruption's size, the pumice thickness, the surge timing and, most importantly, whether heat or asphyxiation killed most victims.

The uncertainty matters for how the disaster is understood and taught. Popular sources give higher death tolls and single-cause stories that the peer-reviewed literature does not support, so readers should prefer the stratigraphic and bioanthropological studies over tourism pages. The modern lesson is that Vesuvius still threatens a dense population, and that the case for planning rests on the physical evidence of the 79 CE sequence rather than on any reliable forecast of the next eruption.
