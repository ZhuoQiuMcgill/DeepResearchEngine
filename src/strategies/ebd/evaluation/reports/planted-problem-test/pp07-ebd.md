# Sources Put 1918 Flu Deaths at 17–100 Million

No single figure is settled, but the evidence supports a working estimate of roughly **20 to 50 million deaths worldwide** for the 1918–1920 influenza pandemic. The most-repeated figure is about 50 million, from Johnson and Mueller's 2002 study, which the CDC's *Emerging Infectious Diseases* article also cites, with an upper bound of about 100 million ([CDC EID](https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/eid/article/12/1/pdfs/05-0979.pdf)). The lowest recent global re-estimate is **17.4 million**, from Spreeuwenberg et al. (2018) ([Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Reassessing-the-Global-Mortality-Burden-of-the-1918-Spreeuwenberg-Kroneman/73741296e47b01dd87b84b6c72408909ef09a3c2)). Reference works cluster in the middle: PAHO/WHO gives 21 to 50 million ([PAHO/WHO](https://www.paho.org/en/who-we-are/history-paho/purple-death-great-flu-1918)) and Britannica gives 25 to 50 million ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/event/influenza-pandemic-of-1918-1919)). These totals are mostly estimates of excess deaths above a baseline, not counts of recorded deaths, and recorded counts were incomplete almost everywhere. The largest single source of disagreement is India, whose national figure drives much of the gap between the low and high totals. The report treats 20–50 million as its working range because no study has yet reconciled the competing methods.

## Defining the pandemic and what a death toll measures

The "1918 influenza pandemic" is best described as a pandemic that began in 1918 and ran until about 1920. Johnson and Mueller describe it as generally occurring in three waves starting in the northern spring and summer of 1918, with some locations seeing influenza persist into or return in 1920 ([Johnson & Mueller, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11875246/)). Earlier origins are disputed: some researchers place the start in France in 1916 or China and Vietnam in 1917, while many other studies point to the United States ([Smithsonian Magazine](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/journal-plague-year-180965222/)). A 2018 virology review says the source, timing and geographic origin of the virus "have remained tenaciously obscure" ([PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1324197111)).

A "death toll" can mean two different things. Registered deaths are counts from death certificates, such as the 464,959 influenza and pneumonia deaths registered in the US in 1918 alone ([Naval History and Heritage Command](https://www.history.navy.mil/research/library/online-reading-room/title-list-alphabetically/i/influenza/the-pandemic-of-influenza-in-1918-1919.html)). Most global totals instead measure **excess mortality**: deaths in the pandemic years above what would have been expected. Spreeuwenberg et al. defined it by comparing 1918 mortality rates with a reference year, controlling for wars and the underlying mortality trend ([DOI page](https://dx.doi.org/10.1093/aje/kwy191)). The name "Spanish flu" does not reflect where the disease began ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12052-018-0079-5)).

## Worldwide totals range from 17 to 100 million

The high figures rest on upward adjustment of recorded deaths. Johnson and Mueller (2002) concluded that global mortality "was of the order of 50 million" and suggested even this may be substantially understated ([Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Updating-the-Accounts:-Global-Mortality-of-the-Johnson-Mueller/2467605476ef50c18104952427bd8960c43aeb80)), with the authors suggesting the true toll may have been as high as 100 million ([Our World in Data](https://ourworldindata.org/spanish-flu-largest-influenza-pandemic-in-history)). Spreeuwenberg, Kroneman and Paget (2018) instead estimated 15 million deaths in 1918 and 2.5 million in 1919, a total of 17.4 million ([DOI page](https://dx.doi.org/10.1093/aje/kwy191)), and they call this "not as severe as most frequently cited estimates" ([Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Reassessing-the-Global-Mortality-Burden-of-the-1918-Spreeuwenberg-Kroneman/73741296e47b01dd87b84b6c72408909ef09a3c2)). The report uses 20–50 million as its working range, treating 17.4 million as a credible lower bound. The evidence does not decide between 17 and 50 million, because the dispute turns on contested inputs rather than on a settled measurement.

Barro, Ursúa and Weng's 40.1 million total for 1918–1920 is the other major figure, and it is built on 48 countries rather than the whole world ([CEMLA](https://www.cemla.org/actividades/2021-final/2021-07-conference-frbny-ecb/SI.1(PAPER)Barro_Ursua_Weng.pdf)). Their yearly figures as quoted (25.0 million in 1918, 9.2 million in 1919 and 2.8 million in 1920) sum to 37.0 million, not 40.1 million, so one figure in the source needs checking. The 48-country sample also explains part of the gap: it excludes many countries that the 2018 study extrapolates from, so the two totals are not measuring exactly the same population. The evidence does not settle whether 40.1 or 17.4 million is closer to the true figure.

The estimates have moved over time. Jordan's 1920s calculation gave about 21.5 million, Patterson and Pyle (1991) gave 24.7 to 39.3 million, and Johnson and Mueller (2002) reached about 50 million ([Johnson & Mueller, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11875246/)). Murray et al.'s projection of 51 to 81 million deaths concerns a hypothetical 1918-type pandemic in today's population, not a re-estimate of the 1918 toll ([NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/health/health-news/flu-pandemic-could-kill-81-million-people-flna1c9473318)).

The US figures differ mainly because the registration area did not cover the whole country: about 77 percent of the population was in the death registration area in 1918 ([Garenne, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2740912/)). Among the tolls, India is the most contested. Estimates of 10 to 20 million, and a 12 million figure described as the largest national toll ([EPW/IIPS](https://iipsindia.ac.in/sites/default/files/Influenza_Pandemic_TV_Sekher_EPW-2021.pdf)), rest on census-based excess mortality, and the 1921 census was the first point at which excess mortality could be measured ([Journal of Economic History](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-economic-history/article/demographic-shocks-and-womens-labor-market-participation-evidence-from-the-1918-influenza-pandemic-in-india/BF5ABA7397D01389E72F6E87865C2A12)). Chandra and Christensen argue that Spreeuwenberg's India figure of 6.17 million is a significant underestimate, because it relied on official death rates that underreport deaths ([AJE letter](https://academic.oup.com/aje/article-abstract/188/7/1404/5368300?redirectedFrom=fulltext)). This matters for the global total, since India's figure heavily shapes the 17.4 million estimate.

Coverage is thinnest in regions that the major global datasets omit. A recent review notes that Eastern Europe, the Arab Middle East and parts of Africa are not covered in these estimates because data were unavailable ([Cambridge Elements](https://www.cambridge.org/core/elements/191820-influenza-pandemic/E2695A213FACA1CD66D1AC9144EE7FD1)). The Kenya study describes East African records as very sparse ([PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6631354/)), and it reports pandemic-era deaths of about 25 per 1,000 population from September 1918 to March 1919 ([MDPI](https://mdpi.com/2414-6366/4/2/91/htm)).

## Records gaps and age patterns widen the uncertainty

Every estimate accepts that recorded deaths are too low, so the disagreement is about how to correct them. Records were undercounted in several ways. A US CDC Museum exhibit notes that the total mortality data relied on only a small part of the country, and that states including Alabama, Georgia, Mississippi and Texas were not in the statistical record ([CDC Museum](https://cdcmuseum.org/exhibits/show/influenza/1918-influenza-pandemic/mortality-stats-1918)). Chandra and Christensen cite a government report that underregistration in colonial India ranged from 35% to 55% depending on region ([AJE letter](https://academic.oup.com/aje/article-abstract/188/7/1404/5368300?redirectedFrom=fulltext)). In Japan, official vital records are widely recognised as affected by underreporting ([EID/PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3647405/)). No source quantifies how much of the 17 to 100 million range comes from records problems rather than from methods.

The age pattern also affects the models, because excess-mortality estimates depend on age-specific rates. A 2026 study of 48 populations finds that young adults aged 20 to 40 had higher excess mortality than other groups, but it finds that excess mortality among the youngest children and the elderly varied across populations ([IJE](https://academic.oup.com/ije/article/55/3/dyag061/8679000); [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13178679/)). In Copenhagen, people over 65 showed no measurable excess in 1918–1919 ([Vaccine](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0264410X11003008?via=ihub)). A 2013 Canada and US study found that the mortality peak was at age 28 and argued that it reflected early exposure to the 1889–90 pandemic ([PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0069586)). Claims that as many as 95 to 99 percent of deaths were in young adults are weakly supported; one 2014 commentary makes the 95% claim while citing a source that was not verified ([AJE](https://academic.oup.com/aje/article/187/12/2503/5091398)). The age pattern is consistent across studies, but its effect on the global total is unquantified.

## Conclusion

The pandemic killed millions, and the best-supported answer is a range rather than a point. The 1918–1920 death toll is most usefully stated as roughly 20 to 50 million worldwide, with the 2018 figure of 17.4 million and the 2002 figure of 50 million marking the lower and upper ends of the serious scholarly debate. The 100 million figure appears repeatedly but rests largely on the same upward-adjustment reasoning as the 50 million estimate, so it should not be read as independent confirmation.

The disagreement is narrower than the headline range suggests, because most of it turns on one input. India's national figure, which varies from about 6 million in the sanitary commissioner's registration count to about 20 million in the upper models, determines much of the gap between Spreeuwenberg's total and the others. Resolving that would require better national data, and the Eastern European, Middle Eastern and African estimates remain largely unmeasured. Anyone citing a single figure should name the source and whether it measures recorded deaths or excess mortality.
