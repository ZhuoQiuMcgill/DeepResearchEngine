# Challenger broke apart during ascent, not re-entry, from a cold-stiffened O-ring

The notes contradict the question's premise. Challenger did not break apart during re-entry. It broke apart during ascent, about 73 seconds after launch on 28 January 1986, and the orbiter never reached orbit ([Wikipedia, STS-51-L](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/STS-51-L); [NASA, STS-51L](https://www.nasa.gov/mission/sts-51l/)). The official cause was the failure of the pressure seal in the aft field joint of the right solid rocket booster. Hot gas leaked through that joint, reached the external tank, and set off the breakup. The Rogers Commission tied the seal failure to cold temperature, which stiffened the O-rings, and to a flawed joint design. It also found that Morton Thiokol and NASA managers overrode engineers' warnings, and that the launch decision rested on incomplete information and a management structure that let safety problems bypass key managers. Post-accident reforms created new safety offices and a cold-weather launch limit, but the 2003 Columbia board found the safety function still "largely silent and ineffective." The cause is well documented, though the temperature evidence and the weight given to management culture remain contested.

## Key terms and how they are measured

"Break-up" is measured here as the onset of structural failure on the telemetry timeline. The Wikipedia timeline, which reproduces NASA telemetry, places it at T+73.162 seconds at an altitude of 14,600 m (47,900 ft) ([Wikipedia, Timeline of the STS-51-L mission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline_of_the_STS-51-L_mission)). "Re-entry" is the descent back through the atmosphere after orbit. No source in the notes describes Challenger doing this. The only descent after the breakup was the crew cabin's ballistic fall, which "peaked at an altitude of 65,000 feet approximately 25 seconds after breakup" and struck the ocean about two minutes and 45 seconds after breakup ([NASA, Challenger Crew Report](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/space-shuttle/sts-51l/challenger-crew-report/)). The "re-entry" wording most likely confuses this accident with Columbia's 2003 loss during re-entry, but no source in the notes establishes that link.

## What happened in flight

The failure chain began at liftoff. Smoke appeared at the right booster's field joint within the first second, and a solid-fuel "plug" temporarily sealed it until wind shear dislodged it around T+36 seconds ([Wikipedia, Timeline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline_of_the_STS-51-L_mission)). Camera records show a plume at the aft strut around T+58 seconds and flame on the external tank by T+60 seconds, with the plume changing shape around T+64 seconds, consistent with burn-through of the liquid hydrogen tank ([Wikipedia, Timeline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline_of_the_STS-51-L_mission)). The Rogers Commission said the lower strut was severed or pulled away from the weakened hydrogen tank, allowing the right booster to rotate around the upper strut ([NASA, Rogers Commission Vol. 1 ch. 3](https://www.nasa.gov/history/rogersrep/v1ch3.htm)). Telemetry shows lateral acceleration at T+72.525 seconds, and the Pilot's "Uh-oh" came about half a second later ([Wikipedia, Timeline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline_of_the_STS-51-L_mission)). Radar and optical data confirmed a catastrophic breakup ([NASA, Rogers Commission Vol. 3 appendix O](https://www.nasa.gov/history/rogersrep/v3appo.htm)).

The mission was STS-51-L, the 25th Space Shuttle mission and Challenger's tenth flight. It was to deploy the TDRS-B communications satellite, and the planned landing at Kennedy Space Center came about six days after launch ([Encyclopedia Astronautica, STS-51-L](https://astronautix.com/s/sts-51-l.html); [Wikipedia, TDRS-B](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TDRS-B)). Seven crew members died ([NASA, STS-51L](https://www.nasa.gov/mission/sts-51l/)). NASA's crew report says it is "possible, but not certain" that the crew lost consciousness after the loss of cabin pressure, and that the cause of death "cannot be positively determined" ([NASA, Challenger Crew Report](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/space-shuttle/sts-51l/challenger-crew-report/)).

Sources differ slightly on exact timings. The Rogers Commission places the lower strut failure at about 72.20 seconds, while the timeline gives 73.16 seconds for the breakup onset ([NASA, Rogers Commission Vol. 1 ch. 3](https://www.nasa.gov/history/rogersrep/v1ch3.htm); [Wikipedia, Timeline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline_of_the_STS-51-L_mission)). The more precise figures are secondary, so "about 73 seconds" is the safer statement.

## Cause of the break-up: the seal, the cold, and a disputed temperature record

The Rogers Commission concluded that the loss was caused by the failure of the pressure seal in the aft field joint of the right solid rocket motor. It found that a combustion-gas leak through that joint weakened or penetrated the external tank, initiating breakup, and that no other shuttle element or the payload contributed and sabotage was not a factor ([NASA, Rogers Commission Ch. IV](https://www.nasa.gov/history/rogersrep/v1ch4.htm)). The commission described the joint as faulty in design and "unacceptably sensitive" to temperature, physical dimensions, materials, reusability, processing and dynamic loading ([NASA, Rogers Commission Ch. IV](https://www.nasa.gov/history/rogersrep/v1ch4.htm)).

The temperature mechanism is the central technical point. The commission found that O-ring resiliency is directly related to temperature, and that at the cold launch temperature the O-ring probably would not be pressure-actuated to seal the gap in time ([NASA, Rogers Commission Ch. IV](https://www.nasa.gov/history/rogersrep/v1ch4.htm)). The ambient launch temperature was **36 °F**, and the estimated temperature at the joint was **28 °F ± 5 °F** ([NASA, Rogers Commission Ch. IV](https://www.nasa.gov/history/rogersrep/v1ch4.htm)). The joint figure is an estimate, not a measurement. Other factors compounded the risk: ice could unseat the secondary O-ring, putty could delay pressure actuation, and the joint gap opened about 0.029 and 0.017 inches at the primary and secondary O-rings ([NASA, Rogers Commission Ch. IV](https://www.nasa.gov/history/rogersrep/v1ch4.htm)).

**The temperature evidence is disputed, and the notes contain two sides.** The Rogers Commission's distress table shows that of 21 launches at or above 61 °F, only four showed O-ring thermal distress, and that every launch below 61 °F showed distress in one or more O-rings ([NASA, Rogers Commission Ch. IV](https://www.nasa.gov/history/rogersrep/v1ch4.htm)). An unreviewed blog analysis argues that if only the flights showing distress are plotted, temperature looks uncorrelated between 53 °F and 75 °F ([Mechacker News](https://kunnas.com/mn/MN-000040)). This report follows the Rogers table. It uses all flights, including those without distress, it is an official primary document, and the blog does not cite its data. The blog's point is nonetheless a fair caution: conditioning on distress is a biased sample, and the sub-scale tests did not reproduce the real joint, so the evidence does not settle the exact temperature threshold ([NASA, Rogers Commission Ch. IV](https://www.nasa.gov/history/rogersrep/v1ch4.htm)).

The launch temperature itself is also reported inconsistently. The primary report gives 36 °F ambient, while Feynman's public account gives 32 °F ([Feynman.com, The Challenger Disaster](http://feynman.com/science/the-challenger-disaster/)), and a popular source gives about −2 °C for the booster joint ([Make Tech Easier](https://maketecheasier.com/mte-q-how-did-a-cracked-rubber-o-ring-the-size-of-a-coin-stiffened-by-a-florida-cold-snap-in-january-1986-destroy-a-space-shuttle-73-seconds-after-launch/)). This report uses the primary report's figures, since they come from the investigation's own data.

The debris record adds one open question. About **20 percent** of the external tank was recovered, mostly intertank and lower hydrogen-tank pieces ([NASA, Rogers Commission Ch. IV](https://www.nasa.gov/history/rogersrep/v1ch4.htm); [AmericaSpace](https://www.americaspace.com/2014/01/25/missed-warnings-the-fatal-flaws-which-doomed-challenger-part-1/)). Post-accident NTSB analysis, reported in April 1986, described "aerodynamic breakup of the entire orbiter" ([UPI Archives](https://www.upi.com/Archives/1986/04/09/Challengers-nose-section-with-the-astronauts-inside-apparently-ripped/7900186430659/)). Recovered hydrogen debris showed only mild surface scorching, which is in tension with the timeline's account of a tank burn-through ([NASA, Rogers Commission Vol. 2 enclosure 6](https://www.nasa.gov/history/rogersrep/v3appoe6.htm)). The notes do not resolve this, so the severity of the tank burn-through remains the main open point in the physical record.

## Responsibility: warnings overruled before launch

The most consequential finding is that the warnings were documented and were overridden. Roger Boisjoly wrote a memo in July 1985 warning that the booster design could lead to a catastrophic event ([Wikipedia, Roger Boisjoly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roger_Boisjoly)). Morton Thiokol had reported O-ring design problems as early as 1979 ([EBSCO, Challenger Accident](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/challenger-accident/)). On the night before launch, Thiokol engineers recommended no launch below **53 °F**, the lowest previous launch temperature ([Wikipedia, Rogers Commission Report](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rogers_Commission_Report)). A second source gives the recommendation as 12 °C, which is close to 53 °F ([Make Tech Easier](https://maketecheasier.com/mte-q-how-did-a-cracked-rubber-o-ring-the-size-of-a-coin-stiffened-by-a-florida-cold-snap-in-january-1986-destroy-a-space-shuttle-73-seconds-after-launch/)). Thiokol's vice president Bob Lund said unless the temperature reached at least 53 degrees, "I don't want to fly" ([Time, A Serious Deficiency](https://time.com/archive/6705671/a-serious-deficiency/)).

NASA's Mulloy insisted there was no demonstrable link between temperature and O-ring erosion ([Time, A Serious Deficiency](https://time.com/archive/6705671/a-serious-deficiency/)). Thiokol management first adopted the no-launch recommendation, then reversed it after NASA challenged the evidence ([Lingenfelter](https://davidlingenfelter.substack.com/p/challenger-when-the-system-knew)). Sources differ on how the reversal happened. Wikipedia says senior management overruled engineers after a 30-minute offline caucus ([Wikipedia, Rogers Commission Report](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rogers_Commission_Report)). Boisjoly says the engineering team was deliberately excluded from the management meeting ([Wikipedia, Roger Boisjoly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roger_Boisjoly)). Allan McDonald, who did not sign the launch approval, says Thiokol supervisors approved it by fax to NASA ([Wikipedia, Allan J. McDonald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allan_J._McDonald)). The commission concluded that senior decision-makers did not receive all relevant facts and that it was "highly unlikely" they would have launched had they known them ([IHMM](https://ihmm.org/when-speaking-up-is-not-enough-how-professionals-intervene-by-david-j-peters-cshm-chmm-cmq-oe/)).

The gap between engineers and managers on risk is also stark. NASA managers' estimate of catastrophic failure was about 1 in 100,000, while low-level engineers put it near 1 in 100 ([CSMonitor, 1986](https://www.csmonitor.com/1986/0612/a1space9.html)).

## Organisational interpretations: where the sources disagree

The Rogers Commission found that NASA and Morton Thiokol both failed to respond adequately to the known flaw, and that both came to treat erosion and blow-by as an acceptable risk because they "got away with it last time" ([NASA History, Rogers Report ch. 6](https://www.nasa.gov/history/rogersrep/v1ch6.htm)). Sociologist Diane Vaughan's study places the cause in NASA's "culture of production," which normalised deviance, and she explicitly found no malice ([H-Net review](https://www.h-net.org/reviews/showrev.php?id=364); [Flight Safety Australia](https://www.flightsafetyaustralia.com/2017/05/safety-in-mind-normalisation-of-deviance/)). McDonald places more of the blame on NASA engineers who pressured Thiokol into agreeing to launch ([Wikipedia, Allan J. McDonald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allan_J._McDonald)). Feynman's appendix argued that NASA management had inverted the meaning of safety language ([NASA fandom mirror, Rogers Commission Report](https://nasa.fandom.com/wiki/Rogers_Commission_Report)). Notably, these disputes concern how complete the explanation is, not whether the seal failed. The sources found do not show independent engineers contesting the O-ring mechanism.

The commission's view that the failure was one of information flow and the view that it was one of management judgment are both present in the record. The sources do not settle which weighed more. The commission's wording combines both.

## Aftermath: reforms, return to flight and the 2003 verdict

The commission recommended an independent Office of Safety, Reliability and Quality Assurance reporting to the Administrator, a Shuttle Safety Panel, and a flight rate matched to resources ([NASA History, Rogers recommendations](https://www.nasa.gov/history/rogersrep/v1recomm.htm)). NASA Administrator James Fletcher established the office on 8 July 1986 ([NASA SP-4219, ch. 15](https://www.nasa.gov/history/SP-4219/Chapter15.html)). Its redesign recommendations led to a capture latch, custom shims, a third O-ring serving as seat and thermal barrier, heaters to keep the joint above 75 °F, and a switch to Viton seals ([NASA, Implementation of the Recommendations](https://www.nasa.gov/history/rogersrep/v6ch1.htm)). Because that page is a 1987 status document, the notes do not confirm the final as-flown configuration. A trade press report also describes NASA selecting a new booster design to replace the joint ([C&EN](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/cen-v064n033.p005b)).

The launch-temperature rule changed slowly. Before the accident, the launch criteria set ambient temperature at 31 °F, and there were no joint-temperature criteria ([NASA History, Rogers hearings](https://www.nasa.gov/history/rogersrep/v5part1a.htm)). By the late 1990s, a rule barred launch at 35 °F or colder, with a waiver the launch director could approve with the safety director's concurrence ([NASA STS-103 launch criteria](https://lhea.gsfc.nasa.gov/archive/sm3a/sts103-landing.html)). A secondary blog says NASA changed cold-weather criteria after Challenger, but cites no primary source, so the notes do not confirm that claim ([Sentinel Mission](https://sentinelmission.org/blog/what-happened-after-the-challenger-disaster/)).

Return to flight came with STS-26 on Discovery, launched **29 September 1988** after a **32-month** hiatus ([NASA History, STS-26](https://www.nasa.gov/history/30-years-ago-sts-26-returns-shuttle-to-flight/)). NASA reported **more than 200 changes** to the Shuttle system, including a redesigned solid rocket motor, a crew escape pole and full-pressure suits ([NASA History, STS-26](https://www.nasa.gov/history/30-years-ago-sts-26-returns-shuttle-to-flight/)). NASA's STS-26 press kit counts **more than 100 mandatory modifications** to the orbiter ([NASA STS-26 press kit](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/sts-026r-press-kit.pdf)). The two figures cover different scopes, the whole system and the orbiter, so they do not conflict.

The Columbia Accident Investigation Board judged the reforms insufficient. It concluded that NASA had failed to learn many of Challenger's lessons ([Wikipedia, Space Shuttle Challenger disaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Shuttle_Challenger_disaster)). It found that by 2003, practices present at Challenger, including inadequate concern over deviations, a silent safety programme and schedule pressure, had returned ([MIT, Columbia lessons](http://sunnyday.mit.edu/papers/issc04-final.pdf)). It described the safety programme as "largely silent and ineffective" ([CAIB Vol. I](https://ehss.energy.gov/deprep/archive/documents/0308_caib_report_volume1.pdf)). A Wikipedia statement that NASA implemented all recommended changes conflicts with these CAIB findings, and the report follows the CAIB ([Wikipedia, Columbia Accident Investigation Board](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia_Accident_Investigation_Board)).

## Conclusion

The core answer is clear. Challenger broke apart about 73 seconds into its ascent because hot gas leaked through a cold-stiffened O-ring seal in the right booster joint, and the external tank then failed. The mechanism rests on primary NASA and Rogers Commission documents. Responsibility is documented as well: engineers warned in writing, recommended no launch below 53 °F, and were overruled after a reversal. Whether that failure is best described as one of management culture, pressure from NASA, or information flow remains contested.

The evidence also suggests that the lesson was only partly learned. The reforms created new offices and procedures, and the cold-weather limit later became more explicit, but the CAIB found that the same safety and schedule pressures had returned. For anyone reading the 1986 record today, the most useful takeaway is that a known technical risk was defined, documented and then accepted through organisational decisions. The gaps that remain, including the exact temperature threshold, the tank burn-through severity and the final flight configuration of the redesigned joint, do not change that central conclusion.
