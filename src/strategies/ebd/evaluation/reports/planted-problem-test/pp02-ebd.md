# Galileo Refined and Used the Telescope to Remake Astronomy

Galileo did not invent the telescope. Dutch spectacle makers did, and the earliest firm record is a patent application filed in **October 1608**, roughly a year before Galileo's first instrument ([AAS](https://aas.org/posts/story/2017/10/month-astronomical-history-invention-telescope); [NOVA, PBS](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/nova/education/tech/galileos-telescope.html)). What Galileo did was build his own instrument from 1609, raise its magnification from about 3x to roughly 20x by late 1609 and to around 30x by early 1610, and point it systematically at the sky ([ADS, 2009 study](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ExA....25...17S/abstract); [AAS](https://aas.org/posts/story/2017/10/month-astronomical-history-invention-telescope)). His 1610 *Sidereus Nuncius* reported lunar relief, four moons of Jupiter, a star-resolved Milky Way and Saturn's "ears," and these findings moved astronomy from naked-eye observation toward instrument-based observation. Their acceptance was uneven: the Jupiter moons and Venus phases were checked within about a year, while the Copernican meaning of the observations and the reality of sunspots and Saturn's ring were argued for decades. Telescope astronomy then spread through better instrument designs and state-funded observatories, though the sources do not show the telescope alone produced these changes.

## Dutch spyglass of 1608 came before Galileo

A telescope here means an optical instrument that makes distant objects look larger and nearer. The earliest form was a spyglass with a convex objective and a concave eyepiece, and its power is measured as magnification, such as "3x" for three times larger. Historians define an "invention" as the first documented making of the instrument, while an "improvement" raises its power or makes it useful for a purpose. Under these definitions, the **strongest documentary evidence** is Hans Lippershey's application for a 30-year patent to the States General on **2 October 1608**, described as the first written record of an instrument "for seeing things far away as if they were near" ([AAS](https://aas.org/posts/story/2017/10/month-astronomical-history-invention-telescope)). A History Today account adds that Lippershey showed a spyglass to Prince Maurice on **25 September 1608** and filed a week later ([History Today](https://www.historytoday.com/archive/who-invented-telescope)). Lippershey's instrument is described as having about **3x magnification** ([Space.com](https://www.space.com/21950-who-invented-the-telescope.html)).

The priority question is not fully settled. Jacob Metius of Alkmaar applied for a similar design weeks later, and the States General discussed his application, which was denied ([EBSCO](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/biography/hans-lippershey/)). Zacharias Janssen's claim rests on later testimony: his son said his father built a telescope around 1590 or 1604, and historian Cornelis de Waard found a **1634** note by Isaac Beeckman recording the 1604 version ([Wikipedia, Zacharias Janssen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zacharias_Janssen)). A **1655** testimony by William Boreel is the other main support, and a **1816** investigation found problems with it, including the 1608 patent records and the lack of any record of Janssen as a spectacle maker before 1615 ([Wikipedia, Zacharias Janszoon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zacharias_Janszoon)). EBSCO's Janssen entry therefore treats the documented patent applications of Lippershey and Metius as the strongest claims ([EBSCO](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/biography/zacharias-janssen/)). Cornelis Drebbel's description of telescopes in **1609** predates Galileo's public use, but it is reported only through secondary sources ([Jacob Metius, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacob_Metius)). Van Helden's **1974** paper in *Isis* changed the debate from who was the true inventor to how the instrument was developed ([KNAW](https://dwc.knaw.nl/wp-content/HSSN/2011-12-Origins.pdf)).

## Galileo's instrument reached about 20x to 33x

Galileo's contribution is best seen in the numbers. Popular and reference sources agree that the Dutch instrument was about 3x, but the 4–10x range reported for some early Dutch telescopes conflicts with that figure ([Tel Aviv University](https://www.tau.ac.il/education/muse/museum/galileo/telescope.html)). Galileo's instrument, by contrast, progressed quickly. The table below sets out the reported stages and their sources.

| Date | Reported power | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Summer 1609 | Starting instrument, about 3x, reached within days | [WorldAtlas](https://www.worldatlas.com/space/who-invented-the-telescope-2026.html) |
| 25 August 1609 | 8–9x, shown to Venetian lawmakers | [MacTutor](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/SH/galileo_3_sh.pdf) |
| End of 1609 | About 20x | [AAS](https://aas.org/posts/story/2017/10/month-astronomical-history-invention-telescope) |
| Early 1610 | About 30x (2009 study) | [ADS, 2009 study](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009ExA....25...17S/abstract) |
| Maximum, undated | 33x (weak, aggregator source) | [Baidu Baike](https://baike.baidu.com/en/item/Galileo%20telescope/1495924) |

The **2009 Florence-led study** is the most authoritative technical account in the notes. It reports that Galileo began improving the Dutch spyglass in summer 1609 and reached 30x within months, and that he stopped the 37 mm objective down to a 15 mm aperture with a diaphragm to sharpen images ([Academia.edu, 2009 study](https://www.academia.edu/89121435/Galileo_s_telescope_history_scientific_analysis_and_replicated_observations)). The surviving instrument's magnification is **21x**, because its original eyepiece was lost in the 19th century ([Academia.edu, 2009 study](https://www.academia.edu/89121435/Galileo_s_telescope_history_scientific_analysis_and_replicated_observations)). Galileo's design kept the convex objective and concave eyepiece in a long tube, and its main limit was a narrow field, about half the width of the Moon ([ATNF](https://www.atnf.csiro.au/resources/education/senior-astrophysics/observing/galileo/)). The historians' disagreement concerns method, not invention. The 2009 study notes that Sidereus Nuncius and Galileo's letters say what he did with the instrument but disclose no theory of it ([ResearchGate, 2009 study](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/225716816_Galileo%27s_telescope_History_scientific_analysis_and_replicated_observations)). A PhilArchive analysis describes the received view that the first spyglass had no theory of magnification and that Galileo improved its power ([PhilArchive](https://philarchive.org/archive/YAAGKO)), while another argues that improving magnification was "too intricate a problem" to solve by chance ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/257417960_Magnification_How_to_turn_a_spyglass_into_an_astronomical_telescope)). The Worth Library notes that Galileo was not the only observer with a telescope in summer 1609 but was the first to publish his findings ([Edward Worth Library](https://astronomy.edwardworthlibrary.ie/astronomy-and-astronomers/galileo-and-the-telescope/)).

## Galileo's 1609–1610 observations and their contested reception

The core evidence for what Galileo saw is his book *Sidereus Nuncius* (March 1610), which described lunar surface features, the Jupiter satellites and telescopic views of the Milky Way with drawings ([Museo Galileo](https://catalogue.museogalileo.it/object/GalileoGalileiSidereusNunciusFacsimile.html)). He estimated lunar mountains at least four miles high from sunlit peaks beyond the terminator ([Wikipedia, Sidereus Nuncius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidereus_Nuncius)). On **7 January 1610** he first noted three Jupiter moons, taking them for fixed stars, and by **15 January** he had concluded they orbited Jupiter ([Wikipedia, Galilean moons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galilean_moons)). Their significance was that they showed some bodies orbit a centre other than Earth, though they did not refute geocentrism alone, because Jupiter could move while keeping its companions ([Gingerich, Chapin Library](https://chapin.williams.edu/pasachoff/galileo.html)).

Confirmation came first from Jesuits. At Cardinal Bellarmine's request, the Collegio Romano professors confirmed Galileo's telescopic observations in **1611**, with Clavius dissenting on the Moon's surface ([Stanford Encyclopedia](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2018/entries/galileo/); [Vatican Observatory PDF](https://www.vaticanobservatory.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/Jesuits-and-Galileo.pdf)). Clavius hesitated over the lunar irregularities and said he could not see the four moons through his telescope ([Coyne, Jesuits and Galileo](https://www.scienceonthenet.eu/content/article/george-v-coyne-sj/jesuits-and-galileo-tradition-and-adventure-discovery/february); [Wikipedia, Christopher Clavius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_Clavius)). Lunar mountains were therefore the most persistently disputed of the early claims ([Wikipedia, Galileo affair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galileo_affair)). Some opponents disputed the reality of the moons on other grounds. Francesco Sizzi argued that there could be no more than seven planets, though his orbital calculation rested on an erroneous Callisto position ([Astroblog](http://astroblogger.blogspot.com/2009/03/blogging-starry-messenger-jupiter.html)). Martin Horký reported that Magini and another observer could not see the satellites and attributed the findings to glass distortion ([Wikipedia, Martin Horký](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Hork%C3%BD)).

Priority is contested. Simon Marius claimed observations from late November 1609 and published in 1614, but his oldest surviving satellite observation dates from late December 1610 ([Rice Galileo Project](https://galileo.library.rice.edu/sci/marius.html)). Other scholars hold that he discovered the satellites nearly simultaneously and independently ([ResearchGate, Marius](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/333890720_Simon_Marius's_Mundus_Iovialis_and_the_Discovery_of_the_Moons_of_Jupiter)). Galileo accused Marius of plagiarism in *Il Saggiatore* (1623) ([ResearchGate, Marius](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/333890720_Simon_Marius's_Mundus_Iovialis_and_the_Discovery_of_the_Moons_of_Jupiter)). Kepler's reception is also contested: one source says his own observations with a telescope Galileo sent to Rudolf II confirmed Galileo's findings ([arXiv 1407.7380](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1407.7380)), while the Linda Hall Library reports that Kepler lacked a telescope good enough to replicate them ([Linda Hall Library](https://www.lindahall.org/about/news/three-bright-galilean-stars-three-copies-of-sidereus-nuncius-in-kansas-city/)). The Venus phases were the most decisive observation against the Ptolemaic system, because Venus should show only a narrow sickle in that system ([Oxford](https://www.cabinet.ox.ac.uk/galileos-most-decisive-telescopic-observation-phases-venus-1610-23-0)). The phases fit both the Copernican and Tychonic systems, so they excluded only Ptolemy ([Wikipedia, Phases of Venus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phases_of_Venus)). Galileo's first published account of the phases appeared in the **1613** *Letters on Sunspots* rather than in *Sidereus Nuncius* ([Physics UNLV](https://www.physics.unlv.edu/~jeffery/astro/ptolemy/venus_phases.html)). Palmieri argues that there is no proof Galileo received Castelli's letter of 11 December 1610 ([Palmieri, JHA 2001](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/002182860103200202)).

Sunspots and Saturn took longer. Christoph Scheiner discovered sunspots in March or April 1611 and argued they were small bodies crossing the Sun, while Galileo argued they lay on or near its surface ([Rice Galileo Project](https://galileo.library.rice.edu/sci/scheiner.html); [University of Chicago Press](https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/O/bo8540269.html)). Scheiner's *Rosa Ursina* of **1630** followed more than fifteen years of observation ([UCAR](https://www2.hao.ucar.edu/education/scientists/christoph-scheiner-1575-1650)). Saturn was the clearest failure of the 1610 telescope. Galileo saw a "three-bodied" planet, and the ears disappeared around 1612 before returning by 1616 ([Museo Galileo](https://catalogue.museogalileo.it/indepth/SaturnsRings.html); [Astronomy.com](https://www.astronomy.com/today-in-the-history-of-astronomy/july-30-1610-galileo-sees-saturns-rings/)). Huygens resolved the enigma as a ring, set out in *Systema Saturnium* (**1659**) ([Museo Galileo](https://catalogue.museogalileo.it/indepth/SaturnsRings.html)).

## After 1610: better instruments and state observatories

The telescope's design changed quickly after Galileo. Kepler described a two-convex-lens telescope in **1611** but never made one, and the design had a wider field and higher magnification ([AIP](https://history.aip.org/exhibits/cosmology/tools/tools-first-telescopes.htm); [ADS, JAHH 2022](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022JAHH...25..511M/abstract)). Its higher power brought more aberration and difficult alignment ([AIP](https://history.aip.org/exhibits/cosmology/tools/tools-first-telescopes.htm)). The first printed mention of a two-convex-lens telescope appeared in Scheiner's *Rosa Ursina* (**1631**), and a Keplerian telescope was made in **1645** ([arXiv 1704.05661](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1704.05661)). The origin of the astronomical telescope is disputed: Fontana's claim to have made one in 1608 was "generally not taken seriously," and the earliest reliable dating is about 1615–1616 ([ADS, JAHH 2022](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022JAHH...25..511M/abstract)). Newton's **1668** reflector, motivated by his conclusion that refractors would always suffer from colour, avoided lens aberration but "remained largely a curiosity" until the 18th century ([AIP, Early Reflectors](https://history.aip.org/exhibits/cosmology/tools/tools-early-reflectors.htm)). James Gregory had published a reflector design in **1663**, so Newton was not first ([Aeon](https://aeon.co/ideas/how-many-great-minds-does-it-take-to-invent-a-telescope)). Well into the 18th century, astronomers mainly used long refractors ([AIP, Early Reflectors](https://history.aip.org/exhibits/cosmology/tools/tools-early-reflectors.htm)).

Observing methods also changed. Huygens discovered Titan on **25 March 1655** while studying Saturn's rings ([Astronomy.com](https://www.astronomy.com/today-in-the-history-of-astronomy/march-25-1655-christiaan-huygens-discovers-titan/)). The magnification of his telescope is reported as 50x by [EBSCO](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/huygenss-contributions-astronomy/) and 43x by [Our Planet](https://ourplnt.com/christiaan-huygens-discovered-titan/), so the figure is uncertain. Only the objective of the 1655 telescope survives ([Carl Kop](https://carlkop.home.xs4all.nl/huyglens.html)). Giovanni Domenico Cassini found the gap between Saturn's rings A and B in **1675**, which showed that the rings were two segments rather than one solid ring ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/science/Cassinis-Division)). The gap then informed theory: Laplace published in **1789** that the rings consisted of many small components, and Maxwell showed that a solid ring would be unstable ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Saturn-planet/The-ring-system); [Wikipedia, Rings of Saturn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rings_of_Saturn)). The telescope revealed the phenomenon, and mathematical physics explained it. Picard introduced a telescopic sight with a micrometer for measuring small angles, and by the mid-18th century the transit instrument was the standard positional tool ([The Cosmic Elk](http://www.cosmicelk.net/telrev.htm); [arXiv 1202.2005](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1202.2005)). Those micrometer and aerial-telescope claims rest on a secondary website and a student-level PDF, so they are less secure than the Huygens and Cassini dates ([Skyward Innovation](https://cuas.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/7/asgarosforum/318/Skyward-Innovation-The-Rise-of-Aerial-Telescopes-in-17th-and-18th-Century-Astronomy.pdf)).

Institutions followed. The Paris Observatory was founded in **1667** at Colbert's request ([Consulat général de France](https://hongkong.consulfrance.org/The-Paris-Observatory-at-the); [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Paris-Observatory)). Its first national almanac, the *Connaissance des temps*, appeared in **1679** to help sailors fix longitude with Jupiter's satellite eclipses ([Paris Observatory, Wikipedia mirror](https://rangerrik.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/10/Paris_Observatory)). Charles II founded the Royal Observatory at Greenwich on **22 June 1675** to address the longitude problem ([Astronomy.com](https://www.astronomy.com/today-in-the-history-of-astronomy/june-22-1675-the-royal-observatory-greenwich-is-founded/)). Its first Astronomer Royal, John Flamsteed, received £100 a year and later a £500 stipend to build the observatory ([The Observatory](https://www.the-observatory.org/history/history-of-the-royal-observatory/)). Greenwich's early work was positional astronomy, and later programmes shifted toward astrophysics ([Royal Observatory Greenwich](https://www.royalobservatorygreenwich.org/articles.php?article=1)). The institution closed as a research body in **1998** ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Royal-Greenwich-Observatory)). Because the sources show funding and purpose mainly for Paris and Greenwich, they cannot show how widely this model spread.

## Conclusion

The record supports a narrower claim than the question's premise. Galileo did not invent the telescope, and the 1608 Dutch patents, the Lippershey and Metius priority dispute, and the Janssen testimony are the documentary base for that. His contribution was to turn a spyglass into a systematic astronomical instrument, reaching roughly 20x to 30x within about a year, and to publish observations that others could test. The **1611 Jesuit confirmation** and the Venus phases show that the observations could be checked quickly, while Saturn, sunspots and the Copernican implications required decades of further work.

The sources do not provide the comparison the research question might need: none directly measures the telescope's lasting effect against new mathematics such as calculus or against theories of gravitation such as Newton's. Historians now study the instrument's development as a problem of experiment, lens-grinding and measurement, and a 2026 *Springer* article proposes a new account of 17th-century telescope performance, though its full text is not available here ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00016-026-00353-4)). The evidence therefore supports the view that the telescope changed astronomy through instruments, observing practice and institutions, while leaving open how much of that change was due to the instrument as opposed to the theory and mathematics around it.
