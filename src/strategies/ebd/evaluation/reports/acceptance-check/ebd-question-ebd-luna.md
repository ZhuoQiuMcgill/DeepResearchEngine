# Use Context to Guide Design

Environment-Based Design (EBD) is a methodology for changing an existing environment by designing and introducing an artifact; it starts from the environment, works for it, and changes it. Its core activities are **environment analysis, conflict identification, and solution generation**, pursued recursively rather than as a one-way checklist ([Zeng, “Environment-Based Design (EBD): a Methodology for Transdisciplinary Design”](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3233/jid-2015-0004)). Here, “environment” means the relevant components and relationships in which a product or system must work, including natural, built, and human contexts—not only nature or the product’s immediate physical surroundings ([Zeng, “Environment-Based Design (EBD)”](https://users.encs.concordia.ca/~design/reference/EBD-Final.pdf)). EBD’s purpose is to reveal requirements and tensions that may be unclear at the outset, then develop solutions that address them. This makes it relevant to open-ended design problems, although the reported applications do not establish that EBD is superior to other methods. The acronym is also used for **Evidence-Based Design**, a different approach, so the field and full name matter.

## EBD derives requirements from the environment

In EBD, designers first define the relevant system: its components, their relationships, and the lifecycle situations in which the product or service must operate. The environment can be organized into natural, built, and human categories, and examined across areas such as technology, manufacturing, assembly, and markets; which elements matter depends on the problem ([“Environment-Based Design of Software” thesis](https://www.collectionscanada.gc.ca/obj/thesescanada/vol2/QMG/TC-QMG-7194.pdf)). Requirements are not assumed to be fully specified in advance: the method treats the product’s working environment as a source of structural and performance requirements ([Zeng, “Environment-Based Formulation of Design Problem”](https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=2e65d497a65bdf667af0cff5b0e4a2084cc8a80b&repid=rep1&type=pdf).

A central tool is the **Recursive Object Model (ROM)**. Designers represent objects and relations, ask questions about what the objects are and how or why actions occur, add relevant answers to the model, and repeat the inquiry across lifecycle events ([Yong Zeng, “Environment-Based Design (EBD)”](https://users.encs.concordia.ca/~design/reference/EBD-Final.pdf)). This question-driven analysis is intended to expose relevant conditions without prematurely assuming what the final solution should look like. The resulting environment model is scoped to the design problem; the sources do not provide a complete inventory that would apply to every project.

## Three recursive activities connect analysis to solutions

The method’s three activities are **environment analysis**, which defines the current system; **conflict identification**, which finds undesired tensions among its relationships or requirements; and **solution generation**, which develops ways to address them ([Yong Zeng, “Environment-Based Design (EBD)”](https://users.encs.concordia.ca/~design/reference/EBD-Final.pdf)). These activities can proceed together and recur as the design develops, refining both the problem and the proposed response. A new solution itself becomes part of the environment to consider in the next cycle ([Yong Zeng, “Environment-Based Design (EBD)”](https://users.encs.concordia.ca/~design/reference/EBD-Final.pdf)).

A conflict may arise when an object lacks the resources needed to act as desired, or when it cannot accommodate actions imposed by its environment; another account describes conflicts as competing objects contending for resources ([“An Environment Based Design (EBD) Approach to…” thesis](https://spectrum.library.concordia.ca/id/eprint/985233/1/Ommi_MASc_S2019.pdf); [“Environment-Based Design of Software” thesis](https://www.collectionscanada.gc.ca/obj/thesescanada/vol2/QMG/TC-QMG-7194.pdf)). ROM features such as multiple constraints or multiple relations can flag **potential** conflicts for examination, but do not by themselves prove that a conflict is real or unresolved ([Yong Zeng, “Environment-Based Design (EBD)”](https://users.encs.concordia.ca/~design/reference/EBD-Final.pdf)). Concept generation then develops and refines a product description—including its technology, working principles, and form—against the requirements ([“Environment-Based Design of Software” thesis](https://www.collectionscanada.gc.ca/obj/thesescanada/vol2/QMG/TC-QMG-7194.pdf)).

## Applications range from products to public services

Published examples show EBD being applied beyond conventional mechanical products. They include a rivet-setting tool and a train-passenger litter-disposal design exercise involving **seven graduate-student participants** ([“362, Int. J. Manufacturing Research”](https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=27c5afac37bc1d7f353f1efe8e8c288fe4decdff&repid=rep1&type=pdf); [Yong Zeng, “Environment-Based Design (EBD)”](https://users.encs.concordia.ca/~design/reference/EBD-Final.pdf)), as well as a telecom-expense-management software application ([“Environment-Based Design of Software” thesis](https://www.collectionscanada.gc.ca/obj/thesescanada/vol2/QMG/TC-QMG-7194.pdf)). A medical-device case describes an automated system for reading lateral-flow tests, including software compatible with a laboratory information management system; its authors report **four prototypes before worldwide release** ([“Medical Devices Design Based on EBD: A Case Study”](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/302281152_MEDICAL_DEVICES_DESIGN_BASED_ON_EBD_A_CASE_STUDY)). Other examples include an elevator product-service system, a municipal flow-monitoring quality-management manual and related data-processing software, and aircraft-pylon design ([Yuyang Shi, “Environment-Based Design (EBD) Approach to Formalize Product-Service Systems (PSS) Design Process”](https://spectrum.library.concordia.ca/986816/1/Shi_MASc_F2020.pdf); [Sun, Zeng, and Zhou, “Environment-Based Design (EBD) Approach to Developing Quality Management Systems: A Case Study”](https://users.encs.concordia.ca/~design/reference/JIDPS-EBD-QMS.pdf); [Yang, Dou, and Zeng, “Environment-Based Design (EBD): Using Only Necessary Knowledge for Designer Creativity”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/proceedings-of-the-design-society/article/environmentbased-design-ebd-using-only-necessary-knowledge-for-designer-creativity/D8E51379968F4823E0BC19E017F7C075)).

The approach can also be used to diagnose a problem rather than produce a finished design: a Nairobi municipal-solid-waste study applied environment analysis and conflict identification to identify critical issues ([Du et al., “Environment-Based Design (EBD) Approach to Identify Critical Issues in Managing Municipal Solid Waste: Nairobi, Kenyan Case Study”](https://isprs-archives.copernicus.org/articles/XLVI-4-W1-2021/21/2021/)). A quality-management case illustrates why the method may help when customer requirements are unclear: a request for a quality manual did not specify its content or structure, so analysis of the organization and its processes helped shape the manual and supporting software ([Sun, Zeng, and Zhou, “Environment-Based Design (EBD) Approach to Developing Quality Management Systems”](https://users.encs.concordia.ca/~design/reference/JIDPS-EBD-QMS.pdf)). These cases demonstrate a range of uses, not a comprehensive comparison of EBD with alternative methods.

## Distinguish Environment-Based from Evidence-Based Design

The acronym EBD is ambiguous. **Evidence-Based Design** uses credible research to inform built-environment decisions and seeks to assess outcomes; this differs from Environment-Based Design’s emphasis on modeling the product’s environment, identifying conflicts, and generating solutions ([The Center for Health Design, “About EBD”](https://www.healthdesign.org/certification-outreach/edac/about-ebd)). Healthcare architecture’s use of an eight-step EBD process refers to Evidence-Based Design, not the method described here ([The Center for Health Design, “About EBD”](https://www.healthdesign.org/certification-outreach/edac/about-ebd)). The wording “environmental-based design” appears in a thesis describing an approach attributed to Zeng, but the available sources do not establish it as a separate, standardized method ([Cheligeer, “An EBD-Enabled Design Knowledge”](https://spectrum.library.concordia.ca/id/eprint/990958/1/Cheligeer_PhD_F2022.pdf)).

The chronology also calls for care: a **1991** paper proposed recursive logic as a theoretical account of design, while a **2004** paper formulated design problems in environment-based terms; later publications describe EBD explicitly as the three-activity methodology ([Zeng and Cheng, “On the Logic of Design”](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/0142694X9190022O); [Zeng, “Environment-Based Formulation of Design Problem”](https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=2e65d497a65bdf667af0cff5b0e4a2084cc8a80b&repid=rep1&type=pdf); [Zeng, “Environment-Based Design (EBD): a Methodology for Transdisciplinary Design”](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3233/jid-2015-0004)). A **2019** overview presents EBD as a procedure for innovative and creative design ([Zeng and Zhang, “Environment-Based Design (EBD): A Methodology for Innovative and Creative Design”](https://xuebao.sjtu.edu.cn/EN/abstract/abstract41810.shtml)). These accounts support treating recursive logic as a precursor, not claiming that the full three-activity method was already published in 1991.

## Conclusion

EBD is best understood as a context-first, iterative way to frame and solve design problems: model the relevant environment, derive requirements from it, identify conflicts, and revise solutions as they change that environment. Its flexibility is useful when requirements are incomplete or a project spans interacting people, technologies, and processes.

The method is a framework rather than a fixed checklist: projects may use only some activities, and the sources do not establish a universal stopping rule or comparative advantage. Spelling out **Environment-Based Design** also prevents confusion with the distinct, research-grounded **Evidence-Based Design**.
