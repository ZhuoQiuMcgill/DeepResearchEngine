# Structured literacy reorders early reading instruction

Typical literacy practice is no longer clearly the majority approach. A 2019–2020 survey found about 68% of K–2 teachers reported using balanced literacy ([Shanker Institute, citing EdWeek](https://www.shankerinstitute.org/blog/what-teachers-say-about-literacy)), but a fall 2025 survey of more than 1,200 K–3 teachers found that about half describe a structured approach and nearly one in three a balanced one ([The 74](https://www.the74million.org/article/report-nearly-one-third-of-teachers-still-use-discredited-reading-methods/)). The evidence therefore shows a shifting, mixed picture rather than one dominant practice. The answer to the question still matters: structured literacy is explicit, sequenced teaching of the sound and spelling system, while typical practice centres on meaning-based routines in which phonics is often a minor part. Structured literacy has the stronger evidence on decoding, but the evidence on comprehension and long-term outcomes is thin and disputed, and the two approaches have never been compared in a large, long-term randomized trial.

## What structured literacy is and how it is taught

The main terms need defining first. **Structured literacy** is defined by the International Dyslexia Association (IDA) in its July 2024 working definition as "a comprehensive, integrated approach to reading and writing instruction" that builds on oral language and explicitly teaches the structure of written language ([IDA fact sheet](https://dyslexiaida.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/8_SL-Fact-Sheet.pdf)). IDA says it is an approach rather than a single programme, so schools often combine several programmes to deliver it (same source). **Balanced literacy** has no agreed definition; a 2020 Education Week report notes there is no single definition and recommends classroom observation to see what actually happens ([Education Week 2020 report](https://epe.brightspotcdn.com/1b/80/706eba6246599174b0199ac1f3b5/ed-week-reading-instruction-survey-report-final-1.24.20.pdf)). Because the term is loose, surveys of "balanced" teachers measure the label, not the content taught.

The IDA's language domains are phonology, morphology, semantics, syntax and pragmatics, represented in writing as orthography, and the IDA says word reading, comprehension, spelling and writing should be taught together rather than in isolated blocks ([IDA fact sheet](https://dyslexiaida.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/8_SL-Fact-Sheet.pdf)). Secondary sites often list a six-part version that adds syllables and sound-symbol association, but that list does not appear in the IDA's 2024 definition text, so it should be cited as a paraphrase. Kentucky's guidance adds that lessons should build from simple to complex concepts ([Kentucky Department of Education](https://www.education.ky.gov/curriculum/EarlyLiteracy/Pages/structured_literacy.aspx)).

In practice, the sequence is set by each programme's scope and sequence, and IDA says the order should be adapted to the learner rather than fixed ([IDA fact sheet](https://dyslexiaida.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/8_SL-Fact-Sheet.pdf)). Decodable texts are central to delivery. Scholastic defines controlled vocabulary as words limited to regular letter-sound patterns the student has learned ([Scholastic](https://education.scholastic.com/education/solutions/decodables/what-are-decodable-texts.html)), and Wilson Language describes bridge texts with about 80–85% decodability ([Wilson Language Training](https://www.wilsonlanguage.com/news/three-text-types-to-support-reading-development/)). Structured literacy also relies on screening, diagnostic assessment and progress monitoring. Arizona's screener guide says progress-monitoring data should inform decisions between screening periods ([Arizona Department of Education](https://www.azed.gov/sites/default/files/2022/03/Universal%20Literacy%20and%20Dyslexia%20Screener%20Guide.pdf)), and PLD describes screening as identifying who needs support, diagnostic assessment as specifying what, and progress monitoring as tracking whether the response works ([PLD](https://pld-literacy.org/glossary/universal-screening-or-literacy-screening-check/)).

Outside the United States the same practice has other names. England's policy term is "systematic synthetic phonics" ([The Conversation via Phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2025-12-england-synthetic-phonics-approach-children.html)). Victoria mandated systematic synthetic phonics for Prep to Grade 2 from Term 1 2025, with a minimum of 25 minutes of daily explicit phonics and phonemic awareness teaching ([University of Melbourne library guide](https://unimelb.libguides.com/english-and-literacy/schooltextbooks/y5-6-reading-instruction)). A 2025 Grattan Institute report recommends structured literacy throughout schooling, with a focus on phonics in the early years ([Grattan Institute](https://grattan.edu.au/australia-needs-a-reading-revolution/)).

## What typical literacy practices are and how common they are

Balanced literacy is the most common label in US early-reading classrooms. Its routines are read-alouds, shared, guided and independent reading, and word study ([Reading in Action](https://www.readinginaction.org/science-of-reading-vs-balanced-literacy-what-changed-and-why-it-matters/)). Guided reading is the main small-group routine: a teacher works with students at similar levels, who read leveled texts at their instructional level while the teacher coaches ([Reading in Action](https://www.readinginaction.org/science-of-reading-vs-balanced-literacy-what-changed-and-why-it-matters/)). Balanced literacy grew from a 1990s compromise between whole language and phonics ([Wikipedia, History of learning to read](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_learning_to_read)), and whole language relies on literature immersion and context clues with little explicit phonics ([Mr. Greg](https://www.mrgregenglish.com/the-whole-language-approach-to-reading/)).

Three-cueing is the word-solving method most associated with these practices. Children are taught to use meaning ("does it make sense?"), structure ("does it sound right?") and visual information ("does it look right?") ([Mississippi Department of Education slide deck](https://www.mdek12.org/sites/default/files/Offices/MDE/OAE/OEER/Literacy/Literacy%20Live!%20Leaders/balanced_literacy_v_structured_literacy_slide_deck.pdf)). Ontario's inquiry found the approach rested on Ken Goodman's theory of how fluent adults read, and that the ministry could not show a sufficient scientific basis for it ([OHRC Right to Read report](https://www.ohrc.on.ca/sites/default/files/FINAL%20R2R%20REPORT%20DESIGNED%20April%2012.pdf)).

The strength of these practices is hard to pin down, because the data are old and inconsistent. In a 2019 Education Week survey, **72%** of teachers in 674 early-elementary and special education classrooms said their schools used balanced literacy ([Science News](https://www.sciencenews.org/article/balanced-literacy-phonics-teaching-reading-evidence)). Another 2019 finding put the figure at **68%** of teachers calling balanced literacy their philosophy ([Education Week, Data](https://www.edweek.org/teaching-learning/data-how-reading-is-really-being-taught/2019/12)). These figures differ in wording and sample, and no newer national US survey was found. In the 2019 survey, **75%** of teachers working with early readers reported teaching three-cueing (same source). Use of three-cueing is now contested in law. One source counts at least 10 other states that have banned it ([Ocpathink](https://ocpathink.org/post/independent-journalism/ban-on-three-cueing-in-reading-nears-final-passage)), while another says at least 11 states had explicitly banned it by the end of 2024 ([KOMU](https://www.komu.com/news/state/missouri-lawmakers-ban-controversial-reading-instruction-model-as-primary-method/article_a97b5a7e-4ebd-4c74-b0a2-865accd2e576.html)). The difference likely reflects how states are counted; the report uses the range.

Where balanced literacy includes phonics, the sources disagree. **Wikipedia's balanced literacy entry says direct phonics instruction is included**, and a Mississippi state deck lists phonics taught in mini-lessons or word study ([Mississippi Department of Education slide deck](https://www.mdek12.org/sites/default/files/Offices/MDE/OAE/OEER/Literacy/Literacy%20Live!%20Leaders/balanced_literacy_v_structured_literacy_slide_deck.pdf)). **Reading Universe says phonics is usually not a focus** of balanced literacy ([Reading Universe](https://readinguniverse.org/article/explore-teaching-topics/big-picture/the-courage-to-teach-reading-differently)), and a literacy blog describes systematic phonics as a supplement ([lisforliteracy](https://lisforliteracy.com/what-is-balanced-literacy/)). The report follows the view that phonics is present in most balanced programmes but in small, embedded doses, because the Mississippi state material is the most official source and the other views describe typical classrooms. The evidence does not settle how much phonics a typical balanced classroom teaches, and the 2019 survey shows only about half of teachers defining balanced literacy named phonics ([Shanker Institute](https://www.shankerinstitute.org/blog/what-teachers-say-about-literacy)).

## How the approaches differ and what the evidence shows

The two approaches differ most in order and explicitness. The table summarises the contrast as the sources describe it.

| Feature | Structured literacy | Typical balanced practice |
|---|---|---|
| Phonics | Explicit, planned sequence of sound-symbol correspondences ([IDA fact sheet](https://dyslexiaida.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/8_SL-Fact-Sheet.pdf)) | Usually embedded or in mini-lessons ([Mississippi Department of Education slide deck](https://www.mdek12.org/sites/default/files/Offices/MDE/OAE/OEER/Literacy/Literacy%20Live!%20Leaders/balanced_literacy_v_structured_literacy_slide_deck.pdf)) |
| Texts | Decodable texts matched to taught patterns ([Scholastic](https://education.scholastic.com/education/solutions/decodables/what-are-decodable-texts.html)) | Leveled and predictable texts ([Reading in Action](https://www.readinginaction.org/science-of-reading-vs-balanced-literacy-what-changed-and-why-it-matters/)) |
| Word-solving | Decode letter by letter | Context, structure and pictures ([Wikipedia, Three cueing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three_cueing)) |
| Assessment | Screening, diagnostic and progress monitoring cycle ([Wyoming Literacy Framework](https://edu.wyoming.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/Component-6-Effective-Evidence-Based-Framework-for-Literacy-Instruction.pdf)) | Running records and observation ([Mississippi Department of Education slide deck](https://www.mdek12.org/sites/default/files/Offices/MDE/OAE/OEER/Literacy/Literacy%20Live!%20Leaders/balanced_literacy_v_structured_literacy_slide_deck.pdf)) |

The strongest comparison is a 2025 exploratory analysis (Hansford et al.) of **40 structured literacy studies and 28 balanced literacy studies**. Its average effect was **0.43 unweighted and 0.46 weighted for structured literacy**, against **0.19 and 0.29 for balanced literacy** ([Springer, 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s44217-025-00922-8)). The authors call it "exploratory" rather than a meta-analysis, say programme selection was not systematic, and say the findings are hypothesis-generating. Decoding showed the largest structured advantage (d = 0.44), while reading differences were small (0.13–0.15) and not significant, and comprehension favoured balanced literacy numerically (same source). The same paper says funnel-plot tests suggested small-study inflation for structured literacy, and its abstract gives a long-term difference of .13 where the body reports .26 (same source). The paper's numbers therefore need caution.

**Reading Recovery's gains are disputed.** An i3 scale-up evaluation found an effect of **1.17 at mid-first grade, 0.51 by the end of first grade and 0.16 on the third-grade state test** ([ResearchGate, i3 evaluation](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/303970638_Reading_Recovery_An_Evaluation_of_the_Four-Year_i3_Scale-Up)). May et al. report large first-grade short-term effects, similar to the RCT ([ERIC, Long-term impacts](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED628331.pdf)). The Literacy Council of North America says a 2022 regression-discontinuity study found no sustained gains through grades 3–4, and it disputes that method while claiming large positive short-term outcomes ([LCNA](https://myliteracycouncil.org/revisiting-the-recently-reported-longitudinal-study-of-reading-recovery/)). The 2025 Springer analysis reads the same RD results as fading gains ([Springer, 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s44217-025-00922-8)). The report follows the fading-gains reading, because the third-grade figure is far below the first-grade figures and the 2022 RD results are not disputed by the sources that report them, but the RD method itself is contested, so the evidence does not decide the long-term question.

**The National Reading Panel and its critics also disagree.** The NRP concluded that systematic phonics produced the greatest improvements and recommended it for routine classroom use, and that it was more effective for children from low-socioeconomic backgrounds ([NICHD](https://www.nichd.nih.gov/newsroom/releases/nrp); [Reading Rockets](https://www.readingrockets.org/topics/curriculum-and-instruction/articles/findings-national-reading-panel)). Stuebing et al. defend the systematic phonics effects against re-analyses of the NRP and call them practically significant ([Stuebing et al., 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3024599/)). Bowers (2020), cited in the 2025 Springer paper, argues that earlier meta-analyses did not show systematic phonics improves long-term comprehension and that the effect over whole-language instruction is small ([Springer, 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s44217-025-00922-8)). The report follows the view that systematic phonics reliably beats unsystematic or no phonics for decoding and is modestly better than whole-language instruction, while the comprehension and long-term claims are not shown.

Learner groups are where the evidence is thinnest. The 2025 analysis found five systematic phonics studies with dyslexic samples and no balanced literacy studies at all ([Springer, 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s44217-025-00922-8)). For struggling and intervention samples, structured literacy effects were larger in grades 1–3 (d = 0.52) than in kindergarten (d = 0.36) (same source). For English learners, a 2024 observational study found those with dyslexia gained as much or more than English-proficient peers under a structured decoding approach, but it was not a comparison with balanced literacy ([Frontiers in Education](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2024.1495043/full)). Critics also warn that structured approaches can stifle fluency and prosody ([University of Iowa Reading Research Center](https://irrc.education.uiowa.edu/blog/2019/04/explanation-structured-literacy-and-comparison-balanced-literacy)), and a 2025 academic critique argues England's decodable books often have very limited content ([The Conversation via Phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2025-12-england-synthetic-phonics-approach-children.html)).

## What changing approach involves for schools and families

Public cost figures are mostly per-teacher or per-state rather than per-student. California's 2025 budget set aside **$200 million** for a recommended list of teacher professional development, and districts using a listed programme receive funding, though training is not mandatory. Formula funding gives about **$1,686 per TK-5 teacher**, and listed programmes range from **$199 to $1,400** ([EdSource](https://edsource.org/2026/teachers-training-science-of-reading/765498)). Louisiana's fiscal note estimates **$25.4 million a year** for 400 literacy coaches at $60,000 each, plus specialists and coach development ([Louisiana Legislature](https://legis.la.gov/legis/ViewDocument.aspx?d=1287478)). Michigan's budget includes **$50 million** for science-of-reading training ([CSG Midwest](https://www.csg.org/2026/10/30/mlc-chairs-initiative-on-literacy-new-laws-on-teacher-training-staffing-requirements-central-to-states-push-to-improve-literacy/)). Individual teacher training is cheaper: one IDA-approved provider charges **$1,280** for a 45-hour classroom-teacher course ([Glean Education](https://gleaneducation.com/services/)). IDA accreditation sets minimum hours of **45 clock hours** for classroom teachers, **90** for interventionists and **135** for specialists, according to a vendor comparison citing the IDA handbook ([Illuminate Ed Therapy](https://www.illuminateedtherapy.com/resources/structured-literacy-training-programs-compared)). These prices come from vendors and are not independently verified.

Time is the other large change. Tasmania requires at least **one hour of structured literacy a day** in government primary schools, according to a government statement and a 2025 Education Matters article ([Education Matters](https://educationmattersmag.com.au/tasmanian-primary-schools-set-to-lift-literacy-rates/)). A reading-practice blog describes Mississippi's K–3 guidance as a 120-minute core block, presented as a state example rather than a universal mandate ([Reading Simplified](https://readingsimplified.com/feeds/blog/reading-instruction-minutes-per-day)). One interventionist certification requires lessons four to five days a week for 45 minutes, which its own page says one district with 90 minutes a week does not meet ([ESC 4](https://esc4.net/certification/dyslexia-interventionist-certification/)). No independent study links instructional minutes to pupil outcomes.

Policy requirements vary by place. Pennsylvania requires schools to adopt a structured literacy curriculum in kindergarten through grade 3 by the 2027–28 school year ([Mon Valley Independent](https://www.monvalleyindependent.com/2026/06/01/all-schools-are-required-to-adopt-structured-literacy-heres-what-some-teachers-already-using-it-say/)). Washington's law requires districts buying K–4 early literacy curricula from September 2027 to follow structured literacy ([Chronicle](https://www.chronline.com/stories/new-law-focuses-on-literacy-in-the-early-grades,398939)). California recommends training without requiring it ([EdSource](https://edsource.org/2026/teachers-training-science-of-reading/765498)). Ontario removed cueing strategies from its curriculum, but its teacher licensure standards do not identify structured literacy components ([Dyslexia Canada](https://dyslexiacanada.org/national-snapshot/ontario/)). Mandatory phonics screening checks operate in South Australia, New South Wales, Tasmania, Victoria and Queensland ([Springer narrative review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s44020-026-00123-2)). England's Year 1 phonics check is statutory ([DfE administration guidance](https://dera.ioe.ac.uk/id/eprint/42044/1/2026%20phonics%20screening%20check%20administration%20guidance%20-%20GOV.UK.pdf)), and the 2026 expected standard is 31 out of 40, the first change since 2012 ([DfE statistics](https://explore-education-statistics.service.gov.uk/find-statistics/phonics-screening-check-attainment/2025-26)). A blog reports 82% of Year 1 pupils met the standard in 2026, citing provisional figures ([Edu Archives](https://eduarchivestutor.com/blog/phonics-screening-check-results-2026-pass-mark-31/)), but that figure was not confirmed in the official statistics retrieved.

The risks are consistent across jurisdictions. Teachers find the changes large and do not always understand the reasons ([Education Week, 2022](https://www.edweek.org/teaching-learning/why-putting-the-science-of-reading-into-practice-is-so-challenging/2022/07)). A 2025–26 Fordham report finds gaps in training, materials and implementation, especially in high-poverty schools, and it found many instructional materials used by elementary teachers did not meet widely accepted scientific standards ([Fordham Institute](https://fordhaminstitute.org/national/research/teachers-desk-science-reading-progress-report)). Implementation supports mainly focus on early elementary students, and middle-school teachers may not be trained in decoding instruction ([K-12 Dive](https://www.k12dive.com/news/what-you-need-to-know-about-the-science-of-reading/830845/)). Pupil progress during transition has not been independently evaluated. Tasmania's government says its strategy is working, without an attached evaluation, and an ABC report from 2023 says international tests show Australian literacy has not improved in recent years ([ABC News](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2023-09-10/literacy-teaching-revolution-underway/102834334)). England's disadvantaged pupils meeting the standard rose from **67% to 68%**, and other pupils from **84% to 86%** ([DfE statistics](https://explore-education-statistics.service.gov.uk/find-statistics/phonics-screening-check-attainment/2025-26)), but those figures are not linked to any transition.

## Conclusion

The question assumed that typical practice is what most classrooms do. The evidence no longer supports that cleanly: teacher surveys show balanced literacy was dominant in 2019–2020, while the 2025 survey points to a mixed picture in which structured approaches are now at least as common. The practices differ most in how explicitly phonics is taught, in the sequence of teaching, and in the texts children read. The evidence supports structured literacy for decoding, with a moderate and consistent advantage, but it does not yet show a comprehension or long-term advantage, and the comparison data are exploratory and partly inconsistent.

For schools, the practical question is less whether to move than how. Costs are concentrated in training, coaching and materials rather than licences, and the time change depends on whether a school can re-sequence an existing block or must add intervention time. Policy is moving quickly, but enforcement and funding vary, and the risks reported most often are teacher readiness, aligned materials and older students. Families need to check what their child is being taught, since the same label can describe very different lessons. Future decisions should be guided by independent outcome studies that follow pupils into later grades, which the current evidence does not yet supply.
