# Tight Funding Squeezed NHS Services and Staff

Public funding for the NHS in England has kept rising in cash terms, but it has grown more slowly than the service's long-run average and much more slowly than demand. The King's Fund reports that NHS spending grew by an average of **3.6% a year in real terms since 1955/56**, but **2.8% a year between 2015/16 and 2022/23** ([King's Fund](https://www.kingsfund.org.uk/insight-and-analysis/data-and-charts/nhs-budget-nutshell)). The Health Foundation puts growth since 2010 at **1.4% a year**, a different period and measure, so the two figures are not directly comparable ([Health Foundation](https://www.health.org.uk/reports-and-analysis/briefings/health-and-social-care-funding)). Over the same years, the waiting list reached a record **7.7 million in September 2023** ([House of Commons Library](https://commonslibrary.parliament.uk/research-briefings/cbp-7281/)) and stood at **7.38 million at the end of September 2026** ([Going Private UK](https://goingprivateuk.co.uk/nhs-waiting-list-september-2026/)). Official sources document the timing of funding changes and the service pressures that followed, but they do not establish that funding caused the decline, since demand, the pandemic and productivity are also cited. The 2025 Spending Review set day-to-day NHS growth at about 3% a year in real terms, and the Autumn Budget 2026 added only **£190 million** for winter, leaving larger decisions to the 2027 spending review ([Carnall Farrar](https://www.carnallfarrar.com/insights-uk-budget-2026-nhs/)).

## Funding grew slower than need, with pandemic spikes

Three terms need defining. "Public funding" here means the money voted to the Department of Health and Social Care for NHS England, mainly day-to-day (resource) and capital budgets; the NHS Confederation notes that the scope of NHS England's budget has expanded, which makes year-on-year comparisons hard ([NHS Confederation](https://www.nhsconfed.org/publications/spending-review-2025)). "Real terms" means figures adjusted for inflation, and sources use different deflators, which is one reason their growth rates differ ([Institute for Government](https://www.instituteforgovernment.org.uk/sites/default/files/2023-01/NHS-crisis-does-government-have-a-plan.pdf)). "Waiting list" means patients waiting for planned hospital treatment, counted as pathways; the Public Accounts Committee reported **7.4 million pathways** in its waiting-times inquiry ([PAC, HC 820](https://committees.parliament.uk/publications/50242/documents/271529/default/)).

The main settlements form a sequence. The 2010 Spending Review gave a **0.4% real-terms increase over four years**, with the budget rising from **£104bn to £114bn** ([HM Treasury](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/203826/Spending_review_2010.pdf); [Haringey council briefing](https://www.minutes.haringey.gov.uk/documents/s18457/CSR%20-%20whole.pdf)). The Long Term Plan settlement added **£33.9bn in cash terms by 2023/24**, an average of **3.4% a year in real terms** ([House of Commons Public Accounts Committee](https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/cm5801/cmselect/cmpubacc/344/34404.htm)), though the Commons Library puts the real rise nearer **3.3%** once higher inflation estimates are included ([House of Commons Library](https://commonslibrary.parliament.uk/research-briefings/cbp-8798/)).

| 2020–2022 | Pandemic emergency funding | £63bn for health services in 2020–21, per government ([GOV.UK](https://gov.uk/government/news/additional-54-billion-for-nhs-covid-19-response-over-next-six-months)); BMA estimate £47bn ([IfG](https://www.instituteforgovernment.org.uk/sites/default/files/2023-01/NHS-crisis-does-government-have-a-plan.pdf)) |

| 2026 | Autumn Budget | £190m for winter 2026/27 ([Carnall Farrar](https://www.carnallfarrar.com/insights-uk-budget-2026-nhs/)) |

The pandemic money distorts the headline series. Government and BMA totals for 2020/21 differ by about £16bn, and the sources do not reconcile the definitions ([GOV.UK](https://gov.uk/government/news/additional-54-billion-for-nhs-covid-19-response-over-next-six-months); [IfG](https://www.instituteforgovernment.org.uk/sites/default/files/2023-01/NHS-crisis-does-government-have-a-plan.pdf)). The IFS excludes the withdrawn pandemic years from its "flat for two years" comparison, and the King's Fund notes that the 2022/23 peak reflected extra pandemic investment that later ended ([IFS](https://ifs.org.uk/news/nhs-spending-has-risen-less-quickly-was-planned-last-election-despite-pandemic-and-record); [King's Fund](https://www.kingsfund.org.uk/insight-and-analysis/data-and-charts/nhs-budget-nutshell)). Per head, the Health Foundation projects real growth of only **1.2% a year** from 2013/14 to 2028/29 after adjusting for population and ageing ([Health Foundation](https://www.health.org.uk/reports-and-analysis/analysis/health-care-funding)).

Spending also varies by nation. In 2018/19, spending per head was **£2,269 in England, £2,396 in Scotland, £2,402 in Wales and £2,436 in Northern Ireland** ([Institute for Government](https://www.instituteforgovernment.org.uk/explainer/devolution-and-nhs)). The Barnett formula does not consider need ([Institute for Government](https://www.instituteforgovernment.org.uk/sites/default/files/publications/devolved-public-services.pdf)). For 2026/27 the IFS reports a **0.2% real rise** in Scotland's health and social care budget and a **2.2% real fall** in Wales's, against England's planned **2.2% real rise** ([IFS](https://ifs.org.uk/news/scottish-budget-and-spending-review-signal-big-shifts-health-and-investment-spending-reliance); [IFS](https://ifs.org.uk/publications/public-service-spending-and-performance-wales); [IFS](https://ifs.org.uk/articles/next-weeks-scottish-fiscal-update-will-show-more-money-year-cuts-looming-next-year)). These are single-year comparisons.

## Waiting lists and emergency care worsened as capacity shrank

The waiting list is the clearest service measure, but its 2010 baseline is weak. The only figure found, **2.6 million waiting in May 2010**, comes from a Labour Party document, and no official NHS England figure for that date was located ([Labour](https://labour.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2018/07/NHS_Tory_Failure.pdf)). Before 2010 the list fell from 4.2 million to 2.3 million between 2007 and 2009, then grew from 2012 ([Nuffield Trust](https://www.nuffieldtrust.org.uk/news-item/the-growing-problem-of-treatment-waiting-times)). At end-September 2026, **116,353 people had waited more than 52 weeks**, up from 113,339 in August, and the target of under 1% over 52 weeks by March 2026 was missed ([The British Eye](https://thebritisheye.com/2026/11/12/nhs-waiting-list-england-highest-year/)). In May 2026 only **65.6% of waits were within 18 weeks**, against a 92% standard ([Health & Protection](https://healthcareandprotection.com/nhs-waiting-list-climbs-back-to-7-3-million/)). One aggregator calls the list's peak a "2024 peak", which conflicts with the Commons Library's September 2023 peak; the Library's date is preferred here ([Britclock](https://britclock.co.uk/nhs/waiting-list/)).

Emergency and cancer performance show similar strain. Four-hour A&E performance bottomed at **65.2% in December 2022** and was **75.4% in July 2026**, against a 95% standard ([UK Government Tracker](https://ukgovernmenttracker.com/nhs)). The Library's different "over four hours" measure peaked at **50.4% in December 2022** ([House of Commons Library](https://researchbriefings.files.parliament.uk/documents/CBP-7281/CBP-7281.pdf)). Demand reached **2.49 million A&E attendances in July 2026**, the busiest month on record ([NHS Alliance](https://thenhsalliance.org/resources/nhs-activity-tracker-august-2026)). Cancer 62-day performance was **71.2% in July 2026** against an 85% standard on the rebuilt measure, and the 2025/26 annual report gives **72.8% against 75%** ([House of Commons Library](https://researchbriefings.files.parliament.uk/documents/CBP-7281/CBP-7281.pdf); [NHS England](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6a992bc45a0c25165ae46825/nhs-england-annual-report-and-accounts-2025-2026.pdf)). The cancer standard changed in October 2023, so older figures are not comparable ([UK Government Tracker](https://ukgovernmenttracker.com/nhs)).

Capacity fell while occupancy stayed high. General and acute overnight beds fell **8%** between Q1 2010/11 and Q4 2019/20, from 110,568 to 102,194 ([Nuffield Trust](https://www.nuffieldtrust.org.uk/resource/hospital-bed-occupancy)). Total bed stock fell **8.3%** over the same period, and average occupancy has exceeded **85% since 2010** ([BMA](https://www.bma.org.uk/advice-and-support/nhs-delivery-and-workforce/pressures/nhs-hospital-beds-data-analysis)). Mental health beds fell **24%**, from 23,515 to 17,863 ([Nuffield Trust](https://www.nuffieldtrust.org.uk/resource/hospital-bed-occupancy)). Up to one in three beds was occupied by patients fit to leave but without social care ([BMA](https://www.bma.org.uk/advice-and-support/nhs-delivery-and-workforce/pressures/nhs-hospital-beds-data-analysis)).

Social care and public health cuts are the most cited funding link. Funding for adults aged 65 and over **fell 21% between 2009/10 and 2015/16**, and a Health Foundation-funded working paper found that these cuts led directly to more A&E use ([Health Foundation](https://www.health.org.uk/blogs/what-do-we-know-about-the-effects-of-cutting-public-funding-for-social-care)). Real NHS mental health funding fell **8% between 2010 and 2015**, with 2,100 beds lost ([PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6373963/)). The public health grant has been cut since 2015/16 and is **over 25% lower per person in real terms** ([The Lowdown](https://lowdownnhs.info/funding/13507/)).

The mortality evidence is the most contested. A UCL study in BMJ Open linked spending cuts to "nearly 120,000" excess deaths and recorded **45,368 excess deaths between 2010 and 2014**; the sources do not reconcile these two figures, and the authors say no firm conclusions on cause can be drawn ([UCL News](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2017/nov/austerity-linked-120000-extra-deaths-england)). A York study linked cuts to health, public health and social care with **57,550 extra deaths** between 2010 and 2014, of which healthcare spending accounted for **33,888**; the government declined to apologise ([Disability News Service](https://www.disabilitynewsservice.com/austerity-cuts-to-social-care-and-health-caused-57000-deaths-research-suggests/)). A 2024 BMJ Public Health study found no direct link between local austerity and outsourcing, which is an explicit contrary view ([BMJ Public Health via DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/89cb452322a04cab8c0b69e9ebd176d2)). These studies rely on modelled counterfactuals, so they are consistent with a funding effect but do not prove one. Age UK reported about **1.93 million bed days lost since June 2010** to patients waiting for social care, an older advocacy figure ([Age UK](https://www.ageuk.org.uk/latest-press/archive/nearly-2-million-nhs-days-lost-as-people-remain-in-hospital/)).

## Staff pay, vacancies and deficits moved in different directions

Workforce numbers improved while pay and finances did not. Vacancies were **100,326 (6.7%) in June 2026**, down from 105,602 (7.1%) a year earlier ([NHS Vacancy Statistics](https://digital.nhs.uk/data-and-information/publications/statistical/nhs-vacancies-survey/april-2015---june-2026-experimental-statistics)). Doctor numbers rose **23%** and nurse numbers **20%** in the five years to July 2026, though the sources give no 2010 staff series ([House of Commons Library](https://commonslibrary.parliament.uk/research-briefings/cbp-7281/)). Government evidence to the pay review body attributes the falling vacancy rate partly to trusts adjusting workforce plans to their finances, so part of the fall may reflect fewer funded posts ([DHSC and NHS England](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6ad21fdaa89985bb970267dc/nhs-pay-review-body-written-evidence-2027-to-2028.pdf)). About **130,200 FTE staff (10%) left** in the year to November 2025 ([House of Commons Library](https://researchbriefings.files.parliament.uk/documents/CBP-10539/CBP-10539.pdf)).

Real pay fell for most of the period. The top of band 5 fell about **9% in real terms between 2010/11 and 2019/20** ([Full Fact](https://fullfact.org/online/september-2020-nhs-pay/)). A union-commissioned London Economics report put starting-nurse pay **up 17% in cash terms but down about 10% in real terms** since 2010/11 ([Channel 4 FactCheck](https://www.channel4.com/news/factcheck/factcheck-have-nurses-already-had-a-pay-rise)). The Health Foundation finds average NHS earnings still **8–11% lower in real terms than in 2010** for doctors, allied health professionals and nurses, even after a 2024 recovery ([Health Foundation](https://www.health.org.uk/reports-and-analysis/analysis/how-much-should-nhs-staff-be-paid)). The 2026/27 award of **3.3%** was accepted on 12 February 2026 ([GMB](https://www.gmb.org.uk/public-services/nhs-and-ambulance/nhs/)). The government's claim that the award is above forecast inflation of 2.2% rests only on a pay-information website, while unions cite CPI of 3.4% at announcement ([NHS Pay Rise 2026/27 guide](https://nhstakehomepaycalculator.co.uk/guides/nhs-pay-rise-2026/); [NHS pay bands guide](https://nhstakehomepaycalculator.co.uk/guides/nhs-pay-bands-2026/)). The RCN and 12 other unions said the award did not restore lost pay ([Nursing in Practice](https://www.nursinginpractice.com/latest-news/unions-demand-nurse-pay-talks-after-inadequate-nhs-rise/)).

Finances improved in 2025/26, but through management action. Draft 2025/26 plans totalled a **£4.4bn deficit**, or £6.6bn before deficit support funding ([NHS England](https://www.england.nhs.uk/long-read/month-12-financial-position-2025-26-4-june-2026/)). The outturn recorded a **£563m deficit**, £400m lower than in 2024/25 on a like-for-like basis, and agency spend fell **from £2.1bn to £1.2bn** ([NHS England](https://www.england.nhs.uk/long-read/month-12-financial-position-2025-26-4-june-2026/)). NHS England credits board ownership and tighter control. Critics argue the deficit reflects underfunding, and the headline deficit can differ by roughly half depending on whether deficit support is counted. Earlier, the PAC found systems overspent by **£621m in 2022–23** and ended 2023–24 with an aggregated **£1.4bn deficit**, despite **£4.5bn of extra funding** ([PAC](https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/cm5901/cmselect/cmpubacc/350/report.html)). The PAC adds that technology investment stalled because funds were redirected to cover ICB deficits ([PAC](https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/cm5901/cmselect/cmpubacc/350/report.html)). Wales ran a **£199m** annual deficit in 2025-26 ([Audit Wales](https://www.audit.wales/news/nhs-struggling-control-day-day-costs-despite-continued-funding-increases)). No audited English trust or ICB accounts were located, so the deficit history cannot be checked against audited figures.

## Experts dispute whether money or productivity is the constraint

Funding-first experts stress capital and inflation. The King's Fund puts the NHS maintenance backlog at **£17.3bn** and says capital budgets continue to be raided ([King's Fund](https://www.kingsfund.org.uk/insight-and-analysis/press-releases/tkf-eric-data-2026)). Nuffield Trust argues the 2025 settlement is "more than wiped out" by inflation and planned treatment growth ([Nuffield Trust](https://www.nuffieldtrust.org.uk/news-item/nuffield-trust-response-to-the-spending-review-2025)), and says inflation will take at least **£1bn** from next year's NHS budget in real terms ([Nuffield Trust](https://www.nuffieldtrust.org.uk/news-item/nuffield-trust-response-to-the-autumn-budget)). Health capital falls **2.2%, from £13.8bn in 2026/27 to £13.5bn in 2027/28** ([Carnall Farrar](https://www.carnallfarrar.com/insights-uk-budget-2026-nhs/)).

Reform-first accounts stress productivity and demand. The ONS estimates publicly funded healthcare productivity **fell 25.6% in the year ending 2021** ([ONS](https://www.ons.gov.uk/economy/economicoutputandproductivity/publicservicesproductivity/articles/publicservicesproductivityestimateshealthcare/financialyearending2021)). NHS England estimates acute productivity is about **11% below pre-pandemic levels**, or 8% after adjusting for industrial action ([NHS England](https://www.england.nhs.uk/long-read/nhs-productivity/)). The King's Fund says nobody knows why productivity has stayed sluggish ([King's Fund](https://www.kingsfund.org.uk/insight-and-analysis/blogs/nhs-productivity-decline)). Productivity measurement is itself disputed: the Commons Library notes that the Nuffield Trust and Institute for Government found no firm evidence for NHS England's productivity explanation ([House of Commons Library](https://researchbriefings.files.parliament.uk/documents/CBP-10313/CBP-10313.pdf)). The Public Accounts Committee attributes the deficit to demand, estate underinvestment, inflation and workforce issues together, not to funding alone ([PAC](https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/cm5901/cmselect/cmpubacc/350/report.html)).

The government's plan rests on the 10 Year Health Plan. It phases out deficit support funding, worth **£2.2bn in 2025–26**, and aims to move money toward areas of higher need ([NHS England, Fit for the Future](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6888a0b1a11f859994409147/fit-for-the-future-10-year-health-plan-for-england.pdf)). The BMA reads it as containing **no new funding beyond the Spending Review**, assuming **2% a year productivity gains** and surplus budgets by **2029–30** ([BMA](https://www.bma.org.uk/advice-and-support/nhs-delivery-and-workforce/nhs-reforms/reforms-to-the-nhs)). The government describes a **£65bn** ten-year capital settlement ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/10-year-capital-plan-for-health-and-social-care/10-year-capital-plan-for-health-and-social-care)). Independent forecasts are older than the 2025 settlement. The IFS estimates pressures of about **3–4% a year**, against a 2025 plan of about 3% ([IFS](https://ifs.org.uk/sites/default/files/output_url_files/R143-Chapter3-new.pdf)). Nuffield Trust's estimate that the US medicines deal will mean **about 13,500 fewer NHS staff across the UK in 2028** is a single think-tank estimate resting on stated assumptions ([Nuffield Trust](https://www.nuffieldtrust.org.uk/news-item/nuffield-trust-estimates-impact-of-us-trade-deal-on-nhs-workforce)). No independent forecast of English waiting times to 2030 was found.

## Conclusion

The evidence supports the claim that funding changed and that the changes had consequences. Growth fell well below the long-run pace in the 2010s, pandemic money temporarily lifted spending, and the 2025 settlement restored only about 3% a year in real terms. The clearest effects are on finances, capital and pay, where the sources agree on direction. Service effects are documented as timing and scale, including the record waiting list, cancer and A&E shortfalls, and a shrinking bed base under high occupancy. Attributing those effects to funding is the contested part. Mortality studies are observational, the productivity case is disputed, and no official source weighs funding against demand and productivity in one decomposition.

The practical implication is that the next decisions matter more than the history. The 2027 spending review, the productivity review led by Mark Bailie, and the capital position will decide whether the 2025 settlement holds ([HM Treasury, Budget 2026](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6ae20b9039d29f3f9b9b3f55/E03668986_HMT_Budget_2026_TEXT_PRINT__1_.pdf)). Readers should expect continued disagreement over the causes of NHS pressures, and should judge claims about funding against the specific measure, period and baseline each source uses.
