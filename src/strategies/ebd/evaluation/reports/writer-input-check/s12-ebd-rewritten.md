# Income Shocks, Illness and Debt Drive Filings

Surveys of US filers most often name **loss of income, medical problems and family breakdown** as the reasons for filing, and these causes frequently overlap. In the Consumer Bankruptcy Project, **78% of filers cited a decline in income and 65% cited medical issues** ([debt.org](https://www.debt.org/bankruptcy/statistics/)), though this comes from a secondary summary. The sharpest dispute concerns medical costs: survey-based estimates put medical involvement at roughly 46% to 67% of filings, while a hospitalization-based study finds only **about 4% of personal bankruptcies** are caused by hospital stays ([FDIC, Argyle et al.](https://www.fdic.gov/system/files/2024-09/2020-argyle.pdf)). Filers' debt is mostly secured mortgage debt by value, with credit cards the largest unsecured category, and filing counts have risen since 2022 as borrowing costs and household expenses climbed ([Epiq Global](https://www.epiqglobal.com/en-us/resource-center/news/small-business-filings-increase-50-year-over-year-in-first-half-of-2026)). Whether overspending is the main cause remains unresolved, because the one study claiming it covers a single state in 2003. The strongest causal evidence shows that Chapter 13 protection improves later earnings and reduces foreclosure, so the outcome of a filing depends on whether protection is granted ([NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w20520)).

## Key terms and how they are measured

**Personal bankruptcy filings** are individual cases in US federal courts. The Administrative Office of the US Courts counts all cases, including business ones, and reported **608,511 cases in the 12 months to 30 June 2026** ([usbankruptcyhelp.com, citing AOUSC](https://www.usbankruptcyhelp.com/current-bankruptcy-filing-statistics)). **Medical bankruptcy** is not an official category. It is a survey measure: filers who report medical bills or illness-related income loss as a contributing cause ([Amplitude](https://livingwithamplitude.com/article/many-bankruptcy-filers-cite-illness-and-medical-bills-as-factors/)). **Insolvency** in England and Wales is broader, covering bankruptcies, debt relief orders (DROs) and individual voluntary arrangements (IVAs), which together recorded **10,920 individuals in April 2026** ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/individual-insolvencies-april-2026)). **Overspending** is not defined in the sources; the UC Davis Delaware study treats it as debt high relative to income, such as large mortgage and auto loans ([UC Davis](https://www.ucdavis.edu/news/reckless-spending-not-illness-or-job-loss-causes-most-bankruptcy)).

## Income loss, illness and divorce lead the stated triggers

Survey evidence consistently ranks income loss first. In a survey of families with children in the Consumer Bankruptcy Project, **70% said they filed after losing a job, taking a pay cut or facing an unexpected income drop**, with a sudden family medical condition second and divorce third ([Federal Reserve Bank of Boston](https://www.bostonfed.org/-/media/Documents/cb/PDF/Bankruptcies.pdf)). Warren and Tyagi report that **87% of these families cited one or more of job loss, medical problems or divorce**, and 40.4% gave two reasons ([Caucus 99](https://caucus99percent.com/content/resilience-two-income-trap-reflections-time-and-money); [book text](https://dokumen.pub/the-two-income-trap-why-middle-class-parents-are-going-broke-elizabeth-warren-amelia-warren-tyagi-9780465009916-2003010496.html)). A 2015 student note on a nationwide survey found medical bills had the highest share of agreement at 26%, with loss of job second ([Maine Law Review](https://mainelawreview.org/2015/04/08/medical-debt-as-a-cause-of-consumer-bankruptcy/)). Divorce is smaller: one secondary summary puts it at roughly **1 in 10 filings** ([Auditing & Accounting](https://auditingaccounting.com/bankrupting-the-american-dream-how-medical-bills-job-loss-and-high-costs-drive-u-s-bankruptcies)).

The medical share has risen over time as measured by surveys. A 1981 San Antonio court-required survey found **about 8% of filers cited a medical reason**, but the authors note the data were collected differently from later studies ([Two-Income Trap](https://dokumen.pub/the-two-income-trap-why-middle-class-parents-are-going-broke-elizabeth-warren-amelia-warren-tyagi-9780465009916-2003010496.html)). A 2001 survey of 1,771 filers in five states found **46.2% cited illness or medical bills at least in part** ([NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/amp/wbna6895896)). The table below sets out the main estimates and why they differ.

The medical dispute is a measurement dispute. Himmelstein-group surveys, which ask filers about contributing causes, report medical problems in roughly **62% to 67% of filings**, and a 2019 study found the share was **67.5% after the Affordable Care Act against 65.5% before** ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2019/02/11/this-is-the-real-reason-most-americans-file-for-bankruptcy.html)). Critics respond that the Himmelstein sample includes only people who had already filed, so it cannot show that medical debt caused filing, and a **Department of Justice study of more than 5,000 cases (2000–2002) found 54% of bankruptcies involved no medical debt** ([AEI](https://www.aei.org/research-products/speech/clarifying-the-research-on-medical-bankruptcy/); [RealClearMarkets](https://www.realclearmarkets.com/articles/2009/07/30/the_medical_bankruptcy_myth_97335.html)). A rebuttal that controls for debt levels finds no statistical link between medical debt and bankruptcy ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/7269373_Medical_Bankruptcy_Myth_Versus_Fact)). The report follows the view that medical problems are a common contributing trigger, not the majority cause, because the surveys do not separate primary from secondary reasons and the controlled comparison finds no link; the evidence does not settle the main-cause share.

Newer evidence on direction is more specific. A 2026 *Health Affairs* study linking trauma registry records to credit reports found **bankruptcy filings rose by 3.2 per 1,000 injured patients at fifteen months, a 6% relative increase** ([Health Affairs](https://www.healthaffairs.org/doi/10.1377/hlthaff.2025.00847)). An NBER working paper finds that small and larger medical debts (at or above $500) are not meaningfully predictive of default, and its authors say the correlation does not establish causation ([NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w33644)). The report follows the *Health Affairs* result that injury raises filing risk, while accepting that the effect is modest and that medical debt on a credit file is a weak predictor on its own.

## Unsecured debt and weak incomes set the stage

Filers are heavily indebted and often middle-income. In a sample of 606,120 US filings from four districts (2004–2018), **average total debt was about $240,000**, of which **more than $95,000 was unsecured** and **nearly $110,000 was mortgage debt**, and **77% of filers held credit-card debt** ([FDIC, Argyle et al.](https://www.fdic.gov/system/files/2024-09/2020-argyle.pdf)). Medical debt was held by more than half of filers but made up less than 5% of an average filer's total debt, so it can trigger a filing without being most of the balance sheet ([FDIC, Argyle et al.](https://www.fdic.gov/system/files/2024-09/2020-argyle.pdf)). The sources conflict on the secured share. The Argyle paper reports unsecured debt at 53% of liabilities, while an accreditation-site summary of a "BAPCPA Report" reports 53% secured ([Top Dollar](https://www.accrediteddebtrelief.com/blog/bankruptcy-statistics/)). The report follows the Argyle paper because it is a primary study with a stated sample, and the summary gives no year or sample.

Filers differ from comparable households. Compared with credit users, filers have **significantly higher unsecured debt and collections**, and are **nearly two and a half times as likely to have a delinquent debt** before filing ([Dobbie, Goldsmith-Pinkham and Yang](https://economics.yale.edu/sites/default/files/dobbie_bankruptcy.pdf)). Among homeowners, the median filer has a debt-to-income ratio of 3.61 and negative net worth of $36,076, against 0.98 and $396,500 for the median non-filing homeowner ([Foohey, Lawless and Thorne](https://www.ablj.org/consumer-bankruptcy-household-debt-and-the-big-picture-pamela-foohey-bob-lawless-and-deborah-thorne-debts-grip-vol-99-issue-3-html/)). Filers are also older on average, with a **median age of 49**, and **single women make up about 33% of filers** according to a secondary summary of the Consumer Bankruptcy Project ([debt.org](https://www.debt.org/bankruptcy/statistics/)). A 2007–2009 Survey of Consumer Finances profile differs sharply, finding **50% of filers lacked a high school diploma** ([Consumer Interests Annual](https://www.consumerinterests.org/assets/docs/CIA/CIA2017/cia%202017%20evans%20bauchet%20op.pdf)), so the demographic picture depends on sample and year.

Aggregate trends are mixed. US court counts peaked above two million in 2005 before the 2005 reform, and an NBER study estimates the reform **roughly halved the household filing rate** ([NBER Digest](https://www.nber.org/digest/dec19/bankruptcy-reform-2005-sharply-reduced-filings)). Since 2022 counts have risen, and Epiq reports **293,265 individual filings in the first half of 2026, up 12%**, which it links to higher borrowing costs and a softer job market ([Epiq Global](https://www.epiqglobal.com/en-us/resource-center/news/small-business-filings-increase-50-year-over-year-in-first-half-of-2026); [ABF Journal](https://www.abfjournal.com/epiq-total-bankruptcy-filings-increase-12-in-h1-26/)). Yet the household debt-to-income ratio was **about 78% in Q2 2026, down from about 86% in 2022** ([TD Economics](https://economics.td.com/us-household-debt)), so aggregate debt levels alone do not explain the recent rise. The New York Fed series, which counts consumers with new bankruptcy notations, was about 124,000 in Q1 2026 and flat on the prior quarter ([NC Bankruptcy Expert](https://ncbankruptcyexpert.com/2026/07/22/law-review-economics-federal-reserve-bank-new-york-quarterly-report-household-debt-and)). That measure tracks credit-report entries, not filings, so the apparent conflict with rising court counts is a difference in what each series measures.

Unemployment is also disputed. A 19-year state panel finds **a 10% rise in the unemployment rate is associated with a 14.4% rise in personal bankruptcies** ([SSRN, Platt, Demirkan and Platt](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1524484)), and a 1991 Chicago Fed study found the largest rises in unemployment coincided with the largest rises in filings ([Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago](https://www.chicagofed.org/publications/chicago-fed-letter/1991/december-52)). Studies using individual data often find the filing decision unrelated to local unemployment ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/5209498_The_Effect_of_Unemployment_Benefits_Welfare_Benefits_and_Other_Income_on_Personal_Bankruptcy)), and a CBO review records that one model found unemployment significant and another did not ([CBO](https://www.cbo.gov/sites/default/files/cbofiles/ftpdocs/24xx/doc2421/bankruptcy.pdf)). The report follows the aggregate link as the better-supported finding, because it appears across states and periods, but the evidence does not establish that an individual's job loss causes filing at a fixed rate. Pandemic-era filings fell despite high unemployment, which suggests income support matters ([Harvard Business School](https://www.hbs.edu/ris/download.aspx?name=21-041.pdf)).

## Overspending is an untested minority explanation

The overspending view rests on one study. A UC Davis analysis of **all Delaware personal filings in 2003** concluded that simple overspending drove most bankruptcies, and found bankrupt households had a **median income of $25,738 against $43,341 for solvent households** ([UC Davis](https://www.ucdavis.edu/news/reckless-spending-not-illness-or-job-loss-causes-most-bankruptcy)). The study's author suggests some people deliberately overspend to use the system, but this is an inference rather than a measured result ([UC Davis](https://www.ucdavis.edu/news/reckless-spending-not-illness-or-job-loss-causes-most-bankruptcy)). Because the data are cross-sectional debt ratios, the same figures can reflect income shocks that followed borrowing, so they cannot separate choice from misfortune.

Other sources point to events outside the filer's control. A Canadian insolvency practitioner's 2019 study of 5,800 Ontario insolvencies reports **8 in 10 filers were employed at filing**, and that triggers are often catastrophic life events ([Hoyes Michalos](https://www.hoyes.com/press/joe-debtor/causes-of-insolvency/)). A 2026 vendor survey of 1,421 US adults found cost-of-living pressure (43.4%) and tariffs (41.7%) led the reported causes, with overspending near zero, though the same page gives inconsistent overspending figures ([StudyFinds](https://studyfinds.com/most-americans-months-away-from-financial-collapse-bankruptcy-survey/)). A law-firm newsletter ranks overspending and credit-card debt third behind medical expenses and job loss ([Hampton Roads Legal Services](https://www.hamptonroadslegal.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/HRLS-June-2022-Newsletter.pdf)). No source found shows overspending as the majority cause of recent US or UK filings, so the report treats it as one contributing factor, not the dominant one.

## Non-court alternatives and recovery after filing

In England and Wales, non-court routes already carry most individual insolvency volume. In the October 2026 release, **8,427 IVAs, 4,064 DROs and 696 bankruptcies** were recorded, and **one in 352 adults entered insolvency over the 12 months to 31 October 2026** ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/individual-insolvencies-october-2026)). DROs are for people with low income and few assets and must be applied for through an approved debt adviser, and **48,850 were granted in 2025–26**, up from 24,267 in 2022–23 ([GOV.UK annual report](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/insolvency-service-annual-report-and-accounts-2025-to-2026/insolvency-service-annual-report-and-accounts-2025-2026)). The **£90 application fee was removed in April 2024 and the debt limit rose from £30,000 to £50,000 in June 2024** ([Traders Union](https://tradersunion.com/news/geopolitics-trade/show/2660235-insolvency-service-debt-relief-upgrade/)). An older StepChange guide gives a limit under £15,000, but it predates these changes, so the 2024 figure is the one to use. A debt management plan (DMP) distributes one affordable payment among eligible non-priority creditors ([Support Fund](https://supportfund.co.uk/advice/insights/step-change-debt-help)).

In the US, alternatives are described as first steps rather than measured programmes. A nonprofit credit counsellor negotiates a single monthly payment over **3 to 5 years**, and a counselling session is required before filing ([US Bankruptcy](https://usbankruptcy.net/alternatives/)). Debt settlement is described as a last resort before bankruptcy ([Experian](https://www.experian.com/blogs/ask-experian/alternatives-to-debt-management-plans/)). No official source gives the number of US consumers who use DMPs, settlement or consolidation, so their reach cannot be compared with filings.

Outcomes differ by route. Using random assignment of filings to judges, Dobbie and Song find **Chapter 13 protection raises annual earnings by $5,562 and lowers five-year foreclosure by 19.1 percentage points** ([NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w20520)). Dobbie and Goldsmith-Pinkham find the effects are largest in states with more creditor-friendly laws ([Yale](https://economics.yale.edu/sites/default/files/dobbie_bankruptcy.pdf)). Credit scores recover faster than earnings: a Philadelphia Fed working paper finds scores rebounded quickly after filing ([Philadelphia Fed](https://www.philadelphiafed.org/-/media/frbp/assets/working-papers/2014/wp14-25.pdf)), while a Federal Reserve Board study finds filers keep more limited unsecured credit and pay higher rates than comparable non-filers, with access improving over time ([Federal Reserve Board](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/household-borrowing-after-personal-bankruptcy.htm)). These findings are mostly pre-2020 and US-based, and the UK and US recovery data are not comparable.

## Conclusion

The most defensible answer to why people file is that income loss, illness and family breakdown trigger most filings, and that unsecured and mortgage debt, combined with weak incomes, determines how deep the financial hole is. The medical share is the most contested number. Survey figures of 46% to 67% and the 4% hospitalization estimate measure different things, and the accurate conclusion is that medical shocks are a common contributing trigger, not a proven majority cause. The overspending explanation has the weakest support for recent filings, since it depends on a single 2003 state study.

For policy and personal decisions, the evidence points to income protection and access to non-court options as levers that act on the triggers, while the recovery findings suggest that a granted court discharge matters more than the filing itself. The gaps are large: there are no recent US survey shares, no official cause statistics in either country, and no comparison of UK non-court routes with bankruptcy outcomes. Future estimates will need to separate primary from contributing causes and compare filers with non-filing households over the same period.
