# Opponents Argue Fairness and Mismatch Against Preferences

Opponents of affirmative action, meaning race-conscious preferences in college admission, public employment and government contracting, rest their case on three claims: the Constitution and civil-rights law forbid classifying people by race, racial preferences are unfair to individual applicants, and they harm the students they are meant to help through mismatch and stigma. The Supreme Court adopted the first two in 2023, ending race-conscious college admissions, while the mismatch and stigma claims remain contested empirically. Opponents have won most of their policy changes through state ballot bans, litigation and executive orders rather than through new statutes, and courts and voters have not settled the question. Public opinion depends heavily on wording: a 2023 Pew survey found half of Americans disapproved of colleges considering race in admissions, while a May 2026 online poll found only 26% opposed affirmative action programs described as improving access to higher education. Evidence on effects is also divided. The strongest recent study finds that ending preferences in California lowered minority degree attainment, which cuts against the mismatch hypothesis, but the evidence on bar passage and federal contracting remains disputed.

## Opponents rest on colour-blindness, fairness and stigma

Key terms need defining first. "Affirmative action" in this debate means policies that give applicants or firms a benefit because of race or ethnicity, and opponents mostly target race-conscious rather than class-based or outreach programs. "Strict scrutiny" is the highest constitutional test; the 2023 majority applied it and said that "race itself cannot be a factor in admissions decisions" ([FindLaw, SFFA](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/us-supreme-court/20-1199.html)). "Mismatch" is the hypothesis that students admitted with very large academic preferences struggle and fare worse than they would at a school matching their preparation ([ECGI podcast summary](https://www.ecgi.global/publications/podcasts/richard-sander-ucla-law-a-mismatch-critique-of-affirmative-action)). "Underrepresented minority" (URM) usually means Black, Hispanic and Native American students in the studies cited below ([Bleemer, CSHE](https://cshe.berkeley.edu/publications/affirmative-action-mismatch-and-economic-mobility-after-california%E2%80%99s-proposition-209)).

The first argument is constitutional colour-blindness. Justice Thomas's concurrence in the 2023 case argues that "all forms of discrimination based on race—including so-called affirmative action—are prohibited" ([Justia, 600 U.S. 20-1199](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/600/20-1199/)). Edward Blum, president of Students for Fair Admissions (SFFA), called the 2023 ruling "the beginning of the restoration of the colorblind legal covenant" ([The Harvard Crimson](https://www.thecrimson.com/article/2023/6/30/sffa-decision-reaction/)), and argued that the same logic covers internships, scholarships, hiring and contracting ([SFFA anniversary statement](https://studentsforfairadmissions.org/celebrating-the-one-year-anniversary-of-students-for-fair-admissions-v-harvard-unc-and-the-end-of-race-based-affirmative-action/)). Critics answer that the principle is applied selectively, pointing to the majority's own exception for essays about race ([California Law Review](https://www.californialawreview.org/print/incoherence-colorblind-constitution)).

The second argument is individual fairness. The Court said "college admissions are zero-sum," so a benefit to some applicants comes at others' expense ([Justia, 600 U.S. 20-1199](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/600/20-1199/)). SFFA's central example was Asian American applicants, whom it said were disadvantaged compared with similarly qualified Black, Hispanic and white applicants ([Time](https://time.com/6225372/edward-blum-affirmative-action-supreme-court-interview/)). That claim was rejected at trial: the district court found Harvard did not discriminate on the basis of race and did not use quotas ([Wikipedia, Edward Blum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Blum_(litigant))). The same fairness framing now drives Pacific Legal Foundation suits against K-12 and magnet-school admissions, including a case involving New York City's Specialized High Schools ([PLF press release](https://pacificlegal.org/press-release/nyc-mom-challenges-discriminatory-school-admissions-policy/)). The Supreme Court has also granted review in *Association for Education Fairness v. Montgomery County*, which asks whether a facially neutral policy must show aggregate disparate impact on the targeted group ([Latin Times](https://www.latintimes.com/beyond-affirmative-action-scotus-case-that-could-extend-admissions-rollback-k-12-schools-600343)). Those cases were pending as of the sources' dates.

The third argument is that preferences harm beneficiaries through mismatch and stigma. Richard Sander, a UCLA law professor, argued in a 2004 Stanford Law Review article that without preferences some Black students would not enter law school at all, while others would attend less selective schools and earn better grades ([Manhattan Institute](https://manhattan.institute/article/does-affirmative-action-lead-to-mismatch)). Thomas's SFFA concurrence adds that affirmative action places students where they are less likely to succeed ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/1186454682)). On stigma, Thomas argues that affirmative action rests on a premise that Black people were "inferior" and leaves successful Black people with a stigma of inferiority ([HuffPost](https://www.huffpost.com/entry/clarence-thomas-affirmative-action_n_649e0e12e4b065a315b7e3e2/amp)). Critics say the stigma account is "theoretically impoverished and shallow" and not supported empirically ([BU scholarship record](https://scholarship.law.bu.edu/faculty_scholarship/298)). No peer-reviewed study measuring stigma among beneficiaries appears in the sources, so this claim rests on testimony and opinion.

## Legal and political history moved through rulings, ballots and executive orders

The Supreme Court's position moved in three steps. In *Bakke* (1978), the Court struck down a fixed racial quota at UC Davis medical school, but Justice Powell's deciding vote let race be one factor among many; the decision produced no single majority opinion ([Oyez](https://www.oyez.org/cases/1979/76-811); [Wikipedia, Regents v. Bakke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regents_of_the_University_of_California_v._Bakke)). In *Grutter* (2003), a 5-4 Court upheld the University of Michigan Law School's narrowly tailored use of race to pursue diversity, and it struck down the points-based undergraduate system in the companion case *Gratz* ([Ballotpedia, Grutter](https://ballotpedia.org/Grutter_v._Bollinger); [Wikipedia, Grutter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grutter_v._Bollinger)). O'Connor's majority expected that racial preferences would no longer be needed in about 25 years ([Wikipedia, Grutter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grutter_v._Bollinger)), and that horizon became the target of later challenges.

*SFFA v. Harvard* (2023) held that Harvard's and UNC's programmes violated the Equal Protection Clause ([FindLaw, SFFA](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/us-supreme-court/20-1199.html)). The majority said the programmes lacked measurable objectives, used race in a negative manner, relied on stereotyping and had no endpoint ([Supreme Court syllabus](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/22pdf/20-1199_hgdj.pdf)). Sources differ on the vote count: Ballotpedia lists 6-2 but names three dissenters, and FindLaw reports 6-3. The dissenting opinion by Justice Sotomayor, joined in part by Justices Kagan and Jackson, said the ruling overruled decades of precedent ([Wikisource, Sotomayor dissent](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Students_for_Fair_Admissions_v._President_and_Fellows_of_Harvard_College/Opinion_of_Justice_Sotomayor)). The 6-3 count is the one the dissent supports, so this report follows it. The decision did not expressly overrule *Grutter*, though it effectively ended its diversity rationale ([Ballotpedia, Grutter](https://ballotpedia.org/Grutter_v._Bollinger)).

Opponents also won through the ballot. California's Proposition 209 (1996) banned preferential treatment by race, sex or ethnicity in state employment, public education and public contracting ([CA Secretary of State analysis](https://vigarchive.sos.ca.gov/1996/general/pamphlet/209analysis.htm)). Supporters argued that preferences amounted to reverse discrimination against qualified applicants ([Schwartz Cohen Law](https://schwartzcohenlaw.com/understanding-prop-209-californias-affirmative-action-ban/)). Sources give the 1996 vote as 55% ([Wikipedia, Prop 209](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1996_California_Proposition_209)) and 54% ([Fox News](https://www.foxnews.com/politics/californians-vote-prop-16-affirmative-action)); both are secondary. Voters rejected repeal in 2020 (Proposition 16) by 56% to 44% according to [Ballotpedia](https://news.ballotpedia.org/2020/11/04/california-voters-reject-affirmative-action-proposition/), or 57% to 43% according to the [Federalist Society](https://fedsoc.org/commentary/fedsoc-blog/why-did-california-s-prop-16-fail-a-county-by-county-assessment). In *Schuette* (2014) the Court upheld Michigan's voter-approved ban, reasoning that voters may decide the policy through the democratic process ([Justia, Schuette](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/572/291)).

In employment and contracting, the key instrument was Executive Order 11246, signed by Lyndon Johnson on September 28, 1965, which required federal contractors not to discriminate and to take affirmative action ([Competitive Enterprise Institute](https://cei.org/studies/contradictory-to-our-countrys-laws/)). Because it was an executive order rather than a statute, a president could revoke it. President Trump did so in Executive Order 14173 on January 21, 2025 ([Lexology, Littler report](https://lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=7c7c7c87-ce8b-41a0-95b0-6b245012bf73)). A secondary compliance guide says a Labor Department final rule removing the regulations takes effect October 26, 2026 ([Interviewer Signal](https://interviewersignal.com/blog/federal-hiring-law-changes-2026)); that Federal Register citation was not checked against the primary text. Title VII of the Civil Rights Act was not amended by EO 14173 ([Lexology, Littler report](https://lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=7c7c7c87-ce8b-41a0-95b0-6b245012bf73)).

Since 2023, opponents have shifted to litigation. SFFA dropped its suits against the West Point and Air Force academies after the Defense Department banned race-based admissions ([Higher Ed Dive](https://www.highereddive.com/news/students-for-fair-admissions-drops-lawsuits-west-point-air-force-academy/757503/)). Cases against UC and the UCLA medical school remain pending, with the United States intervening in the UCLA case ([Thompson Coburn, June 2026](https://www.thompsoncoburn.com/insights/higher-education-litigation-summary-june-2026/)). The Department of Justice is reported to be investigating 15 medical schools ([Gibson Dunn](https://www.gibsondunn.com/dei-task-force-update-august-11-2026/)), and a Department report dated October 2026 says it has issued formal findings against at least eight professional schools ([Voice of San Francisco](https://thevoicesf.org/pacific-legal-foundation-joins-lawsuit-challenging-university-of-washington-admissions/)). Those findings are agency positions, not court rulings.

## Effects evidence is contested on graduation, bar passage and contracting

The most cited effects claims come from California. Economist Zachary Bleemer's difference-in-differences study of Proposition 209 found that ending affirmative action pushed underrepresented minority applicants into less selective colleges ([Bleemer, QJE 2021](https://ouci.dntb.gov.ua/en/works/ldNgYBq7/)), and that URM degree attainment fell overall and in STEM ([Bleemer, CSHE](https://cshe.berkeley.edu/publications/affirmative-action-mismatch-and-economic-mobility-after-california%E2%80%99s-proposition-209)). Bleemer's data show affirmative action raised URM enrolment by over 20%, against less than 4% for UC's top-percent policy ([Bleemer site](https://zacharybleemer.com/)). Sander objected that Bleemer's underlying data are not public ([Manhattan Institute](https://manhattan.institute/article/does-affirmative-action-lead-to-mismatch)).

**Graduation disputed:** Bleemer's California study finds that ending preferences lowered URM degree attainment overall ([Bleemer, CSHE](https://cshe.berkeley.edu/publications/affirmative-action-mismatch-and-economic-mobility-after-california%E2%80%99s-proposition-209)), while the Arcidiacono and Lovenheim review reports that bans do not affect minority college graduation rates overall, though completion falls for lower-ranked students ([Arcidiacono & Lovenheim, Duke](https://public.econ.duke.edu/~psarcidi/aa2.pdf); [Wisconsin copy of the 2016 review](https://users.ssc.wisc.edu/~gwallace/Papers/Arcidiacono%20&%20Lovenheim%20(2016).pdf)). The report follows Bleemer for California because his design is a quasi-experimental causal test and is more recent than the review, which predates it; the review's aggregate claim covers many states, so the disagreement is not fully settled.

Law-school mismatch is the second dispute. Sander and Steinbuch argue that Sander's data suggest mismatch could fully explain half of the Black-white bar passage gap ([Sander & Steinbuch, Supreme Court URLs cited](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/URLs_Cited/OT2022/20-1199/20-1199-1.pdf)). Ayres, Brooks and Shelley argue that a second-choice analysis of Bar Passage Study data shows no mismatch effect on bar passage ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0144818821000569)), and a separate comment says correctly coding Sander's missing race data contradicts his conclusion ([JLE comment](https://jle.aals.org/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1801&context=home)). The evidence does not decide this question: each side relies on the same dataset and different coding and assumptions, so the report treats mismatch as unproven for bar passage.

Employment evidence on Executive Order 11246 is the third dispute. Miller's study finds that contractors raised their African American employment share by 0.13 to 0.15 percentage points a year relative to non-contractors ([DOL CLEAR](https://clear.dol.gov/study/persistent-effect-temporary-affirmative-action-miller-2014)), with gains that persisted after the rules were lifted ([AEA archives](https://www.aeaweb.org/research/can-temporary-affirmative-action-policies-have-lasting-effects-from-the-archives)). A Census working paper using linked employer-employee data from 2000 to 2014 finds no effect on employment shares or hiring for any minority group, with effects about 100 times smaller than Miller's ([Census CES WP 22-54](https://www2.census.gov/ces/wp/2022/CES-WP-22-54.pdf); [Cambridge repository](https://www.repository.cam.ac.uk/items/196a6fc1-b50c-44d8-ad84-10102ab7103a)). The evidence does not decide between them, because Miller covers 1978 to 2004 and the Census paper covers a later period after much of the expansion had already happened; the Census design is the more recent test, but it does not overturn Miller's earlier window.

Contracting evidence is more mixed. A study of highway subcontracting requirements finds they did not substantially raise prime contractors' bids or costs, though the authors say that does not prove no effect on contracting ([Cleveland Fed](https://www.clevelandfed.org/en/newsroom%20and%20events/publications/working%20papers/~/media/2024108be812481eb3d3219d036ef424.ashx)). A study of the SBA 8(a) programme finds participants perform well relative to baseline firms, but attributes this to funding rather than mentorship, and finds 8(a) firms substantially more likely to go out of business ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11187-017-9860-z)).

Harvard's trial record shows a further dispute. SFFA's expert found that Asian American applicants received lower personal ratings, and Harvard's expert disputed that the penalty was established ([Cornell JLPP](https://publications.lawschool.cornell.edu/jlpp/2018/11/17/on-the-basis-of-personality-how-harvards-admissions-policy-hurts-asian-americans-and-the-future-of-affirmative-action/); [Harvard Crimson, 2018](https://www.thecrimson.com/article/2018/6/19/asian-american-academics/)). The district court found the disparity did not burden Asian American applicants significantly more than Harvard's race-conscious policy burdened white applicants ([Harvard Crimson, 2019](https://www.thecrimson.com/article/2019/10/2/admissions-suit-decision/)). Estimates of Asian American class share under academic-only admissions conflict, at 43% in one account and above 51% in another ([Expert Institute](https://www.expertinstitute.com/resources/insights/expert-witnesses-in-the-harvard-discrimination-lawsuit-arrive-at-conflicting-conclusions/); [Harvard Crimson, 2018](https://www.thecrimson.com/article/2018/6/19/asian-american-academics/)), and the sources do not resolve it.

## Alternatives and public opinion depend on wording

Opponents have offered few formal packages. The alternatives in the sources are percent plans, socioeconomic preferences, place-based preferences and holistic review. Texas's top-ten-percent rule, adopted in 1997, guarantees admission to public universities for students in the top 10% of their high school class ([NBER Digest](https://www.nber.org/digest/jun20/results-texass-experiment-increasing-college-diversity)). Black, Denning and Rothstein find it raised attendance, graduation and earnings for students from under-resourced high schools without reducing these outcomes for students crowded out ([NBER Digest](https://www.nber.org/digest/jun20/results-texass-experiment-increasing-college-diversity)). Kapor's 2024 study finds it raised enrollment of economically disadvantaged students but had few other diversity effects ([NBER Digest](https://www.nber.org/digest/202408/effects-texas-top-ten-percent-college-admissions-plan)). Cortes and Klasik found little evidence the plan increased racial and financial diversity ([Daily Texan](https://thedailytexan.com/2019/07/30/texas-top-10-rule-makes-little-difference-in-diversity-on-campus-study-says/)).

Across studies, race-neutral tools increase Black and Hispanic enrollment much less than race-conscious admissions. A study covering California and other states found affirmative action raised URM enrollment by over 20%, top-percent policies by under 4% and holistic review by about 7%, and that none of the common alternatives substantially changed socioeconomic composition, with only affirmative action modestly increasing lower-income enrollment ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S004727272300021X)). After the 2023 ruling, the AP reports that some elite campuses dropped Black enrollment to as low as 2% of the freshman class ([OPB/AP](https://www.opb.org/article/2025/10/24/black-enrollment-affirmative-action-ban/)). Brookings notes that class-based preferences do not prevent major declines in Black enrollment in many contexts ([Brookings](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/the-complex-ramifications-of-students-for-fair-admissions-v-harvard/)). The Century Foundation, which favours class-based preferences, reports they produce higher socioeconomic diversity ([Century Foundation](https://tcf.org/content/report/achieving-better-diversity/)); that report is advocacy.

Public opinion is sharply sensitive to wording. Pew's 2023 survey, fielded March 27 to April 2, 2023 among 5,079 panelists, found 50% disapproved of selective colleges taking race and ethnicity into account, 33% approved and 16% were not sure ([Pew fact sheet](https://www.pewresearch.org/fact-sheet/topic-affirmative-action-in-college-admissions/)). Party divides the public most sharply: 74% of Republicans and Republican leaners disapproved ([Pew fact sheet](https://www.pewresearch.org/fact-sheet/topic-affirmative-action-in-college-admissions/)), while Democrats were far less opposed, at 29% ([Pew fact sheet](https://www.pewresearch.org/fact-sheet/topic-affirmative-action-in-college-admissions/)). Among Black adults, 47% approved ([Pew fact sheet](https://www.pewresearch.org/fact-sheet/topic-affirmative-action-in-college-admissions/)). A May 2026 Verasight poll, with 1,000 respondents and a ±3.2% margin, found 58% favoured and 26% opposed "affirmative action programs designed to improve access to higher education" ([Verasight](https://data.verasight.io/politics/a-majority-of-americans-favor-affirmative-action-in-higher-education/)). Gallup's broad question found 61% in favour in 2018 ([Gallup](https://news.gallup.com/opinion/polling-matters/317006/affirmative-action-public-opinion.aspx)). The gap is largely a matter of question design; Cato, summarizing YouGov and Pew, reports about 7 in 10 opposed when affirmative action is described without its purpose, and 5 to 6 in 10 when its intent is mentioned ([Cato](https://www.cato.org/blog/americans-say-they-affirmative-action-yet-oppose-racial-preferences-college-admissions)). Gallup's 2016 reading found 70% said colleges should admit students solely on merit ([Gallup](https://news.gallup.com/poll/193508/oppose-colleges-considering-race-admissions.aspx)). Pew's 2023 respondents who disapprove mostly say race consideration makes admissions less fair ([Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2023/06/08/perceived-impacts-of-factoring-race-and-ethnicity-into-college-admissions/)).

## Conclusion

The case against affirmative action is strongest where it rests on constitutional principle and on the law as now applied: the Supreme Court has adopted colour-blindness and zero-sum fairness, and voters in several states have banned preferences. The empirical case is weaker and more contested. The strongest recent causal study points to lower minority degree attainment after California's ban, and mismatch and stigma remain claims without consistent support. Opponents' alternatives, such as percent plans and socioeconomic preferences, raise enrollment for low-income students but do not restore racial diversity to earlier levels, so the trade-off between race-neutral and race-conscious policy is real.

Public opinion makes the debate's outcome uncertain. Opposition in Pew's 2023 data reached half of adults, but polls that describe affirmative action by its purpose show majority support, so the answer depends on how the question is framed. The newest evidence, including the pending Montgomery County case and the 2026 federal investigations, will test whether the 2023 principle extends from universities to schools, employers and contractors. Readers should treat the 2026 litigation and executive actions as unresolved, and should watch for court outcomes and any public release of the Bleemer data that could settle the graduation and mismatch disputes.
