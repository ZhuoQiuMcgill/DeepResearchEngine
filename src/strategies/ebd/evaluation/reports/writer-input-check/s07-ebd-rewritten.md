# Darwin Explained How Variation Was Sorted

Darwin offered a real but partial explanation. He showed how small, heritable differences among individuals could accumulate into new forms through **natural selection**, but he explicitly did not explain what causes most variation. In the 1859 *Origin* he wrote that "our ignorance of the laws of variation is profound" ([Darwin, *Origin* (1859), Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1228/1228-h/1228-h.htm)). His one attempt at a mechanism of inheritance, pangenesis, was offered in 1868 as a "provisional hypothesis or speculation" ([Darwin Online, *Variation* vol. 2](https://darwin-online.org.uk/converted/published/1868_Variation_F877/1868_Variation_F877.2.html)). Critics in his lifetime pressed the point most sharply through Fleeming Jenkin's 1867 blending argument, which later readers dispute. Mendelian genetics, rediscovered in **1900**, replaced pangenesis, and the modern synthesis tied selection to genetic variation. Today **mutation** is called the ultimate source of new variation, with recombination and gene flow as further sources ([Albert.io](https://www.albert.io/blog/genetic-diversity-ap-biology-review/); [Wikipedia, Genetic variation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetic_variation)). The claim that Darwin explained differences in traits is therefore true only if "explain" means explaining how differences are sorted, not where they come from.

## What the key terms mean here

The sources do not define "trait" formally, so this report uses it for any heritable feature of an organism. Darwin's term "variation" referred to differences among individuals of one species. He treated these as a basic fact: no two members of a species are "cast in the very same mould" ([Darwin, *Origin* (1859), Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1228/1228-h/1228-h.htm)). Modern usage narrows the term to genetic variation, whose sources are listed in the later section. Natural selection, in Darwin's definition, is "the preservation of favourable variations and the rejection of injurious ones" ([Darwin, *Origin* (1859), Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1228/1228-h/1228-h.htm)). Darwin measured none of this numerically. His evidence was qualitative, drawn largely from breeding and comparative anatomy. Modern measures are different: mutation rates per generation, recombination counts and allele frequencies. The notes show that "explanation" is itself graded differently by different readers, which is why the same record can be read as either success or failure.

## What Darwin said caused variation, and how selection sorted it

Darwin's account of variation had two layers. The first was a frank admission that causes were unknown. He wrote that "a cause for each must exist" for every slight difference, yet he did not specify those causes ([Darwin, *Origin* (1859), Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1228/1228-h/1228-h.htm)). He rejected "chance" as "wholly incorrect" but used the word to mark ignorance ([Darwin, *Origin* (1859), Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1228/1228-h/1228-h.htm)). His main working hypothesis was that conditions of life act first on the reproductive system. He based this on the effects of confinement and cultivation on breeding, and on "sporting" buds in plants ([Darwin, *Origin* (1859), Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1228/1228-h/1228-h.htm)).

The second layer was selection. Darwin argued that any variation "however slight and from whatever cause proceeding" that is useful will tend to preserve its possessor and be inherited ([Darwin, *Origin* (1859), Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1228/1228-h/1228-h.htm)). In the 1872 edition he added that a variation that is not inherited is unimportant, and that inheritable deviations are "endless" ([Darwin, *Origin* (6th ed., 1872), Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/2009/2009-h/2009-h.htm)). Selection thus needed heritability, and the 1859 text admitted that the laws of inheritance were "quite unknown" ([Darwin, *Origin* (1859), Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1228/1228-h/1228-h.htm)). That gap is the central limit of his explanation.

Darwin gave environment and use a limited role. He thought climate and food had a slight direct effect, "extremely small in animals" and perhaps greater in plants ([Darwin, *Origin* (1859), Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1228/1228-h/1228-h.htm)). He also held that use strengthens and disuse diminishes organs, and he cited the domestic duck's altered wing and leg bones as an example ([Darwin, *Origin* (1859), Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1228/1228-h/1228-h.htm)). Even so, he conceded that "we cannot tell how much to attribute to the accumulative action of natural selection, and how much to the definite action of the conditions of life" ([Sacred Texts, *Origin* Ch. V](https://www.sacred-texts.com/aor/darwin/origin/or05.htm)).

Pigeon breeding was his main test case. Darwin studied the breeds closely and concluded that all domestic pigeons descend from the rock pigeon, *Columba livia* ([Darwin, *Origin* (1859), Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1228/1228-h/1228-h.htm)). He argued that breeders' "accumulative selection" shows how small variations add up, while noting that human selection acts only on visible traits, whereas nature acts on internal constitutional differences too ([Darwin, *Origin* (1859), Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1228/1228-h/1228-h.htm)). The argument works by analogy. It depends on the common-ancestry premise, and Darwin's sources show that fanciers themselves believed their breeds came from separate species.

Pangenesis supplied the missing inheritance mechanism in 1868. Cells were said to throw off unobservable units, "gemmules," which gather in the reproductive elements ([Darwin, *Variation* (1868), Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/3332/pg3332-images.html)). Darwin argued that variability "depends on at least two distinct groups of causes," including the deficiency, fusion and transposition of gemmules, and the redevelopment of gemmules long dormant ([Darwin, *Variation* (1868), Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Variation_of_Animals_and_Plants_under_Domestication/XXVII)). Through this route, changes from use could pass into offspring. A **Genetics**-journal review notes the cost of this: inheritance of acquired characters "could not explain some evolutionary situations," as Darwin himself knew ([Genetics, Darwin and Genetics](https://academic.oup.com/genetics/article/183/3/757/6063182)). Sources disagree on the framing. One calls pangenesis a mechanism that generated "ample variability on which selection could act" ([Genetics, Darwin and Genetics](https://academic.oup.com/genetics/article/183/3/757/6063182)). The NCSE text says Darwin made "no mention of a mechanism of heredity in the *Origin*" ([NCSE, Excursion Ch. 2](https://ncse.ngo/excursion-chapter-2-genetics-and-evolution)). Both are consistent with the 1859 text, which left inheritance unexplained.

## Limits and objections in Darwin's lifetime

The most important objection came from Fleeming Jenkin's 1867 review. The classic account holds that Jenkin argued that blending inheritance would swamp new advantageous variations, so selection could never establish them ([Wikipedia, Blending inheritance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blending_inheritance)). Darwin's response is documented in the fifth edition, where he wrote that he had not appreciated "how rarely single variations, whether slight or strongly-marked could be perpetuated" ([Wikipedia, Swamping argument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swamping_argument)). An NCSE text says Darwin then "gave added weight to use and disuse and Lamarckian effects" ([NCSE, Excursion Ch. 2](https://ncse.ngo/excursion-chapter-2-genetics-and-evolution)).

The record on Jenkin is disputed. A 2024 reassessment argues that the blending and swamping themes are "strikingly absent from Jenkin's text," and that they were read in later by scholars trying to reconcile selection with Mendelian genes ([PubMed, Hoquet 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38656677/)). That paper says Darwin "was probably mostly unimpressed," and identifies the real problems as intercrossing and reversion ([Springer, J Hist Biol 2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10739-024-09770-y)). A Cambridge-published study instead argues that the swamping argument depended on blending and that a flaw in Jenkin's model was exposed in 1871; once removed, the argument no longer blocks selection within a lineage, given a high enough mutation rate ([Cambridge, British Journal for the History of Science](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/british-journal-for-the-history-of-science/article/abs/did-jenkins-swamping-argument-invalidate-darwins-theory-of-natural-selection/263EE376F7735A474F23DBBD503A363B)). The review's date is also inconsistent in the notes: the review is dated June 1867, while one encyclopedia article dates the objection to 1868 ([Wikipedia, Modern synthesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_synthesis_(20th_century))). The 1867 date is the better-supported one.

A second objection concerned Mendel. The **Genetics** paper of 2016 states there is "compelling evidence that Darwin knew nothing of Mendel then, or at any time of his life" ([Genetics, Darwin's Influence on Mendel](https://doi.org/10.1534/genetics.116.194613)). Mendel, by contrast, acquired the *Origin* in 1863 and probably read it ([Genetics, Darwin's Influence on Mendel](https://doi.org/10.1534/genetics.116.194613)). A 2019 *Heredity* article notes that Focke's 1881 book, which summarised Mendel's experiments, was in Darwin's library, but "the pages of these summaries remain uncut" ([Nature, Mendel and Darwin](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41437-019-0289-9)). A 2022 PNAS article says Darwin had access in 1881 to a published account that mentioned Mendel's work ([PNAS, Mendel and Darwin](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2122144119)). Access is not reading, and the uncut pages are the stronger evidence, so the best reading is that Darwin did not engage with Mendel.

Darwin's pangenesis faced hard empirical tests. Francis Galton's blood-transfusion experiments in 1870 were taken to show that gemmules could not be observed in offspring ([Embryo Project, via Academia.edu](https://www.academia.edu/10552077/Charles_Darwins_Theory_of_Pangenesis)). Darwin replied in *Nature* that he had never mentioned blood in his writings ([Wikipedia, Lamarckism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lamarckism)). Pangenesis was "almost uniformly rejected" when published ([PubMed, The Criticisms of Pangenesis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30037392/)). Whether Darwin was Lamarckian is contested. Britannica says he accepted inheritance of acquired characteristics as one contributory factor ([Britannica, Lamarckism](https://www.britannica.com/science/Lamarckism)). Others argue that Lamarck is "somewhat unfairly" linked to the theory ([Sources.com, Lamarckism](https://www.sources.com/SSR/Docs/SSRW-Lamarckism.htm)). These are mostly reference and blog sources, so the dispute remains open.

## How the explanation was revised after 1882

Pangenesis implied blending inheritance, and blending was discarded after particulate inheritance was accepted after 1900 ([Wikipedia, Blending inheritance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blending_inheritance)). Mendel's work was rediscovered that year by Hugo de Vries, Carl Correns and Erich von Tschermak ([Oregon State Univ. OER](https://open.oregonstate.education/cultivatedplants/chapter/genetics/)). Biologists replaced gemmules with genes and moved through germ-plasm and chromosomal theories ([ASU Embryo Project](https://embryo.asu.edu/pages/charles-darwins-theory-pangenesis)). August Weismann had already challenged pangenesis in 1892 ([Darwin, Then and Now](https://darwinthenandnow.com/inheritance-second-principle-of-natural-selection/)). Pangenesis was not wholly lost, however. A review of its influence states that nearly every major late-nineteenth-century theory of heredity began with a discussion of it, naming de Vries, Weismann and Galton ([PubMed, The Influence of Darwin's Pangenesis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30037393/)).

The modern synthesis of the 1930s and 1940s addressed the variation gap directly. In 1918 Fisher showed that continuous variation could arise from many discrete genetic loci ([Wikidoc, Modern evolutionary synthesis](https://www.wikidoc.org/index.php/Modern_evolutionary_synthesis)). Dobzhansky's 1937 *Genetics and the Origin of Species* is often treated as the synthesis's start ([Social Sci LibreTexts](https://socialsci.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Anthropology/Biological_Anthropology/Book:_Biological_Anthropology_(Saneda_and_Field)/I:_Evolutionary_Theory/1.7:_Modern_Synthesis)). Huxley coined the term "modern synthesis" in 1942 ([Missouri Libraries](https://library.missouri.edu/specialcollections/exhibits/show/darwin/genes)). Its architects treated mutation and recombination as the origin of variation, and selection as the sorting process. A critique notes that this narrowed Darwin's wider list of causes ([SciELO, Evolutionary Genetics](https://www.scielo.br/j/gmb/a/bjgRXLDWH7bjfnQMJx3qVjK/?format=pdf&lang=en)).

Current accounts give mutation as the ultimate source of new alleles. The human mutation rate is the best-quantified figure. A 2015 PLoS Genetics estimate gives **1.61 ± 0.13 × 10⁻⁸ per base per generation**, and the paper notes that published estimates differ by up to a factor of 2 ([PLoS Genetics, Calibrating the Human Mutation Rate](https://journals.plos.org/plosgenetics/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pgen.1005550)). Wikipedia's figure of about 1.1 × 10⁻⁸ and about 64 new mutations per generation is lower and less authoritative ([Wikipedia, Mutation rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutation_rate)). Recombination is comparable in scale: a population genetics text gives about one crossover per chromosome per generation, roughly 35 in total ([UCSD Personal Genomics textbook](http://admixgenomics.ucsd.edu/personal-genomics-textbook/_sources/ancestry/popgen/recombination.md)). In one *Neisseria meningitidis* study, recombination was estimated to be about ten times as important as mutation in generating novel genomes ([MBE, Neisseria meningitidis](https://academic.oup.com/mbe/article/22/3/562/1075927)). The sources do not put mutation, recombination and gene flow on one scale for animals or plants, so no single ranking holds.

Whether mutation or standing variation drives adaptation is unresolved. A 2015 *Genetics* paper found that after a point adaptation proceeds almost exclusively by new mutations, and that faster change depletes standing variation more quickly ([Genetics, Catch Me if You Can](https://dx.doi.org/10.1534/genetics.115.178574)). A 2026 bioRxiv preprint reports that standing variation was primary under some shifts but declined in gradual ones; it has not been peer reviewed ([bioRxiv, polygenic adaptation](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.09.14.751342v1.full)). Historians also dispute the synthesis itself. The extended synthesis argues that developmental processes share responsibility for the origin of character variation ([Royal Society, The extended evolutionary synthesis](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/doi/10.1098/rspb.2015.1019)). A 2017 analysis argues the architects did discuss mutation bias ([ScienceDirect, Modern Synthesis](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/social-sciences/modern-synthesis)).

## Conclusion

Darwin's achievement was to show that heritable variation plus selection could produce divergence and adaptation, and to make the cause of variation an explicit open question. His own texts make the limits plain: the 1859 *Origin* left both the origin and the inheritance of variation unexplained, and the 1868 pangenesis was a provisional guess that later biology replaced. The strongest evidence for this reading comes from Darwin's own words and from the 1900 rediscovery of Mendel's work.

The main remaining disputes concern how much of the gap Darwin's critics identified was real. Jenkin's swamping argument is either a decisive blow or a misread text, depending on which 2024 or 2004 study one trusts. Whether Darwin was Lamarckian, and whether pangenesis was a blunder or a useful stimulus, depends on how far modern findings on acquired characters are read back onto him. Those questions matter for historians and for study, but they do not change the modern answer: mutation supplies new variation, recombination and gene flow reshuffle it, and selection sorts it. Readers who need a precise current figure should check the 2015 human mutation estimate and the 2026 standing-variation preprint against later peer-reviewed work.
