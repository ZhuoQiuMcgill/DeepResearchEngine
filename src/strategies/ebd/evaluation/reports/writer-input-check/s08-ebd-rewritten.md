# Existing Laws Already Reach Repeated Harm

The premise is not accurate. No statute names "sociopaths" as a legal category, but several laws already protect people from repeated harm regardless of the offender's diagnosis. England and Wales has a controlling or coercive behaviour offence carrying a [maximum of five years](https://policing.uk/offences/controlling-coercive-behaviour), and a [stalking protection order](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2019/9/section/2/enacted) that a court can impose without a criminal conviction. US federal law makes stalking punishable by [up to 5 years in prison](https://www.thefederalcriminalattorneys.com/federal-stalking). The gaps are real: these laws are triggered by conduct, not by personality, and coverage is patchy, especially for manipulation that never forms a clear pattern of offending. Legislatures have not created a sociopath-specific law mainly because the label has no stable legal or clinical meaning, the predictive evidence is moderate and contested, and the one large attempt to target dangerous personality disorder in England was costly and abandoned. Proposals that would have made traits a basis for restriction have stalled, and the sources show more debate than enactment. Protection against repeated harm therefore rests on conduct-based law, supplemented by contested civil-commitment regimes that apply only in narrow circumstances.

## What the terms mean and how they are measured

"Sociopath" is not a diagnosis or a legal term in the sources reviewed. The closest clinical category is antisocial personality disorder, which the [DSM defines](https://scholarship.law.upenn.edu/faculty_chapters/106/) with all but one criterion concerning repetitive antisocial behaviour, according to Stephen Morse's US law chapter. "Psychopathy" is a research construct measured mainly with the [Psychopathy Checklist-Revised (PCL-R)](https://theconversation.com/why-psychopath-is-a-dangerous-label-when-it-comes-to-criminal-justice-278133), and The Conversation notes it is "not a legal category" in its own right. "Stalking" in US federal law is a [course of conduct](https://www.cronisraelsandstark.com/federal-stalking) that secondary sources describe as two or more acts, while England's coercive-control offence requires [repeated or continuous behaviour](https://policing.uk/offences/controlling-coercive-behaviour) in an intimate or family relationship.

## Conduct-based laws cover repeated harm, unevenly

Most protection is organised around behaviour. The EU's [Directive 2024/1385](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/EN/legal-content/summary/preventing-and-combating-violence-against-women-and-domestic-violence-throughout-the-european-union.html) criminalises cyberstalking and cyberharassment and allows emergency barring, restraining and protection orders, and the [Directive must be implemented by June 2027](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/RegData/etudes/BRIE/2024/767146/EPRS_BRI(2024)767146_EN.pdf). Commentary argues that it [does not address the full spectrum](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=OJ%3AL_202401385) of violence against women and excludes the most severe forms, such as rape ([ZStW, 2025](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/zstw-2025-0010/html)).

In the United States, the picture is fragmented. Coercive control is [not a federal crime](https://coparentingguide.org/coercive-control-custody/), and [Hawaii is the only state](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/so-sue-me/202609/the-new-state-laws-defining-abuse-without-a-single-bruise) to criminalise it, as a petty misdemeanor, with the provision extended through June 2031 (Psychology Today, September 2026). Other states mainly rely on civil protective orders and custody factors. The sources disagree on the count: one lists [eleven states](https://www.narcissisticabuserehab.com/coercive-control-legislation-around-the-world/) with enacted coercive-control legislation (May 2026), while another lists [four explicit states](https://coparentingguide.org/coercive-control-custody/) (2026). No official register was found to settle the conflict, so the honest answer is that the number is uncertain and probably small.

England and Wales has the most developed tools. Section 76 of the Serious Crime Act 2015 [came into force on 29 December 2015](https://inforrm.org/2016/01/13/controlling-or-coercive-behaviour-section-76-of-the-serious-crime-act-2015-rhory-robertson/) and was designed to close a gap where individual controlling acts would not each be an offence ([LexisNexis](https://www.lexisnexis.co.uk/legal/guidance/controlling-or-coercive-behaviour-offences)). The Stalking Protection Act 2019 allows a magistrates' court to make an order where the defendant has carried out acts associated with stalking, poses a risk to another person, and a prohibition is necessary ([legislation.gov.uk](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2019/9/section/2/enacted)). Such an order lasts for [at least two years](https://zoomlaw.co.uk/2019-9-ukpga.html) unless varied, and breach without reasonable excuse is an offence ([legislation.gov.uk](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2019/9/data.html)). Because the order does not require a conviction, it is the most direct route for someone who has not yet offended but is causing repeated harm.

These tools do not depend on diagnosis. Diagnosis matters mainly in sentencing, detention and risk assessment, not in whether conduct is an offence.

## Why no sociopath-specific law has followed

The first reason is definitional. Antisocial personality disorder is a diagnosis, while psychopathy is a dimensional construct measured by a checklist, and the law has not adopted either as an element of an offence. The Conversation argues that the label carries stigma and can distort sentencing ([The Conversation, 2026](https://theconversation.com/why-psychopath-is-a-dangerous-label-when-it-comes-to-criminal-justice-278133)), and Phys.org reports commentators' view that assessment is [dimensional, not binary](https://phys.org/news/2026-06-psychopath-dangerous-criminal-justice.html) (June 2026).

The second reason is predictive evidence. A 2023 umbrella review pooled 17 effect sizes from 10 studies, about 77,000 participants, and found a [pooled correlation of r = .284](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36608488/) between psychopathy and dangerousness. That measure combined violent and sexual recidivism with self-reported aggression, so it is not a direct prediction of violent offending ([Liverpool post-print](https://livrepository.liverpool.ac.uk/3166624/6/Gillespie_et_al_withSuppMat_CPR_Psychopathy%20&%20dangerousness[post-print].pdf)). An older review reported a [mean effect of 0.79 for violence](https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/CRDWeb/ShowRecord.asp?ID=11997008497&ID=11997008497), though its inclusion criteria were criticised. A 2026 review of violence in non-affective psychosis, covering 13 studies and 1,446 patients, found [few predictors](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41747436/) and none that survived Bonferroni correction. Correlations of this size mean psychopathy scores carry real but partial information. They support individual judgement rather than settle it, which is a poor basis for a statute.

The third reason is treatment. Scholars have argued that the [untreatability assumption](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0160252711001117) has discouraged commitment, but a review of Olver and colleagues found [little evidence](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/333499403_Treatment_of_psychopathic_offenders_Evidence_issues_and_controversies) that targeting personality traits reduces recidivism, while changes in criminogenic needs are linked to lower reoffending. A 2021 meta-analysis of prison psychological interventions found the evidence [inconclusive for most](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8376657/) programmes. If untreatability is contested, a law premised on it rests on uncertain ground.

The fourth reason is legal philosophy. Morse argues that criminal law should [punish actions rather than character](https://illinoislawreview.org/wp-content/ilr-content/articles/2004/2/Morse.pdf). Current US practice holds that psychopathy does not remove criminal responsibility ([Aarhus University](https://pure.au.dk/portal/en/publications/on-blaming-and-punishing-psychopaths/)). Trait-based restriction would shift the question from "what did the person do" to "what kind of person is this", which civil-commitment doctrine tries to keep separate from punishment. The US Supreme Court in [Kansas v. Crane](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/00-957.ZO.html) said civil commitment must not become a mechanism for retribution or general deterrence.

Civil commitment is available, but narrowly. The Court upheld Kansas's [Sexually Violent Predator Act in 1997](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/521/346), which covers a "mental abnormality" or "personality disorder" likely to cause predatory sexual violence. Psychopathy per se is [not considered a sufficient mental abnormality](https://www.academia.edu/100627312/Psychopathy_and_Criminal_Responsibility) for involuntary commitment in the US. In England, the 2007 amendments replaced the treatability test with an [appropriate medical treatment test](https://www.mentalhealthlaw.co.uk/Section_3_Mental_Health_Act_1983), and a commentary argues this could allow long-term detention of high-risk patients with personality disorder on risk alone ([Cambridge, Advances in Psychiatric Treatment](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/advances-in-psychiatric-treatment/article/personality-disorder-and-the-mental-health-act-1983-amended/83CA3190ABD01DBDF892E0A754C4752B)).

European human-rights law sets limits. In [W.A. v. Switzerland (2021)](https://hudoc.echr.coe.int/app/conversion/pdf/?library=ECHR&id=003-7167836-9726000&filename=Judgment+W.A.+v.+Switzerland+-+Violations+in+preventive+detention+of+mentally+ill+serious+offender.pdf), the Court found a violation where a man with a difficult-to-treat personality disorder was held without a suitable institution. In [Ilnseher v. Germany (2017)](https://hudoc.echr.coe.int/app/conversion/pdf/?library=ECHR&id=003-5617448-7103977&filename=Judgment+Ilnseher+v.+Germany+-+retrospective+preventive+detention+of+convicted+murderer.pdf), preventive detention for a mental disorder was justified because the institution was suitable for therapy. In [K.A. v. Austria (19 May 2026)](https://www.echrcaselaw.com/en/echr-decisions/k-a-v-austria-forensic-therapeutic-confinement-domestic-violence-and-the-autonomous-meaning-of-mental-disorder-under-article-5-echr/), the Court found no violation, but said objective medical expertise and a disorder of sufficient severity remain indispensable. These cases show the Court does not ban personality-based detention; it demands a real, severe disorder and fit with treatment.

## Proposals and debates: stalled, narrowed or reversed

The clearest historical attempt was in England. The [Butler Committee (1975)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Butler_Committee) proposed replacing "psychopathy" with "personality disorder" in law, and a 1989 Law Commission draft largely followed, but successive governments ignored both. The [Mental Health Act 2007](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2007/12/pdfs/ukpga_20070012_en.pdf) then removed "psychopathic disorder" as one of the four categories, and its [explanatory notes](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2007/12/notes/division/6/1?view=plain) say that personality disorders without "abnormally aggressive or seriously irresponsible conduct" fell outside the old definition.

The Dangerous and Severe Personality Disorder (DSPD) programme, [launched in 2001](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dangerous_and_severe_personality_disorder), used a severe-personality test based on [PCL-R scores of 25 or above](https://www.psychiatrictimes.com/view/personality-disorder-and-dangerousness) plus dangerousness. Its cost is disputed: one [Channel 4 News report](https://www.channel4.com/news/dangerous-offenders-scheme-to-be-axed) gave about £200m, while a [later review](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/275895870_The_lessons_and_legacy_of_the_programme_for_dangerous_and_severe_personality_disorders_Lessons_and_legacy_of_the_DSPD_programme) gave about £500m. A [Cambridge chapter](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/mind-state-and-society/origins-of-the-dangerous-and-severe-personality-disorder-programme-in-england/7DE2EBB0314A3D0E314D576B83505A8D) reports that attempts to enshrine DSPD in law all failed. Psychiatrists opposed it, with a [BJPsych editorial](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/the-british-journal-of-psychiatry/article/dangerous-and-severe-personality-disorder/EDF88CEB9ECFFD32529F5E590417BD2E) describing opposition to using a medical "diagnosis" for public-protection goals. The Royal College of Psychiatrists' Mike Shooter is quoted saying [most dangerous people do not have a personality disorder](https://www.opendemocracy.net/en/shine-a-light/stuck-in-dangerous-and-severe-personality-disorder-limbo/). Professor Tyrer praised DSPD for improving wider services, which shows the debate is not one-sided.

Other proposals went the same way. The [Law Commission's 2013 insanity proposals](https://oro.open.ac.uk/47651/1/Lisa%20Claydon%20and%20Paul%20Catley%20Abolishing%20the%20Insanity%20Verdict.pdf) did not treat antisocial personality disorder as a qualifying condition. A [parliamentary petition](https://petition.parliament.uk/petitions/174435) says the 2013 recommendation has been ignored, though this is advocacy wording. The [Scottish Law Commission's 2004 report](https://www.scotlawcom.gov.uk/sites/default/files/2026-02/rep195.pdf) recommended excluding psychopathic personality disorder from the insanity defence. In the United States, [Oklahoma redefined its insanity plea](https://www.npr.org/sections/health-shots/2016/08/03/486669552/does-a-psychopath-who-kills-get-to-use-the-insanity-defense) to exclude antisocial personality disorder, with the bill signed in May 2016 and effective that November; NPR notes that "a number of states" did something similar. New York's [Senate Bill S7794](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/bills/2015/2015-s7794), sponsored by Senator Catharine Young, would have added antisocial personality disorder and psychopathy to the sex-offender commitment definition. It passed committee 11–0 in June 2016, and the sources found no later action.

Germany shows a different path. After the Federal Constitutional Court ruling on preventive detention, [about 30% of detainees](https://germanlawarchive.iuscomp.org/index.php/2024/02/27/bverfge-128-326-preventive-detention-sicherungsverwahrung/) received therapy, against 79.3% who appeared to need it. This reflects a treatment-oriented model, not a psychopath statute.

The sources found no victims' organisation, minister or EU body calling for a law against sociopaths. The advocacy visible in the sources is limited to Oklahoma's legislative campaigner and the New York sponsor, plus a 2026 Washington Times letter that is personal opinion rather than a proposal.

## Conclusion

The question assumes a legal vacuum that the evidence does not show. Laws already reach repeated harm through stalking offences, coercive-control offences, protection orders and civil commitment, and these tools do not depend on whether an offender is labelled a sociopath. The real gaps are in manipulation that never forms a provable pattern, in coverage that differs sharply between US states and EU member states, and in the people who have not yet offended but pose a risk. Closing those gaps would most plausibly strengthen conduct-based tools, such as broader coercive-control offences and earlier protection orders, rather than create a category built on a contested label.

Any trait-based law would face three hard problems: unreliable individual prediction, weak evidence that personality-focused treatment works, and a history of costly programmes that were abandoned. The European Court of Human Rights has accepted personality-based detention only with a real disorder, objective expertise and a suitable institution, which sets a high bar. Future debate will likely turn on whether governments fund conduct-based protections and treatment, and on the unresolved commencement and reach of the Mental Health Act 2025, where only one secondary source ([CivilHelp](https://civilhelp.co.uk/mental-health-law/mental-health-act-reform-status)) gives its status.
