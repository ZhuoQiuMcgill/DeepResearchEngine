# Early Academic Failure Drives Engineering Dropout

Academic difficulty is the reason engineering students most often give for leaving, and the first year is when most of them go. In Germany's national series, performance problems were the most important reason for leaving among engineering dropouts, cited by **89% at universities and 86% at universities of applied sciences** in 2017 ([DZHW Forum Hochschule 1/2017](https://www.dzhw.eu/pdf/pub_fh/fh-201701.pdf)). Engineering bachelor dropout at German universities was **32% for the 2020 graduating year**, against 27% for all bachelor's students ([DZHW Brief 03/2020](https://www.dzhw.eu/pdf/pub_brief/dzhw_brief_03_2020.pdf)). In the US, a secondary estimate puts engineering dropout at **40–50%** ([ASEE PEER](https://peer.asee.org/student-dropout-prediction-in-regional-universities-using-automated-machine-learning)), but no official national engineering-only figure was found. Mathematics preparation and first-year course sequencing are the most consistent academic links, and some evidence suggests that teaching structure and belonging also matter. Financial pressure and paid work are reported in several studies, but the evidence on their causal weight is disputed. Retention interventions show promising results, though most evaluations are observational, and no evaluated engineering study measures what leavers themselves say would have kept them enrolled.

## Academic difficulty leads the reasons students give

Engineering dropout has no single agreed definition, and the figures below use different ones. In Germany, a student counts as a dropout if they enrolled at a university but left without a first degree, so students who graduate abroad are counted as dropouts ([arXiv 2110.12777](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.12777)). The UK uses non-continuation, which the Taylor & Francis source notes is not the same as dropout ([Engineering students' educational experiences](https://doi.org/10.1080/13596748.2026.2647621)). In the US, "attrition" often mixes leaving the university with switching majors, and one review states that **less than half** of students who start in engineering graduate in engineering ([Who leaves and who stays?](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/49600715_Who_leaves_and_who_stays_Retention_and_attrition_in_Engineering_Education)).

Across survey and interview studies, the most common reasons are academic. A review of **50 engineering attrition studies** grouped the factors into six broad categories, including classroom climate, grades and conceptual understanding, and high school preparation, and most of the 50 studies used survey data ([Geisinger & Raman, IJEE 2013](https://www.ijee.ie/articles/Vol29-4/14_ijee2746ns.pdf)). In a survey of **624 students at eight Spanish universities**, difficulty, poor academic performance and poor relationships with professors were the main reasons for dropping out, while lack of vocation was less frequent ([Factors Affecting Engineering Students Dropout](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/330523818_Factors_Affecting_Engineering_Students_Dropout_A_Case_Study)). A German DZHW analysis from 2018 concludes that engineering dropout is strongly determined by performance problems, above all insufficient preparation and poor coping with coursework ([DZHW-Projektbericht Oktober 2018](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/328531049_DZHW-Projektbericht_Oktober_2018_Deutsches_Zentrum_fur_Hochschul-und_Wissenschaftsforschung)).

Non-academic reasons appear alongside these. A multi-year single-institution study in the US ranked lack of belonging as a stronger influence than academic factors, though this comes from a secondary summary ([Leaving Engineering](https://www.academia.edu/2360757/Leaving_Engineering_A_Multi_Year_Single_Institution_Study)). A UK study of second-year students at UCL (128 surveys, 15 interviews) identified career perceptions, motivation, expectations, confidence and belonging as the main factors in staying or leaving ([Staying or leaving](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/03043797.2020.1711707)). German data also name motivation: "false expectations about the course content or career profile" appear among motivational reasons ([DZHW slides](https://www.dzhw.eu/pdf/pub_vt/21/2017-02-18_vortrag_studienabbruch_isleib.pdf)), but no engineering-specific share was given.

The academic-versus-motivation debate is unresolved. The review notes a "weeding out" view and reports that ten of the fifty studies found leavers did not have greater conceptual difficulties than stayers, while GPA results remain contradictory ([Geisinger & Raman, IJEE 2013](https://www.ijee.ie/articles/Vol29-4/14_ijee2746ns.pdf)). Most grade-based evidence is predictive, while most motivation evidence comes from leavers' self-reports, which can be unreliable. Because grades can reflect interest as well as ability, the evidence does not settle which is the larger cause. This report treats performance problems as the best-supported primary reason in German and Spanish data, with motivation and belonging as co-existing causes.

## Most leave in the first two semesters, with national figures that do not agree

Germany provides the clearest national series, but its figures differ by method. The DZHW 2020 brief gives **35% for mechanical engineering** and **32% for civil engineering** ([DZHW Brief 03/2020](https://www.dzhw.eu/pdf/pub_brief/dzhw_brief_03_2020.pdf)). A 2022 brief, counting students who delayed graduation as still enrolled, gives **35% at universities and 20% at universities of applied sciences** ([DZHW-Brief 05|2022](https://www.dzhw.eu/pdf/pub_brief/dzhw_brief_05_2022.pdf)). A separate figure from the Federal Statistical Office, reported by a student-advice magazine, gives **about 15%** of students starting between 2018 and 2020 leaving engineering within six semesters ([stuzubi.de](https://stuzubi.de/online-magazin/studiengaenge-mit-den-meisten-studienabbrecherinnen/)). The DZHW and Federal Statistical Office figures use different methods and cohorts, and no source reconciles them, so the German range is roughly 15% to 35% depending on the measure. The DZHW series suggests a broadly flat rate in the 30s over recent editions, though the editions are not continuous.

Timing is consistent across systems. German engineering sources say most dropouts and course changes happen in the first two subject semesters ([acatech POSITION](https://www.acatech.de/publikation/studienabbruch-in-den-ingenieurwissenschaften-hochschuluebergreifende-analyse-und-handlungsempfehlungen/download-pdf/?lang=de_excerpt)), and one civil engineering programme reports up to 40% leaving after the first or second semester ([Ruhr-Universität Bochum](https://news.rub.de/wissenschaft/2019-04-04-lehr-lernforschung-mit-den-richtigen-strategien-studienabbruch-vermeiden)). Across all German fields, **47% of dropouts leave in semesters 1–2** ([studienabbruch-und-weiter.de](https://studienabbruch-und-weiter.de/zahlen-daten-fakten-studienabbruch/)). In the US, a Pittsburgh engineering school reports that about half its engineering attrition falls in the first year ([ASEE PEER](https://peer.asee.org/engineering-attrition-student-characteristics-and-educational-initiatives.pdf)). Longitudinal Louisville data show the largest retention drop in the spring of the first year and high attrition in and just after the second year ([ASEE PEER](https://peer.asee.org/identifying-bottlenecks-in-undergraduate-engineering-mathematics-calculus-i-through-differential-equations.pdf)). No source compares the timing for engineering with other fields in the same dataset, so the data do not show that engineering has a sharper early-exit pattern.

Group differences are less well quantified. One ASEE paper reports **25% attrition for first-generation STEM students against 13% for continuing-generation students**, though this is a secondary citation of one study ([ASEE, Persistence and the Pandemic](https://peer.asee.org/persistence-and-the-pandemic-retention-of-historically-underrepresented-first-year-engineering-students-before-and-after-covid-19.pdf)). A separate ASEE paper claims nearly **70% attrition** for underrepresented groups in engineering, but it gives no national source, so it should be treated as unverified ([Impact of Peer Mentoring](https://www.academia.edu/65529515/Impact_of_Peer_Mentoring_on_Student_Learning_and_Connection_To_Engineering)). In the NCES data, **32% of women** who left STEM switched to another major, against **26% of men**, but this measures switching rather than leaving university ([Inside Higher Ed on NCES](https://www.insidehighered.com/news/2013/11/27/study-tracks-attrition-rates-stem-majors)). No national statistic gives an engineering dropout rate by gender, first-generation status or family income.

## Mathematics, teaching design and money shape the risk

Mathematics is the strongest academic link. At the University of Louisville, completing the four-course mathematics sequence was associated with a **93% graduation rate**, compared with a **68%** first-year retention metric ([IEEE](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/9028542/)). The first mathematics course and first-semester maths grade were strong predictors ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/339796078_Barriers_and_bottlenecks_in_engineering_mathematics_Math_completion_predicts_persistence_to_graduation)). A Spanish study of **3,889 first-year students** across five programmes found that entrance scores, failure rates and exam absence were strongly associated with dropout, while gender was not significant ([IEEE Xplore](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/11150407/)). A Mexican study reports average dropout of **11–19% per semester** and links the highest risk to lower maths grades ([MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-7102/15/3/287/htm)). The effects of physics are less clear. The UK–Norway maths and physics study does not give effect sizes in the retrieved abstract ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/350358794_Proofs_Student_Performance_in_First-Year_Math_and_Physics_Courses_as_Predictor_of_Student_Dropout_in_Engineering_Programs)).

Teaching format is linked to failure more than to leaving. A meta-analysis of **225 STEM studies** found average failure rates of **21.8% under active learning and 33.8% under lecturing**, and students in lecture classes were **1.5 times more likely to fail** ([PNAS, Freeman et al. 2014](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1319030111); [Experts@Minnesota](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/active-learning-increases-student-performance-in-science-engineer/)). The authors note that withdrawal effects are documented but not fully quantified ([Freeman et al. PDF](https://cdn.serc.carleton.edu/files/nagt/profdev/twp/sanbernardino/freeman_et_al._2014.pdf)). The sample is mostly US and mixed STEM, not engineering. A 2025 preprint suggests the amount of active class time matters more than the presence of active elements ([bioRxiv](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.06.01.657285v1.full?utm=)). Curriculum structure is also implicated. German sources cite a formula-heavy curriculum and performance pressure as decisive ([Uni Duisburg-Essen](https://www.uni-due.de/genderportal/naturtechnik_dropout.shtml)), but these come from qualitative or older work. No source compares project-based and lecture-based first years with degree retention as the outcome.

Money is the most disputed link. A survival analysis of term-time employment finds that students working full-time alongside full-time study are less likely to complete, and that working more than about **20 hours a week** carries the same dropout risk as full-time work ([ERIC](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1082237)). A related paper argues that the higher exit rate of non-working students in cross-sectional data may reflect academic difficulty pushing students to stop working, rather than work causing dropout ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/272122514_Working_while_studying_the_impact_of_term-time_employment_on_dropout_rates)). A different analysis reports that **employment had no influence on dropout** and that academic performance is the main predictor, though it is not engineering-specific ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/340876609_Student_Employment_as_a_Possible_Factor_of_Dropout)). The report follows the view that heavy paid work is a risk, because it has the stronger design, but the evidence does not decide the question for engineers: the first study is not engineering-specific either, and the reverse-causality reading is plausible. Financial pressure is reported more widely. A German analysis states that financial problems were a decisive reason for about **one-fifth of all dropouts**, without an engineering breakdown ([DZHW Forum Hochschule](https://www.dzhw.eu/pdf/pub_fh/fh-201002.pdf)). A Brazilian survey found that **71%** of students agreed that working students are more prone to dropout, but these are perceptions, not outcomes ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s44217-025-01092-3)). Mental health is also linked. A civil engineering case study at a private university reports that dropout rose after COVID-19, mainly from mental health problems ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9160475/)). A US deans' brief reports that **50%** of surveyed engineering students screened positive for a major mental health condition, but its sampling is not described ([Cal Poly](https://ceng.calpoly.edu/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/Engineering-Student-Mental-Health-Research-Brief-for-Deans-2.pdf)).

Career prospects do not clearly drive leaving. US salary projections put **$81,198** for engineering bachelor's starting salaries and **$81,535** for computer science for the Class of 2026 ([DegreeCalc, citing NACE](https://degreecalc.com/blog/average-starting-salary-by-major/)). A careers site claims engineering has the highest average starting salary of any bachelor's degree, which conflicts with these figures. The NACE projections are more specific and are preferred here. No study links salary to students' decisions to leave.

## What helps students stay

The strongest design is a field experiment on female peer mentoring. Women who had female mentors showed higher belonging, motivation, confidence, retention in engineering and aspirations than women without mentors, and the effects were still present a year after mentoring ended ([PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1613117114)). Male mentors gave limited benefits. Other evaluations are weaker. A high-risk curriculum at a private research university decoupled first-year physics and calculus and added support, and first-year STEM retention rose from **86.1% to 93.6%** and second-year retention from **76.4% to 85.4%** (p < 0.001). The comparison is between treatment and control groups, and the excerpt does not state randomisation ([Frontiers in Education](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2022.1087064/full)). A randomised belonging intervention with 1,063 students emphasised identity support, especially for first-generation students, but its excerpt reports no retention effect ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40594-025-00530-w)). Bridge programmes show null-to-modest results: one ASEE version reports no significantly higher retention than the college overall ([ASEE](https://peer.asee.org/implementation-and-effects-of-a-bridge-program-to-increase-student-learning-and-retention-in-engineering-programs.pdf)). Mentoring and advising results are largely programme reports. One mentoring programme reports gains of about **3–5 percentage points** ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/271453978_Improving_retention_by_mentoring_and_tutoring_freshmen_students)), and a mechanical engineering advising pilot reports improved four-year retention, though neither has a comparison group that rules out self-selection. The German Acatech/TU9 position calls entrance assessment, self-assessment and integrated support effective, but the claims come from a press release and summaries ([idw-online](https://idw-online.de/de/news685470)).

Evidence on what leavers themselves say would have helped is absent. The exit surveys and reviews name reasons for leaving, not what would have kept students enrolled. The Baden-Württemberg study across all fields finds that study conditions matter for two-thirds of dropouts but are the decisive trigger for fewer than one in ten ([Ministry of Science, Baden-Württemberg](https://mwk.baden-wuerttemberg.de/fileadmin/redaktion/m-mwk/intern/dateien/pdf/Studium_und_Lehre/Studie_Motive_und_Ursachen_des_Studienabbruchs_an_baden-w%C3%BCrttembergischen_Hochschulen_Kurzversion.pdf)). No engineering study tests counselling or mental health services against retention, and no systematic review of engineering retention interventions was found.

## Conclusion

The best-supported answer is that engineering students leave mainly because they struggle academically, especially in mathematics and the first year, and that this struggle is compounded by weak belonging, unclear expectations and, for some, heavy paid work. The national figures are not comparable: German engineering dropout is about one-third at universities on the DZHW measure, but the Federal Statistical Office gives about 15%, and the US figure is a secondary estimate. Any policy response should therefore target the first two semesters, where most exits occur in every system examined.

The evidence is strongest where it is least causal. Predictive studies and German survey series are robust on who leaves, but intervention studies are mostly observational, and nothing tests whether a given support keeps engineering students enrolled to graduation. Missing data include India, the UK engineering dropout trend, and leavers' own views on what would have helped. Closing those gaps would tell institutions whether to invest in mathematics support, belonging programmes or financial help, and in which order.
