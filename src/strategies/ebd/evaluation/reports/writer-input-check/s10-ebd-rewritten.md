# Eugenics Breaks Consent, Equality and Evidence Standards

Eugenics is considered unethical mainly because its coercive forms violate bodily integrity and consent, rest on prejudiced classifications of people, and hand reproductive decisions to the state or to experts. The strongest condemnation covers state-run, involuntary sterilisation. Courts, governments and UN bodies have condemned these programmes, and several governments have since apologised and paid compensation. The ethical verdict is less settled for voluntary selection of embryos. Professional bodies call current polygenic embryo screening unproven and unethical, while some philosophers defend a "liberal" form of eugenics and public approval for health-related screening is substantial. The evidence does not show that most people or scholars reject eugenics as a whole, because no source measures that view directly. The defensible position is therefore narrower: the case against coercive eugenics is strong and well documented, and the case against voluntary selection depends on contested empirical claims about prediction and on contested claims about social pressure.

## What the key terms mean and how they are measured

"Eugenics" is hard to define neutrally. The [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/eugenics/) notes that the term carries strong negative valence because it is tied to forced sterilisation and Nazi mass killing. "Liberal" or "new" eugenics refers to advocates who accept that past eugenic practices were wrong but argue that individual reproductive technologies differ from them ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/eugenics/)). "Polygenic embryo screening" means sorting embryos created through IVF by their genetic profile to predict traits, as offered by several companies ([Live Science opinion](https://www.livescience.com/health/genetics/new-tech-allows-parents-to-score-ivf-embryos-for-desirable-traits-and-its-in-desperate-need-of-regulation-opinion)). "Involuntary sterilisation" is defined by Human Rights Watch as sterilisation carried out without free and informed consent, including when a person is forced or coerced ([HRW briefing, 2011](https://www.hrw.org/sites/default/files/related_material/2011_global_DR.pdf)).

## Core ethical objections

The objections fall into five groups, and each rests on a different kind of reasoning. The first is harm to bodily integrity and dignity. The UN Special Rapporteur on disability described forced sterilisation of girls and young women with disabilities as "systemic violence," carried out "under the guise of 'best interests'" and causing "irreversible harm" ([OHCHR, October 2017](https://www.ohchr.org/en/press-releases/2017/10/forced-sterilization-young-women-disabilities-must-end-un-rights-expert-says)). Japanese survivors reported lasting pain after procedures that included removal of reproductive organs or radiation ([Al Jazeera, April 2019](https://www.aljazeera.com/amp/news/2019/4/24/japans-abe-apologises-to-victims-of-forced-sterilisation)).

The second objection concerns consent. The UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities calls for banning all medical interventions on persons with disabilities without free and informed consent ([OHCHR, A/73/161](https://www.ohchr.org/en/documents/thematic-reports/a73161-report-special-rapporteur-rights-persons-disabilities)). An interagency UN statement from 2014 says no child, including a child with disabilities, should undergo non-therapeutic sterilisation ([UNAIDS, 2014](https://www.unaids.org/sites/default/files/media_asset/201405_sterilization_en.pdf)). The UN Committee on the Rights of the Child has said the "best interests" principle cannot justify practices that conflict with a child's dignity and physical integrity ([HRW letter, 2012](https://www.hrw.org/news/2012/03/09/letter-un-special-rapporteurs-involuntary-sterilization-women-and-girls-disabilities)). Consent is the central safeguard, but the sources show it is weak under pressure. In Japan, about 8,500 people were sterilised with apparent consent, yet lawyers say those cases were likely "de facto forced" ([AFP via Gulf News, July 2024](https://gulfnews.com/world/asia/japans-top-court-rules-forced-sterilisation-law-unconstitutional-1.103405777)).

The third objection is the expressive or social-meaning argument. Disability theorists argue that screening followed by selective termination sends the message that it is better not to exist than to have a disability; this is the "expressivist" argument ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/eugenics/)). Critics add that choosing to remove a trait reinforces prejudice, and that many theorists assume disability is a bad state without adequate evidence from disabled people ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/eugenics/)). This objection is the most contested, because it depends on how flourishing and disability are understood, and no source quantifies the psychological or social harm from non-coerced embryo selection.

The fourth objection is equality. The SEP entry records that racist, sexist, ableist, xenophobic and classist assumptions pervaded early eugenic discourse, and that "feeble-mindedness" was entangled with ideas of race, class and gender ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/eugenics/)). Critics argue that treating race as morally neutral in selection hides the way biases shape choices ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/eugenics/)). The European Society of Human Genetics warned that without oversight, embryo testing with polygenic scores "could easily lead to discrimination and the stigmatization of certain conditions" ([Medical Xpress, January 2022](https://medicalxpress.com/news/2022-01-polygenic-scores-pre-implantation-genetic-unproven.html)).

The fifth objection is epistemic and political. Critics say polygenic prediction is too weak to justify decisions about who may have children. The ESHG (2021) states that the utility of polygenic risk scores for embryo selection is "severely limited" and that no clinical research has assessed their diagnostic effectiveness in embryos ([Forzano et al., 2021](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9090769/)). A 2024 study found inconsistent rankings, with no combination of two methods selecting the same embryo more than half the time ([Medical Xpress, May 2024](https://medicalxpress.com/news/2024-05-polygenic-scores-inaccurate-highly-inconsistent.html)). A 2023 reply in the *European Journal of Human Genetics* argues that the ESHG statement "misrepresents the current scientific consensus" and that polygenic scores can identify higher-risk siblings ([Nature, Tellier et al. reply](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41431-022-01237-0)). The disagreement is partly empirical and partly normative, since ranking embryos by expected well-being requires agreed accounts of well-being ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/eugenics/)).

A further concern is power. Sparrow argues that governments might be obliged to use incentives and campaigns, which would amount to state-sponsored control over reproduction ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/eugenics/)). The Oviedo Explanatory Report links its germline ban to concern that misuse "may endanger not only the individual but also the species itself" ([Journal of Law and the Biosciences, 2020](https://academic.oup.com/jlb/article/7/1/lsaa006/5841599)). The sources show no bioethics commission reasoning specifically on expert authority over who may reproduce.

## The historical record: coercion at scale

The historical record is the strongest reason for the ethical verdict, though the totals vary by source. In the United States, *Buck v. Bell* (1927) upheld Virginia's compulsory sterilisation law by 8–1 ([Encyclopedia Virginia](https://encyclopediavirginia.org/entries/buck-v-bell-1927/)). Estimates of the number sterilised under US laws range from about **36,000 by 1940** ([Institute for Justice](https://ij.org/center-for-judicial-engagement/programs/victims-of-abdication/buck-v-bell-1927/)) to **roughly 60,000** ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Buck-v-Bell)) and **as many as 70,000** ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/sections/health-shots/2016/03/07/469478098/the-supreme-court-ruling-that-led-to-70-000-forced-sterilizations)). The range reflects different years and states counted, and no single federal count was found. *Buck v. Bell* has never been formally overturned, though other Supreme Court rulings hold forced sterilisation unconstitutional ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Buck-v-Bell)). Virginia repealed its law in 1974 and apologised in 2002 ([Encyclopedia Virginia](https://encyclopediavirginia.org/entries/buck-v-bell-1927/)).

Nazi Germany's programme was far larger. The law of July 14, 1933 mandated sterilisation for people identified with nine conditions presumed hereditary ([USHMM](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/eugenics)). Estimates range from **about 360,000** ([EBSCO](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/politics-and-government/nazi-eugenics/)) to **400,000** ([USHMM](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/forced-sterilization-a-form-of-nazi-persecution)). The killing programme (T4 "euthanasia") was grounded in eugenic theory ([USHMM](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/eugenics)). At Nuremberg, the Doctors' Trial ran from 9 December 1946 to 20 August 1947, and sixteen defendants were found guilty ([USHMM](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/the-doctors-trial-the-medical-case-of-the-subsequent-nuremberg-proceedings)). Euthanasia was a tangential issue, with only four of 23 defendants charged with participation in that programme ([Wikipedia, Euthanasia trials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euthanasia_trials)). The Nuremberg Code emerged from the trial's verdict, and its ethics are mainly about consent in experimentation, not sterilisation as such ([Wikipedia, Nuremberg Code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuremberg_Code)). Post-war West Germany never formally abolished the 1933 law, and only the hereditary-court rulings were declared Nazi injustice in 1988 ([AUPress](https://read.aupress.ca/read/psychiatry-and-the-legacies-of-eugenics/section/2245b2ec-330e-4933-94d6-c618024254f5)). None of the 385 mixed-race adolescents sterilised in 1937 received compensation from the German state ([AJPH](https://ajph.aphapublications.org/doi/10.2105/AJPH.2021.306593)).

Other national programmes followed a similar pattern. The table below summarises the main figures, where sources differ, and the later responses.

| Country | Programme and scale | Later response |
|---|---|---|
| Sweden | About 63,000 sterilised, about half forcibly, 1934–1975 ([EDF](https://www.edf-feph.org/sweden-forced-sterilisation/)) | Compensation scheme from 1999 ([Polyphony](https://thepolyphony.org/2025/07/14/who-gets-an-apology/)); one source gives 230,000 victims, which is uncorroborated ([Borneo Post/AFP](https://www.pressreader.com/malaysia/the-borneo-post/20170327/282505773429391)) |
| Canada (Alberta) | 2,834 sterilised from 4,785 cases, 1928–1972 ([University of Toronto](https://exhibits.library.utoronto.ca/exhibits/show/stateinthebedroom/coercedsterilization)) | Apology in November 1999; C$142 million paid in two packages ([CBC](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/alberta-apologizes-for-forced-sterilization-1.169579)) |
| Japan | Eugenic Protection Law, 1948–1996; 16,500 forced sterilisations ([NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/world/japans-top-court-orders-government-compensate-disabled-people-forcibly-rcna160306)) or 25,000 ([DPI-Japan](https://dpi-japan.org/en/2024/10/23/2024-ex-eugenic-protection-act-new-compensation-law/)) | Apology in 2019; law ruled unconstitutional in July 2024; 15 million yen per victim from early 2025 ([Japan Times](https://www.japantimes.co.jp/news/2025/02/10/japan/crime-legal/forced-sterilization-compensation/)) |

The Japanese figures conflict, and the sources differ on which law is meant. A Human Rights Bulletin states that no forced sterilisations were actually carried out under the 1948 law itself ([ScienceOpen](https://www.scienceopen.com/hosted-document?doi=10.13169%2Fintljofdissocjus.5.1.0006)), which contradicts the other estimates; the weight of the sources cited here favours the view that forced sterilisations occurred. The Japanese Supreme Court's 2024 judgment is the most authoritative recent finding on the law's status. The Alberta apology is also instructive: Premier Ralph Klein apologised but said the operations were legal at the time and did not call them criminal ([CBC](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/alberta-apologizes-for-forced-sterilization-1.169579)). Apologies have thus differed in how far they accept legal responsibility. A single secondary report says victims in Peru are still seeking compensation after 370,000 people were sterilised ([Yahoo](https://www.yahoo.com/news/japan-starts-compensation-payments-forced-070000085.html)); no UN document was checked, so this claim is thin.

## Defences of liberal eugenics and the modern selection dispute

Defenders argue that the old eugenics was coercive and state-run, while voluntary parental choice aims at the child's own good and differs in kind. Julian Savulescu's procreative beneficence holds that parents should choose, among possible children, the one whose life is expected to go best ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2016/entries/eugenics/)). Buchanan, Brock, Daniels and Wikler do not accept disability-rights objections to genetic testing, but they warn of "backdoor eugenics" and argue that entrenched reproductive rights make a return to state eugenics highly unlikely ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC80823/)). Critics reply that individual choices, shaped by unjust social norms, can add up to eugenic outcomes, and that the means of choosing traits are not morally neutral ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/sum2017/entries/eugenics/)). Michael Sandel argues that genetic design conflicts with "openness to the unbidden" and with treating children as gifts ([SuperSummary](https://www.supersummary.com/the-case-against-perfection/summary/)). Sparrow argues that liberal eugenics' consequentialist justifications sit uneasily with its promise to preserve reproductive liberty ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/eugenics/)). The dispute turns on the distinction between state coercion and private choice, and critics' replies target exactly that distinction.

Companies now sell this selection. Genomic Prediction, Orchid, Herasight and Nucleus offer embryo selection, and at least two, Nucleus and Herasight, offer testing for intelligence ([Live Science opinion](https://www.livescience.com/health/genetics/new-tech-allows-parents-to-score-ivf-embryos-for-desirable-traits-and-its-in-desperate-need-of-regulation-opinion)). The American Society for Reproductive Medicine's Ethics and Practice Committees concluded that polygenic embryo screening "is not ready for clinical use" and should not be offered as a reproductive service at this time ([ASRM](https://www.asrm.org/news-and-events/asrm-news/press-releasesbulletins/asrm-ethics-and-practice-committees-release-new-report-concluding-polygenic-embryo-screening-is-not-ready-for-clinical-use/)). The companies say their scores are state of the art and carefully validated ([NPR transcript](https://kvcr.org/news/npr-top-stories/2026-05-06/genetic-embryo-screening)). A Herasight company blog argues that the relevant test is sibling discrimination, not population prediction, and that predictive power drops on that harder test for some traits ([Herasight](https://www.herasight.com/blog/embryo-screening-myths)). No independent study in the sources measures within-family accuracy for any trait.

Public opinion is divided, and the sources disagree on what it shows. A US survey published in JAMA Network Open found that **72.0% approved** of polygenic embryo screening overall, but only **36.0%** approved for behavioural traits ([JAMA Network Open](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2818674)); the same 72% figure is reported by [News-Medical](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20240515/Understanding-public-perception-of-polygenic-embryo-screening-for-IVF.aspx) and a [medRxiv preprint](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2023.10.13.23297022.full.pdf). A 2026 Oxford-led study of more than 2,000 people in the US and China found that **61%** were open to testing for non-medical traits, which the authors describe as higher than earlier work suggested ([EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1141221); [NewsBreak](https://www.newsbreak.com/psypost-332799984/4887881691969-how-people-really-pick-ivf-embryos-isn-t-what-they-claim)). Against the view that most people reject eugenics, the 2024 survey's authors reported that a majority approved of screening while also voicing strong concerns about sliding into eugenics ([EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1044330)). The report follows the narrower claim: the polls measure approval of embryo screening, not opinion on eugenics as such, and their answers shift with framing. The evidence therefore does not decide whether most people consider eugenics unethical.

Regulation is uneven. The Oviedo Convention bans heritable genome modification (Article 13) and sex selection for non-medical reasons (Article 14), and it does not name polygenic trait selection ([WorldLII](https://www.worldlii.org/int/other/COETS/1997/1.html)). The UK bans non-medical sex selection by statute ([Wikipedia, HFE Act 2008](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_Fertilisation_and_Embryology_Act_2008)). Polygenic embryo screening "is largely unregulated in the United States" ([ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2024/05/240514141244.htm)). The Oviedo reading is contested: Beriain and colleagues argue that Article 13 bans only clinical germline editing and not research ([SAGE, 2019](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0968533219862590)), and the Council of Europe's bioethics committee in 2021 said the article did not need modification though its meaning needed clarifying ([Genetics and Society](https://www.geneticsandsociety.org/biopolitical-times/european-convention-continues-ban-germline-editing)).

## Conclusion

The ethical case against eugenics is strongest where it is most documented: coerced, state-run and non-consensual sterilisation. Its victims, the courts and governments that have revisited these programmes, and the UN human-rights framework all agree on that. The reasons given, bodily integrity, consent, equality and the danger of state or expert control over reproduction, explain why. They also show that the historical verdict and the verdict on voluntary selection are different questions.

The harder question is whether voluntary selection of embryos is eugenic in the morally relevant sense. The evidence supports caution rather than a settled answer. Professional bodies judge current polygenic screening unproven, and the accuracy disputes are unresolved, with no clinical trial data. Public approval for health conditions is high, and interest in non-medical traits is substantial but depends on framing. Any policy should therefore separate the strongest findings from the contested ones, and should not assume that consent alone answers the concern about social pressure.
