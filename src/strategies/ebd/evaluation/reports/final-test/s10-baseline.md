# Why Eugenics Violates Consent, Equality and Dignity

Eugenics is considered unethical for three linked reasons: it overrides the autonomy of the people it touches, it ranks human lives by perceived worth, and it has been carried out through coercion that killed and sterilised people on the basis of weak science. Philosophers such as Habermas and Sandel object to prenatal design on the grounds that it threatens the future person's self-definition and the gift-like character of life, and disability critics argue that it expresses social prejudice ([SEP, Eugenics](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2026/entries/eugenics/)). The historical record gives these principles concrete content: **roughly 60,000 Americans** were sterilised across **32 states** ([Britannica, Buck v. Bell](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Buck-v-Bell)), and about **63,000 Swedes**, mostly women, were sterilised between 1934 and the 1970s ([European Disability Forum](https://www.edf-feph.org/sweden-forced-sterilisation/)). Nazi Germany's programme reached about **400,000 sterilisation procedures** ([USHMM, Deadly Medicine](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/deadly-medicine-creating-the-master-race)) and was extended to murder under Aktion T4. Current debate centres less on state programmes, which have been repudiated, than on polygenic embryo selection, where defenders stress individual choice and critics stress unreliable prediction and social harm. Professional bodies such as the European Society of Human Genetics now call that practice unproven and unethical, although the evidence and the regulatory position remain contested.

## Moral principles: autonomy, equality and the meaning of a life

The core objections rest on a small set of principles. Habermas argues that the morality of human rights and equality depends on a shared human nature, and that prenatal design threatens the future person's capacity for self-definition and sense of responsibility for their own identity ([Sjdr, The Future of Human Nature](https://sjdr.se/articles/10.1080/15017410500246111); [SEP, Eugenics](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2026/entries/eugenics/)). A person who learns that their genetic makeup was programmed, he suggests, may feel less free and less authentic ([Notre Dame Philosophical Reviews](https://ndpr.nd.edu/reviews/the-future-of-human-nature/)). Sandel's objection is different in kind. He argues that enhancement expresses a "drive to mastery" and a failure to appreciate the gifted character of human powers, and that the disposition it reveals is objectionable even where it is safe and fair ([Harvard WCFIA](https://www.wcfia.harvard.edu/publications/case-against-perfection-ethics-age-genetic-engineering)). He also argues that a parent's wish for a genetically chosen child conflicts with the unconditional love parents should have ([Wikipedia, Bioconservatism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bioconservatism)).

A second line of argument is about justice and non-discrimination. Buchanan, Brock, Daniels and Wikler treat genetics as raising questions of distributive justice, equality of opportunity and the role of human nature in ethics ([PhilPapers, From Chance to Choice](https://philpapers.org/rec/BUCFCT-3)). Critics such as Sparrow and Asch argue that even liberal eugenics can reinforce social prejudice against people with disabilities ([SEP, Eugenics](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2026/entries/eugenics/)). The disability-rights "expressivist" objection holds that screening followed by selective abortion sends a negative message about existing people with disabilities. Defenders reply that one can value the person while devaluing the disability, but critics respond that this separation is hard to sustain for people who experience disability as central to their identity ([SEP, Eugenics](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2026/entries/eugenics/)).

Bodily autonomy and physical integrity also feature in the case. Sterilisation guidance argues that coerced sterilisation violates respect for autonomy and physical integrity, beneficence and non-maleficence ([UNAIDS](https://www.unaids.org/sites/default/files/media_asset/201405_sterilization_en.pdf)). The sources retrieved do not give a systematic philosophical account of "equal moral worth" or "human dignity" specific to eugenics; these concepts appear mainly as background in the Habermas and disability critiques.

## The historical record: scale, targets and harms

The historical abuses make the principles concrete, although the figures vary by source and the differences matter. The table below sets out the main estimates.

|---|---|---|

Bell](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Buck-v-Bell); a 2002 LifeSite report gives about 65,000 across 30 states ([LifeSite](https://www.lifesitenews.com/news/virginia-governor-apologizes-for-states-use-of-sterilization/)) |
| California sterilisations | More than 20,000, 1909–1979 | [California AB-3052 bill text](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billTextClient.xhtml?bill_id=201920200AB3052) |
| Virginia sterilisations | About 7,500 (1924–1979) | [Senate press release](https://www.warner.senate.gov/public/index.cfm/2016/9/warner-kaine-bill-to-protect-benefits-for-eugenics-victims-heads-to-president-s-desk); other sources give about 8,000 ([Encyclopedia Virginia](https://encyclopediavirginia.org/entries/eugenic-sterilization-in-virginia/)) and about 8,300 for 1927–1972 ([Encyclopedia Virginia](https://encyclopediavirginia.org/entries/buck-v-bell-1927/)) |
| Sweden sterilisations | About 63,000, mainly women | [European Disability Forum](https://www.edf-feph.org/sweden-forced-sterilisation/); another source gives 62,000 for 1934–1976 ([WSWS](https://www.wsws.org/en/articles/1999/03/euge-19m.html)) |
| Nazi sterilisations | About 400,000 | [USHMM, Deadly Medicine](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/deadly-medicine-creating-the-master-race); a Nuremberg-era overview cites more than 350,000 ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/social-sciences/nuremberg-trials)) |
| Aktion T4 murders | Roughly 250,000 per USHMM; about 300,000 in one Wikidata entry | [USHMM, Euthanasia Program](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/euthanasia-program); [Wikidata](https://www.wikidata.org/wiki/Q154494) |

The discrepancies reflect record gaps, different date ranges and whether extra-legal acts are counted. The Aktion T4 range is particularly wide: the USHMM notes that T4's own internal calculations counted 70,273 victims at six gassing facilities between January 1940 and August 1941 ([USHMM, Euthanasia Program](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/euthanasia-program)), while Wikipedia gives about 200,000 in Germany and Austria and about 100,000 in other European countries ([Wikipedia, Aktion T4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aktion_T4)). No single authoritative global total was found, so ranges are the honest figure.

The harms went beyond the surgery. In US institutions, medical superintendents could authorise sterilisation without patient consent, and sterilisation was sometimes a prerequisite for release ([AUA abstract](https://www.auajournals.org/doi/10.1097/01.JU.0001008828.35887.de.02)). In Sweden, officials reportedly threatened people with the removal of their children or denial of abortions if they refused to sign an application, and orphans were sterilised as a condition of release from children's homes ([The Militant](https://www.themilitant.com/1997/6133/6133_18.html); [WSWS](https://www.wsws.org/en/articles/1999/03/euge-19m.html)). The targets were elastic. The 1914 model law by Harry Laughlin proposed sterilising the "socially inadequate," including people maintained at public expense ([Social Origins of Eugenics](https://www.eugenicsarchive.org/html/eugenics/essay8text.html)). In Nazi Germany, the regime forcibly sterilised people with disabilities, Roma, and Black and multiracial people ([USHMM, Forced Sterilization](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/forced-sterilization-a-form-of-nazi-persecution)). Because categories such as "feeble-minded" and "dependent" overlapped with poverty and ethnicity, the same law could be applied to disabled people, poor women and ethnic minorities alike.

## Coercion and consent: the contested line between voluntary and coerced

The clearest historical distinction is between authoritarian programmes and liberal eugenics. The SEP describes authoritarian eugenics as coercive state programmes, while liberal eugenics rests on prospective parents' free choice and value pluralism ([SEP, Eugenics](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2026/entries/eugenics/)). Critics argue that this distinction does not settle the ethical question. A Harvard Law Review note argues that voluntariness is not the key to whether an intervention is permissible, and that the goal matters more ([Harvard Law Review, Regulating Eugenics](https://harvardlawreview.org/wp-content/uploads/2008/03/regulating_eugenics.pdf)). The same note warns that the real danger is that the state can use eugenics to create the citizens it wants to govern ([Harvard Law Review](https://harvardlawreview.org/wp-content/uploads/2008/03/regulating_eugenics.pdf)). Sparrow argues that governments might pursue non-coercive pressure through incentive systems or targeted campaigns ([SEP, Eugenics](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2026/entries/eugenics/)).

The more recent critique concerns whether consent is free under unequal conditions. Legal commentary holds that consent obtained through threats of economic deprivation cannot be treated as voluntary ([Missouri Law Review](https://scholarship.law.missouri.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=3999&context=mlr)). A 2025 review identifies "contingency-based" and "constrained-market" consent as forms of coercion that can occur even when a patient signs a form ([PMC, Contemporary human rights violations in female sterilization care](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12615512/)). This shifts the debate from state versus individual to the social setting in which choices are made. The sterilisation sources concern contraceptive and institutional sterilisation rather than genetic selection, so no source tests the constrained-market framework directly against embryo selection. The Harvard Law Review note is from 2008, and the sources found do not show whether the coercion critique has changed since.

## Scientific critiques and their ethical weight

Scientific objections matter to the ethical case because eugenic programmes rested on claims about heredity. The SEP notes that authoritarian programmes depended on problematic assumptions about heritability, and that early eugenics treated social conditions such as poverty as inherited traits ([SEP, Eugenics](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2026/entries/eugenics/)). Critics argue that a heritability estimate does not indicate how fixed a trait is ([ResearchGate, The heritability fallacy](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/311347996_The_heritability_fallacy)), and that heritability within groups cannot establish differences between groups ([Debunking Denialism](https://debunkingdenialism.com/2012/08/11/the-widespread-abuse-of-heritability/)).

The critique of polygenic embryo selection is more directly relevant to today's debate. A 2019 *Cell* paper concludes that polygenic embryo screening has limited utility, because scores need enough predictive power to separate embryos within one family's restricted genetic variance ([Cell](https://www.cell.com/cell/fulltext/S0092-8674(19)31210-3)). A NEJM perspective argues that polygenic scores do not capture all genetic variation and can mislead through genetic-determinism expectations, and it calls selection for educational attainment or intelligence deeply concerning given historical eugenic policies ([NEJM](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMsr2105065)). A 2026 *Frontiers* paper argues that selection predicts less for low-heritability, environmentally shaped traits and recommends that professional societies consider prohibiting it ([Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/reproductive-health/articles/10.3389/frph.2026.1771127/full)). Against this, *Nature* (2024) argues that polygenic embryo selection achieves only limited change in one generation, though heritable polygenic editing would have much larger predicted effects ([Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-08300-4)). Savulescu and Kahane argue that critics overstate their case, because parents already make such comparisons ([SEP, Eugenics](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2026/entries/eugenics/)).

These findings support the ethical case without settling it. Weak prediction undermines the claim that selection serves the child's welfare, and it strengthens the argument that individual choices are poorly informed. But, as the notes acknowledge, unreliability alone would not prove that selection is wrong. The stronger ethical point is that the category of "fitness" is value-laden: Bennett argues that the obligation to bring the "best" children to birth implies a lower moral value for disabled lives ([SEP, Eugenics](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2026/entries/eugenics/)). That is a philosophical claim rather than an empirical one, and the scientific and ethical objections reinforce each other.

## Formal law and professional positions

International law gives the prohibition its clearest textual form, though not uniformly. The EU Charter, Article 3(2)(b), requires respect for "the prohibition of eugenic practices, in particular those aiming at the selection of persons" in medicine and biology ([EUR-Lex, Charter](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/PDF/?uri=CELEX%3A12016P%2FTXT)). Its official explanation links this to organised selection programmes, including campaigns for sterilisation and forced pregnancy, which it treats as international crimes under the Rome Statute ([FRA, EU Charter Article 3](https://fra.europa.eu/en/eu-charter/article/3-right-integrity-person)). The Oviedo Convention takes an indirect route. Its Article 13 allows modification of the human genome only for preventive, diagnostic or therapeutic purposes, and only if the aim is not to modify the genome of descendants ([Council of Europe](https://rm.coe.int/1680475c91)). Its explanatory material says the ban responds to fears that genetic progress could endanger "not only the individual but the species itself" ([EMBO Reports commentary](https://link.springer.com/article/10.15252/embr.201745378)). The UNESCO Universal Declaration on the Human Genome (1997) is non-binding, and it protects dignity and bars discrimination based on genetic characteristics, but the retrieved excerpts do not name eugenics ([Global Health Rights](https://www.globalhealthrights.org/instrument/universal-declaration-on-the-human-genome-and-human-rights/)). The sources retrieved do not establish whether the Universal Declaration of Human Rights text mentions eugenics. The Oviedo ratification count also conflicts: one source says 29 countries ([Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/genetics/articles/10.3389/fgene.2019.00532/xml/nlm)), while a 2023 PACE report refers to 30 ([PACE Doc. 15855](https://rm.coe.int/heritable-genome-editing-in-human-beings/1680aca15c)).

Professional bodies are more explicit about selection. The American Society of Human Genetics "unequivocally declares its opposition" to coercive restriction of reproduction based on misuse of genetic information ([ASHG](https://www.ashg.org/advocacy/statement-archive/opposing-eugenics-and-restrictions-on-reproductive-freedom/)). The European Society of Human Genetics calls polygenic risk-score embryo testing "unproven, unethical" and warns of discrimination and stigmatisation ([ESHG](https://www.eshg.org/news/news-details?tx_news_pi1%5Baction%5D=detail&tx_news_pi1%5Bcontroller%5D=News&tx_news_pi1%5Bnews%5D=35&cHash=8fd89e148a48845e18ef43df338ecb5f)), and ESHRE says clinical utility is "low to non-existent" ([ESHRE](https://www.eshre.eu/Europe/Position-statements/PRS)). Scientists have pushed back, arguing the ESHG statement "misrepresents the current scientific consensus" ([Nature/EJHG correspondence](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41431-022-01237-0)). The sources disagree on the American Society for Reproductive Medicine's position. One report describes its 2026 ethics opinion as saying PGT for polygenic disorders should not be used for trait selection ([ASRM](https://www.asrm.org/practice-guidance/ethics-opinions/use-of-preimplantation-genetic-testing-for-polygenic-disorders-pgt-p-an-ethics-committee-opinion-2026/)), but the retrieved excerpts did not include its final recommendation, so that position should be treated as provisional. The WHO takes a governance rather than prohibitive approach, describing heritable editing as "generally considered to be of greater ethical concern" than somatic editing ([WHO](https://www.who.int/teams/health-ethics-governance/emerging-technologies/human-genome-editing)). The UK Nuffield Council's 2024 horizon scan states that polygenic embryo screening is "currently illegal in the UK" ([Nuffield Council](https://cdn.nuffieldbioethics.org/wp-content/uploads/2024-Horizon-Scan-Methods-and-Evidence-Pack.pdf)). In the US, by contrast, polygenic embryo screening is "commercially available… and unregulated as to what types of polygenic outcomes can be screened" ([medRxiv](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2023.10.13.23297022.full.pdf)).

Legal reform has repudiated the programmes more than it has defined a stand-alone eugenics offence. Virginia repealed its law in 1974 and apologised in 2002 ([Encyclopedia Virginia](https://encyclopediavirginia.org/entries/buck-v-bell-1927/)); its governor described the effort as one "in which state government never should have been involved" ([Encyclopedia Virginia](https://encyclopediavirginia.org/entries/eugenic-sterilization-in-virginia/)). Lombardo counts at least sixteen state statutes declared unconstitutional or repealed between 1965 and 1979 ([Georgia State Law Review](https://readingroom.law.gsu.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=2422&context=gsulr)). The last US state repeal was Mississippi in 2008 ([Georgia State University Library](https://exhibits.library.gsu.edu/health-is-a-human-right/rights-denied/forced-sterilizations/)). *Buck v. Bell* was never formally overturned, though its reasoning has been undermined by *Skinner v. Oklahoma* ([Disability Justice](https://disabilityjustice.org/basic-legal-rights/right-to-self-determination-freedom-from-involuntary-sterilization/)). Japan's Eugenic Protection Law authorised forced sterilisation until 1996, and the Supreme Court affirmed the former law was unconstitutional ([Wikipedia, Yumi Suzuki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yumi_Suzuki_(plaintiff))), a secondary account. Current US law mainly relies on consent and procedural safeguards: federally funded sterilisation has required a standard consent form and typically a 30-day waiting period since 1979 ([AMA Journal of Ethics](https://journalofethics.ama-assn.org/article/federal-sterilization-policy-unintended-consequences/2012-02)).

## Defenders' arguments and their critics

Defenders of voluntary selection make several arguments that critics must answer. They argue that current technologies rest on sound science, unlike past programmes built on views of race and intelligence that were "hopelessly wrong" ([SEP, Eugenics](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2026/entries/eugenics/)). They argue that parents already shape children through education and sport, so selection is not morally distinct. Buchanan compares enhancement to literacy and expects costs to fall over time ([SEP, Eugenics](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2026/entries/eugenics/)). Savulescu argues for "procreative beneficence," the obligation to select the child with the best chance at a good life, while opposing legal coercion of parents ([SEP, Eugenics](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2026/entries/eugenics/)).

Critics reply that education engages the child communicatively and addresses the whole person, while selection targets a particular trait ([SEP, Eugenics](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2026/entries/eugenics/)). They note that IVF has remained expensive decades after its introduction ([SEP, Eugenics](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2026/entries/eugenics/)). Sparrow argues that Savulescu's consequentialist justification sits in tension with preserving individual reproductive liberty ([SEP, Eugenics](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2026/entries/eugenics/)). Heritable editing is contested even among bodies. The Nuffield Council concluded in 2018 that heritable editing "could be morally permissible" ([Nuffield Foundation](https://www.nuffieldfoundation.org/news/heritable-genome-editing-action-needed-to-secure-responsible-way-forward)), but critics argue its framing presumes therapeutic efficacy and that reproductive editing cannot be therapeutic where there is no sick patient ([Genetics & Society](https://www.geneticsandsociety.org/biopolitical-times/uks-nuffield-council-releases-report-human-genome-editing)). Surveys show public approval of polygenic screening for health conditions at 72–77%, but for traits at only 30–36%, with eugenics among the main concerns ([Human Reproduction Update](https://academic.oup.com/humupd/article/30/5/529/7684172)).

## Conclusion

Eugenics is considered unethical because it treats people's reproductive lives as objects of social design, and because the programmes that followed produced documented coercion, sterilisation and murder. The principles of consent, equal worth and non-discrimination explain the wrong, while the historical record shows its practical cost. The record is also clear that coercion does not require a state decree: consent obtained under economic or institutional pressure has been treated as no consent at all.

The live question is narrower. Few now defend state-run programmes, but polygenic embryo selection tests whether a prohibition can rest on individual choice. The scientific case against current selection is strong on predictive weakness, and the professional bodies are divided on how far to go. The ethical case against trait selection is more contested, because it depends on how one values disability and whether parental choice can be separated from social effects. Readers should treat the historical figures as ranges, and should treat the current regulatory picture, particularly for the US and the ASRM position, as unsettled.
