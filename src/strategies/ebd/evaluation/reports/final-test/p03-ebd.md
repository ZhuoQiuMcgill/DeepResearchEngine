# Everest stands at 8,848.86 metres, a snow-surface height

Mount Everest's latest accepted height is **8,848.86 m (29,031.7 ft)**, announced jointly by Nepal and China on **8 December 2020** ([Kathmandu Post](https://kathmandupost.com/national/2020/12/08/it-s-official-mount-everest-is-8-848-86-metres-tall)). The figure is an orthometric height referred to the International Height Reference System (IHRS) geoid, and the Survey Department paper describes it as a snow-surface height ([Journal on Geoinformatics, Nepal](https://www.nepjol.info/index.php/NJG/article/download/39479/30219/114898)). No official revision after 2020 appears in the sources, so this remains the current accepted value. The figure is about 0.86 m above the 8,848 m long used by Nepal ([Gulf News](https://gulfnews.com/world/asia/everest-grows-as-china-nepal-agree-on-new-height-1.1607424747749)). The most important caveat is that the joint announcement did not publish a rock-surface height, so the familiar 8,844.43 m figure is a 2005 Chinese value, not a 2020 one ([Kathmandu Post](https://kathmandupost.com/national/2020/12/08/it-s-official-mount-everest-is-8-848-86-metres-tall)). No numerical uncertainty has been published either; officials describe accuracy only as "within centimetres" ([Kathmandu Post](https://kathmandupost.com/national/2020/12/08/it-s-official-mount-everest-is-8-848-86-metres-tall)).

## The 2020 joint survey set the current figure

The 2020 value came from two separate efforts coordinated at government level rather than a single joint field campaign. Nepal's team began fieldwork in early 2018 and finished in 2019, and its members reached the summit on 22 May 2019 to install GNSS and ground-penetrating radar equipment ([Journal on Geoinformatics, Nepal](https://www.nepjol.info/index.php/NJG/article/download/39479/30219/114898); [Kathmandu Post](https://kathmandupost.com/national/2020/12/08/it-s-official-mount-everest-is-8-848-86-metres-tall)). China's team reached the top on 27 May 2020 ([Kathmandu Post](https://kathmandupost.com/national/2020/12/08/it-s-official-mount-everest-is-8-848-86-metres-tall)). The technical team submitted its result on 12 October 2020, the Steering Committee approved it on 18 October, and Nepal's Council of Ministers approved the joint announcement on 25 November 2020 ([Journal on Geoinformatics, Nepal](https://www.nepjol.info/index.php/NJG/article/download/39479/30219/114898)). The methods combined trigonometric levelling, precise levelling, GNSS and gravity surveys, with airborne gravity data merged with surface gravity to build the geoid ([Journal on Geoinformatics, Nepal](https://www.nepjol.info/index.php/NJG/article/download/39479/30219/114898)). The precise levelling ran **248 km** from a datum station at Maadar in Siraha district ([Journal on Geoinformatics, Nepal](https://www.nepjol.info/index.php/NJG/article/download/39479/30219/114898)).

## Snow versus rock height remains the main dispute

The sources disagree on which surface the 2020 figure describes. The Survey Department paper gives 8848.86 m as the final height and, in its discussion of earlier values, treats it as the snow height ([Journal on Geoinformatics, Nepal](https://www.nepjol.info/index.php/NJG/article/download/39479/30219/114898)). A Nepal Intrepid Treks page reports the same figure as the rock surface corrected to the geoid, while also citing the paper as giving it as the snow height ([Nepal Intrepid Treks](https://www.nepalintrepidtreks.com/mount-everest-growth-every-year)). This report follows the primary paper and the Kathmandu Post, which says no rock height was announced, so the figure is treated as a snow height. The evidence does not settle the question fully, because the snow depth used in the 2020 calculation was never published. Several travel sites also say China has recognised 8,844.43 m as a rock height "since December 2020" ([Index Adventure](https://indexadventure.com/blog/mount-everest-height-in-feet-meters-and-km)), but this conflicts with the Kathmandu Post, and no official 2020 rock height has been found.

The snow depth itself is contested. A 2023 study reports a mean summit snow depth of about **9.5 m ± 1.2 m** from radar, far deeper than earlier values of 0.9–3.5 m, and it notes that the 2019–2020 teams used different radar instruments and that "no results were reported" ([The Cryosphere](https://tc.copernicus.org/articles/17/2625/2023/)). If that deeper snowpack is right, a rock height derived from the 2020 data would differ substantially from the snow height, so the two readings of the 2020 figure cannot be reconciled from the available sources. Secondary sites quote much smaller values, such as 92 cm, but the primary sources reviewed do not confirm them.

## Uncertainty and datum limit how precise the figure is

The height is an orthometric height, derived from the summit's GNSS ellipsoidal height and a precise geoid, and the IHRS is the common datum for Nepal and China ([Journal on Geoinformatics, Nepal](https://www.nepjol.info/index.php/NJG/article/download/39479/30219/114898)). The Nepal Survey's national coordinator, Dangol, said the summit GNSS observation lasted 1 hour 16 minutes and that the margin of error is "within centimetres" ([Kathmandu Post](https://kathmandupost.com/national/2020/12/08/it-s-official-mount-everest-is-8-848-86-metres-tall)). That qualitative claim is the only accuracy statement found, so the figure should be quoted to the centimetre only as a reported value, not as a measured precision. Older Indian figures referred to Bay of Bengal sea level ([Irish Seven Summits](https://irishsevensummits.com/everest-new-height-announced/)), so comparisons across eras mix different reference surfaces. No source gives the summit's ellipsoidal height or the geoid undulation there, so the datum choice cannot be checked independently.

## Earlier measurements explain the older figures

The older figures differ mainly because of method, datum and the snow-versus-rock choice, not because the mountain changed. The table summarises the main values.

| Survey | Height reported | Surface | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Survey of India, 1954 | 8,848 m | Not stated in source | [Kathmandu Post](https://kathmandupost.com/national/2020/12/08/it-s-official-mount-everest-is-8-848-86-metres-tall) |
| Chinese survey, 1975 | 8,848.13 m | Not stated in source | [Journal on Geoinformatics, Nepal](https://www.nepjol.info/index.php/NJG/article/download/39479/30219/114898) |
| Italian survey, 1992 | 8,848.65 m | Snow | [Journal on Geoinformatics, Nepal](https://www.nepjol.info/index.php/NJG/article/download/39479/30219/114898) |
| US-sponsored survey, 1999 | 8,850 m | Not stated in source | [Kathmandu Post](https://kathmandupost.com/national/2020/12/08/it-s-official-mount-everest-is-8-848-86-metres-tall) |
| Chinese survey, 2005 | 8,844.43 m | Rock | [Kathmandu Post](https://kathmandupost.com/national/2020/12/08/it-s-official-mount-everest-is-8-848-86-metres-tall) |
| Joint announcement, 2020 | 8,848.86 m | Snow (per primary paper) | [Journal on Geoinformatics, Nepal](https://www.nepjol.info/index.php/NJG/article/download/39479/30219/114898) |

The 2005 Chinese figure is about 4.4 m below the 2020 value, which partly reflects the snow-versus-rock difference and partly different geoid models ([Journal on Geoinformatics, Nepal](https://www.nepjol.info/index.php/NJG/article/download/39479/30219/114898)). Nepal and the world had used the 1954 value until the 2015 earthquake prompted Nepal to re-measure, and some geologists speculated the quake had changed the height ([Journal on Geoinformatics, Nepal](https://www.nepjol.info/index.php/NJG/article/download/39479/30219/114898); [Kathmandu Post](https://kathmandupost.com/national/2020/12/08/it-s-official-mount-everest-is-8-848-86-metres-tall)). The 2020 survey did not test that speculation directly, so the evidence does not show whether the earthquake changed the height.

## Conclusion

For a practical answer, Everest is **8,848.86 m (29,031.7 ft)** above sea level, as jointly announced in December 2020 ([Kathmandu Post](https://kathmandupost.com/national/2020/12/08/it-s-official-mount-everest-is-8-848-86-metres-tall)). That is the figure to use in most contexts, and it is the most authoritative value in the sources. Users who need a rock-surface height should not assume the 2020 announcement provides one, since the only rock figure is from 2005.

The main open issues are the snow depth and the surface reference. Until an official source publishes the snow depth used in the 2020 calculation, or a rock height from the same data, the gap between snow and rock heights and the 2023 radar finding of deeper snow will remain unresolved. Any future revision would likely come from a published uncertainty analysis or a new official survey, and no such source exists as of 2026-11-20.
