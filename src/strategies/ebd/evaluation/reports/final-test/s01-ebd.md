# Tight Funding Has Stretched NHS England Since 2010

Funding for the NHS in England has grown in real terms since 2010, but far more slowly than before, and the slowdown has left the service with longer waits, tighter capacity and a stretched workforce. **Real-terms growth averaged 2.8% a year between 2015/16 and 2022/23**, against a long-run average of 3.6% ([King's Fund](https://www.kingsfund.org.uk/insight-and-analysis/data-and-charts/nhs-budget-nutshell)). The **waiting list reached a record 7.7 million in September 2023** and stood at 7.38 million at the end of September 2026 ([Going Private UK](https://goingprivateuk.co.uk/nhs-waiting-list-september-2026/); [House of Commons Library](https://commonslibrary.parliament.uk/research-briefings/cbp-7281/)). The evidence links these pressures to funding, but it does not isolate funding from demand, the pandemic and productivity. Funding has been tight rather than absent, and the 2025 Spending Review settlement is more generous than most departments' but still below the long-run average ([NHS Confederation](https://www.nhsconfed.org/publications/spending-review-2025); [HFMA](https://www.hfma.org.uk/articles/spending-review-2025-sense-perspective)). Official data show a clear fall in performance, but no source establishes a direct causal chain from individual funding decisions to individual outcomes. Most analysts therefore treat funding as one major cause among several.

## Real-terms growth slowed, then surged and faded

Funding decisions since 2010 produced a sequence of distinct settlements. The 2010 Spending Review set a **0.4% real-terms increase over four years**, with the budget rising from £104bn to £114bn ([HM Treasury](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/203826/Spending_review_2010.pdf)). The Long Term Plan, set out after the June 2018 settlement, added **£33.9bn in cash terms by 2023/24**, an average real increase of **3.4% a year** ([House of Commons Public Accounts Committee](https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/cm5801/cmselect/cmpubacc/344/34404.htm)). Actual growth fell short of that plan. The IFS says the NHS budget rose less than planned even as pandemic funding was added, and that holding spending flat in real terms is unlikely to be sustainable ([IFS](https://ifs.org.uk/news/nhs-spending-has-risen-less-quickly-was-planned-last-election-despite-pandemic-and-record)).

| 2025 | Spending Review 2025 | About 3% a year real-terms growth in day-to-day spending | [Health Foundation](https://www.health.org.uk/reports-and-analysis/analysis/what-does-the-2025-spending-review-mean-for-the-nhs) |
| 2026 | Autumn Budget 2026 (28 October) | £190m extra for NHS England in winter 2026/27 | [Carnall Farrar](https://www.carnallfarrar.com/insights-uk-budget-2026-nhs/) |

The figures are not directly comparable. The 2025 settlement is reported in different terms: the Health Foundation gives **£18.5bn** of extra day-to-day spending by 2028/29, while HFMA gives an NHS England budget rising from **£195.6bn to £226.1bn** in the final year of the review, and the NHS Alliance gives a revenue budget reaching **£232bn** by 2028/29 ([HFMA](https://www.hfma.org.uk/articles/nhs-prioritised-spending-review-leaders-remain-concerned); [NHS Alliance](https://thenhsalliance.org/resources/on-the-day-briefing-spending-review-2025)). These differ because they use different budget lines and baselines, so no single cash series is available. Capital is a particular problem: the **Carnall Farrar analysis says health capital falls 2.2%, from £13.8bn in 2026/27 to £13.5bn in 2027/28**, and the King's Fund says planned NHS capital is lower after the 2026 Budget than under the 2025 Budget ([King's Fund](https://www.kingsfund.org.uk/insight-and-analysis/briefings/autumn-budget-2026-nhs-health-adult-social-care)).

Per-person figures show a weaker picture than totals. The Health Foundation estimates planned day-to-day spending per head, adjusted for population and ageing, will grow only **1.2% a year in real terms from 2013/14 to 2028/29** ([Health Foundation](https://www.health.org.uk/reports-and-analysis/analysis/health-care-funding)). The Health Foundation has also said NHS funding growth was **1.4% a year since 2010**, less than half the long-run average ([Health Foundation](https://www.health.org.uk/reports-and-analysis/briefings/health-and-social-care-funding)).

Devolved nations use the Barnett formula, which gives them a share of changes in English spending rather than a needs-based allocation. In 2018/19 spending per head was **£2,269 in England**, against £2,396 in Scotland, £2,402 in Wales and £2,436 in Northern Ireland, so England was the lowest-funded of the four ([Institute for Government](https://www.instituteforgovernment.org.uk/explainer/devolution-and-nhs)). That pattern has since reversed for 2026–27. The IFS puts **Scotland's health and social care budget at a 0.2% real increase**, England's planned health and social care increase at **2.2%**, and **Wales's health and social care spending falling 2.2%** in real terms ([IFS](https://ifs.org.uk/news/scottish-budget-and-spending-review-signal-big-shifts-health-and-investment-spending-reliance); [IFS](https://ifs.org.uk/publications/public-service-spending-and-performance-wales)). Cardiff University researchers say Welsh NHS spending falls in real terms in 2026–27 for the first time since 2012–13 ([Cardiff University](https://www.cardiff.ac.uk/__data/assets/pdf_file/0016/3033007/Setting-the-Scene-Wales-Report.pdf)). No source retrieved gives Northern Ireland's 2026–27 health settlement, so the comparison is incomplete.

## Waiting lists and emergency care worsened as capacity shrank

The clearest measure of strain is the elective waiting list. The Labour Party's 2010 figure of **2.6 million** is partisan and has no official NHS England confirmation ([Labour](https://labour.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2018/07/NHS_Tory_Failure.pdf)). The list then reached a **record 7.7 million in September 2023** and had fallen to about 7.1 million by July 2026 ([House of Commons Library](https://commonslibrary.parliament.uk/research-briefings/cbp-7281/)). One aggregator describes a "2024 peak", but the Commons Library's September 2023 date is preferred here ([Britclock](https://britclock.co.uk/nhs/waiting-list/)). The latest official release shows the list rising to **7.38 million at the end of September 2026**, and about **116,353 people had waited more than 52 weeks**, against a target of under 1% by March 2026, which was missed ([The British Eye](https://thebritisheye.com/2026/11/12/nhs-waiting-list-england-highest-year/)). In May 2026 only **65.6% of waits were within 18 weeks**, against a 92% standard ([Health & Protection](https://healthcareandprotection.com/nhs-waiting-list-climbs-back-to-7-3-million/)).

Emergency care shows a similar pattern. Four-hour A&E performance bottomed at **65.2% in December 2022** and was **75.4% in July 2026**, against a 95% standard ([UK Government Tracker](https://ukgovernmenttracker.com/nhs)). Demand is at record levels, with **2.49 million A&E attendances in July 2026** ([NHS Confederation](https://thenhsalliance.org/resources/nhs-activity-tracker-august-2026)). Cancer 62-day performance was **71.2% in July 2026** against an 85% standard on the new measure ([House of Commons Library](https://commonslibrary.parliament.uk/research-briefings/cbp-7281/)).

The capacity base has shrunk. English general and acute overnight beds fell **8%** between 2010/11 and 2019/20, and mental health beds fell **24%** to 17,863 by 2025/26 ([Nuffield Trust](https://www.nuffieldtrust.org.uk/resource/hospital-bed-occupancy)). Average occupancy has consistently exceeded 85% since 2010 ([BMA](https://www.bma.org.uk/advice-and-support/nhs-delivery-and-workforce/pressures/nhs-hospital-beds-data-analysis)). The Lowdown, citing a Health Foundation figure, puts the closures since 2010 at **6,025 general and acute beds and 4,734 mental health beds** ([The Lowdown](https://lowdownnhs.info/analysis/nhs-funding-how-did-it-get-here-what-does-it-need/)). Delayed discharges have been a persistent drain. Age UK estimated about **1.93 million bed days lost since June 2010** because patients awaited social care, an older advocacy figure ([Age UK](https://www.ageuk.org.uk/latest-press/archive/nearly-2-million-nhs-days-lost-as-people-remain-in-hospital/)).

The social care squeeze is a central link. Adult social care funding for people aged 65 and over **fell 21% between 2009/10 and 2015/16**, and a Health Foundation-funded working paper found that these cuts led directly to more A&E use ([Health Foundation](https://www.health.org.uk/blogs/what-do-we-know-about-the-effects-of-cutting-public-funding-for-social-care)). The public health grant has been cut since 2015/16 and is over 25% lower per person in real terms, according to The Lowdown ([The Lowdown](https://lowdownnhs.info/funding/13507/)).

## Staff, pay and finances absorbed the squeeze

Staff numbers rose in the five years to July 2026, with **doctors up 23% and nurses up 20%**, and the vacancy rate fell to **6.7% (100,326 posts) in June 2026**, from 7.1% a year earlier ([House of Commons Library](https://commonslibrary.parliament.uk/research-briefings/cbp-7281/); [NHS England Digital](https://digital.nhs.uk/data-and-information/publications/statistical/nhs-vacancies-survey/april-2015---june-2026-experimental-statistics)). The official fall is not all recruitment. DHSC and NHS England told the pay review body that workforce plans were adjusted to trusts' financial position, so part of the fall may reflect fewer funded posts ([DHSC and NHS England](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6ad21fdaa89985bb970267dc/nhs-pay-review-body-written-evidence-2027-to-2028.pdf)). Turnover is high: about **130,200 FTE staff left in the year to November 2025**, against about 147,700 who joined ([House of Commons Library](https://researchbriefings.files.parliament.uk/documents/CBP-10539/CBP-10539.pdf)).

Real pay fell for much of the period. Full Fact found the top of band 5 fell about **9% in real terms between 2010/11 and 2019/20** ([Full Fact](https://fullfact.org/online/september-2020-nhs-pay/)). A union-commissioned London Economics report put starting-nurse pay up 17% in cash terms but down about 10% in real terms ([Channel 4 FactCheck](https://www.channel4.com/news/factcheck/factcheck-have-nurses-already-had-a-pay-rise)). The Health Foundation found average NHS earnings were still **8–11% lower in real terms** than in 2010 even after a 2024 recovery ([Health Foundation](https://www.health.org.uk/reports-and-analysis/analysis/how-much-should-nhs-staff-be-paid)). Unions say nurses and some other staff have lost more than a year's salary in real terms since 2010 ([TUC](https://www.tuc.org.uk/news/nhs-workers-have-have-lost-over-years-worth-salary-2010-new-tuc-analysis-reveals)).

The 2026/27 award is disputed. The government accepted a **3.3%** pay review body recommendation on 12 February 2026 ([GMB](https://www.gmb.org.uk/public-services/nhs-and-ambulance/nhs/)). The RCN and 12 other unions said it was not enough, and UNISON said it would feel like a pay cut for most staff ([Nursing in Practice](https://www.nursinginpractice.com/latest-news/unions-demand-nurse-pay-talks-after-inadequate-nhs-rise/); [Open Access Government](https://www.openaccessgovernment.org/nhs-staff-furious-as-unions-warn-3-3-pay-rise-isnt-enough/206596/)). The government's claim that the award is above forecast inflation (2.2%) appears only on a pay-information website, while unions cite CPI of 3.4% at announcement ([NHS Pay Guide](https://nhstakehomepaycalculator.co.uk/guides/nhs-pay-rise-2026/)). Both sides can be literally correct because they use different inflation measures and periods.

Finances improved in 2025/26 but through management action rather than new money. NHS England reports that draft plans showed a **£4.4bn deficit** (£6.6bn before deficit support funding), and that systems finished with a **£563m deficit**, £400m lower than in 2024/25 on a like-for-like basis ([NHS England](https://www.england.nhs.uk/long-read/month-12-financial-position-2025-26-4-june-2026/)). Agency spend fell from £2.1bn to £1.2bn over the same period ([NHS England](https://www.england.nhs.uk/long-read/month-12-financial-position-2025-26-4-june-2026/)). These are management reports rather than audited accounts. In Wales, audited accounts show an annual deficit of £199m in 2025–26, with six of seven health boards failing the break-even duty, though agency spending fell 61% below its 2022–23 peak ([Audit Wales](https://www.audit.wales/news/nhs-struggling-control-day-day-costs-despite-continued-funding-increases)).

## Causes: funding, demand and productivity

The strongest evidence linking funding to outcomes is for 2010 to 2015. In 2010–2016, NHS services received on average **1.3% extra funding a year**, against demand growth of more than 3% a year ([Journal of Epidemiology and Community Health](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6352397/)). Mortality studies go further but remain contested. A UCL study in BMJ Open linked health and social care cuts to nearly **120,000 excess deaths** in England, estimating 45,368 excess deaths between 2010 and 2014 against pre-2010 trends; the authors say no firm conclusions on cause can be drawn ([UCL](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2017/nov/austerity-linked-120000-extra-deaths-england)). A York study put the figure at **57,550** more deaths than expected in 2010–2014, of which healthcare spending alone accounted for 33,888 ([Disability News Service](https://www.disabilitynewsservice.com/austerity-cuts-to-social-care-and-health-caused-57000-deaths-research-suggests/)). Both depend on modelled counterfactuals, and the government disputed the framing. A 2024 BMJ Public Health study found no direct link between local austerity and for-profit outsourcing of NHS services ([BMJ Public Health](https://doaj.org/article/89cb452322a04cab8c0b69e9ebd176d2)).

Productivity is the main contested factor. The ONS estimates publicly funded healthcare productivity **fell 25.6% in the year ending 2021** ([ONS](https://www.ons.gov.uk/economy/economicoutputandproductivity/publicservicesproductivity/articles/publicservicesproductivityestimateshealthcare/financialyearending2021)). NHS England estimates acute hospital productivity was about **11% below pre-pandemic levels** (8% after adjusting for industrial action), citing social care constraints among other causes ([NHS England](https://www.england.nhs.uk/long-read/nhs-productivity/)). The King's Fund says productivity growth was held back by low input growth during austerity, fell sharply during Covid-19, and that nobody knows why it has stayed sluggish ([King's Fund](https://www.kingsfund.org.uk/insight-and-analysis/blogs/nhs-productivity-decline)). The Commons Library notes that the Nuffield Trust and Institute for Government found no firm evidence for NHS England's explanation of reduced productivity ([House of Commons Library](https://researchbriefings.files.parliament.uk/documents/CBP-10313/CBP-10313.pdf)).

The Public Accounts Committee (PAC) points to several causes at once. Its report on financial sustainability records systems overspending by £621m in 2022–23 and ending 2023–24 with an aggregated £1.4bn deficit, despite £4.5bn of extra government funding and a NHS England underspend of £1.7bn. It also says technology investment stalled because capital was redirected to cover ICB deficits ([PAC](https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/cm5901/cmselect/cmpubacc/350/report.html)). That pattern supports both sides: underinvestment is real, but so is weak financial control. The PAC's January 2026 report on clinical negligence says government liability quadrupled over 20 years to **£60bn in 2024–25** ([PAC](https://committees.parliament.uk/committee/127/public-accounts-committee/news/211608/clinical-negligence-govt-and-nhs-fail-to-act-despite-repeated-warnings-over-patient-safety/)).

## Future plans rest on productivity and a 2027 review

The government's plan depends on the 2025 Spending Review and the 10 Year Health Plan. The plan sets a three-year revenue and four-year capital settlement from 2026–27 and phases out deficit support funding worth £2.2bn in 2025–26 ([Fit for the Future](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6888a0b1a11f859994409147/fit-for-the-future-10-year-health-plan-for-england.pdf)). The government also claims £10bn of efficiencies and savings in 2025–26, equal to 5.2% of its day-to-day budget ([HM Treasury](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6ae20b9039d29f3f9b9b3f55/E03668986_HMT_Budget_2026_TEXT_PRINT__1_.pdf)). This is a government figure with no independent verification in the sources. The BMA reads the plan as containing no new funding beyond the Spending Review, expecting 2% a year productivity gains and surplus budgets by 2029–30 ([BMA](https://www.bma.org.uk/advice-and-support/nhs-delivery-and-workforce/nhs-reforms/reforms-to-the-nhs)). The government describes a £65bn ten-year capital settlement ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/10-year-capital-plan-for-health-and-social-care/10-year-capital-plan-for-health-and-social-care)).

Independent forecasts suggest the pressure outruns the plan. The IFS says spending would need to rise about **3.6% a year** to meet pressures, which is the long-run average but far above what has happened since 2010; this report predates the 2025 Spending Review ([IFS](https://ifs.org.uk/sites/default/files/2024-05/IFS-REPORT-The-past-and-future-of-UK-health-spending.pdf)). The Nuffield Trust says the 2025 settlement is "more than wiped out" by inflation and planned treatment growth, and that soaring inflation will take at least £1bn from next year's NHS budget in real terms ([Nuffield Trust](https://www.nuffieldtrust.org.uk/news-item/nuffield-trust-response-to-the-spending-review-2025); [Nuffield Trust](https://www.nuffieldtrust.org.uk/news-item/nuffield-trust-response-to-the-autumn-budget)). The Nuffield Trust also estimates a US medicines deal would mean about **13,500 fewer NHS staff across the UK in 2028**, a single think-tank estimate resting on stated assumptions ([Nuffield Trust](https://www.nuffieldtrust.org.uk/news-item/nuffield-trust-estimates-impact-of-us-trade-deal-on-nhs-workforce)). The King's Fund warns that short-term "sprint" funding to cut waiting lists is not sustained recovery ([King's Fund](https://www.kingsfund.org.uk/insight-and-analysis/press-releases/nhs-performance-08102026)).

No independent forecast of English waiting times or staffing to 2030 was found, and the 2027 Spending Review had not taken place as of 2026-11-20. That leaves the central question open: whether productivity gains can close a gap that the forecasts put at roughly 3–4% of annual pressure against about 3% of planned growth.

## Conclusion

Funding has changed substantially since 2010, and the evidence shows it has mattered, but the cleanest effects are financial and capacity-related rather than proven outcome effects. Real-terms growth fell well below the long-run trend in the 2010s, a pandemic surge temporarily raised it, and the 2025 settlement restored modest growth, while capital and pay remain pressure points. The strongest link is between funding restraint, bed losses and the failure to keep pace with demand, especially in social care and emergency care.

The debate is less about whether funding has been tight than about how much of the current state is a money problem. Funding-first sources point to capital backlogs, pay losses and deficits. Reform-first sources point to productivity, demand and management, and the PAC and NAO reports support both readings. The government's bet is that productivity gains and multi-year settlements will let the NHS do more with about 3% real growth, but the 2026 Budget's small new sum, the capital cut and the deferral of key decisions to 2027 leave that bet largely untested.
