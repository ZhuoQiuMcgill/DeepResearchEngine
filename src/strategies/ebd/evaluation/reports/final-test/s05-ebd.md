# Opponents Frame Affirmative Action as Unfair Discrimination

Opponents of affirmative action in the United States rest their case on four arguments: race-based treatment violates a colorblind constitutional principle, it is unfair to individual applicants in a zero-sum contest, it harms the students it is meant to help through mismatch, and it stigmatizes beneficiaries and divides society. The Supreme Court majority adopted the first two in 2023, ending race-conscious admissions at Harvard and UNC ([Supreme Court opinion](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/22pdf/20-1199_hgdj.pdf)). The mismatch and stigma arguments have weaker empirical support, and the outcome evidence is disputed. The strongest recent causal study finds that ending affirmative action lowered minority degree attainment in California, while an older review finds no overall graduation effect, and the law-school mismatch estimates are contested. Public opinion depends heavily on wording: **Pew found 50% disapproved of selective colleges considering race in 2023** ([Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/fact-sheet/topic-affirmative-action-in-college-admissions/)), while **a May 2026 Verasight poll found 26% opposed programs designed to improve access to higher education** ([Verasight Data Library](https://data.verasight.io/politics/a-majority-of-americans-favor-affirmative-action-in-higher-education/)). Opposition has moved from undergraduate admissions into K-12 schools, military academies, scholarships and federal contracting.

## Colorblindness and zero-sum fairness anchor the case

The core reason is a principle that the Constitution and civil-rights law forbid treating people differently by race. Justice Thomas's concurrence argues that "all forms of discrimination based on race—including so-called affirmative action—are prohibited" ([Justia, 600 U.S. 20-1199](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/600/20-1199/)). Chief Justice Roberts wrote that "eliminating racial discrimination means eliminating all of it" ([Wikipedia, Constitutional colorblindness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitutional_colorblindness)). Edward Blum, president of Students for Fair Admissions (SFFA), called the 2023 ruling "the beginning of the restoration of the colorblind legal covenant" ([The Harvard Crimson](https://www.thecrimson.com/article/2023/6/30/sffa-decision-reaction/)) and argued the same logic applies to internships, scholarships, hiring and contracting ([SFFA anniversary statement](https://studentsforfairadmissions.org/celebrating-the-one-year-anniversary-of-students-for-fair-admissions-v-harvard-unc-and-the-end-of-race-based-affirmative-action/)). The principle has moved into federal policy: Trump's January 2025 order directs agencies to terminate "discriminatory and illegal preferences" ([White House](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2025/01/ending-illegal-discrimination-and-restoring-merit-based-opportunity/)). Critics answer that the colorblind rule is applied inconsistently, and that the majority's own allowance for essays about race undercuts it ([Maryland Law Review](https://digitalcommons.law.umaryland.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=4020&context=mlr); [California Law Review](https://www.californialawreview.org/print/incoherence-colorblind-constitution)).

The second argument is that preferences are unfair to individual applicants. The Court said "college admissions are zero-sum," so a benefit to some necessarily comes at others' expense ([Justia, 600 U.S. 20-1199](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/600/20-1199/)). SFFA's main example was Asian American applicants, whom it argued were disadvantaged compared with similarly qualified Hispanic, Black or white applicants ([Time](https://time.com/6225372/edward-blum-affirmative-action-supreme-court-interview/)). The same framing now drives Pacific Legal Foundation cases against K-12 and magnet-school admissions, including a challenge to New York City's Specialized High Schools criteria ([PLF press release](https://pacificlegal.org/press-release/nyc-mom-challenges-discriminatory-school-admissions-policy/)). The Supreme Court agreed on October 19, 2026 to hear a Montgomery County case on aggregate disparate impact ([Latin Times](https://www.latintimes.com/beyond-affirmative-action-scotus-case-that-could-extend-admissions-rollback-k-12-schools-600343)), and the outcomes of these cases are not yet known.

The Harvard record is the main empirical test of the fairness claim, and it is contested. The district court found that Harvard did not discriminate on the basis of race and did not use quotas ([Wikipedia, Edward Blum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Blum_(litigant))), and that its personal-rating disparity did not burden Asian American applicants significantly more than race-conscious policies burdened white applicants ([Harvard Crimson, 2019](https://www.thecrimson.com/article/2019/10/2/admissions-suit-decision/)). The First Circuit affirmed ([Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/cert/20-1199)). The Supreme Court majority relied on the First Circuit's finding that race consideration led to an **11.1% decrease** in Asian American admissions ([Supreme Court opinion](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/22pdf/20-1199_hgdj.pdf)), while the dissent said that figure rested on a single footnote and could not displace the district court's factfinding ([Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/20-1199)). Expert estimates also conflict: Arcidiacono estimated Asian Americans would form **43% of the class** on academic factors alone ([Expert Institute](https://www.expertinstitute.com/resources/insights/expert-witnesses-in-the-harvard-discrimination-lawsuit-arrive-at-conflicting-conclusions/)), while the Harvard Crimson, reporting on court filings, gave a figure above 51% ([Harvard Crimson, 2018](https://www.thecrimson.com/article/2018/6/19/asian-american-academics/)).

## Mismatch and stigma claims lack settled support

Mismatch is the main argument that preferences harm their beneficiaries. Richard Sander holds that students admitted with very large academic preferences may struggle and face lower odds of passing the bar ([ECGI](https://www.ecgi.global/publications/podcasts/richard-sander-ucla-law-a-mismatch-critique-of-affirmative-action)), and Sander and Stuart Taylor's 2013 book *Mismatch* argues that affirmative action hurts the students it is meant to help ([BU scholarship record](https://scholarship.law.bu.edu/faculty_scholarship/298)). Thomas made similar points in his SFFA concurrence ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/1186454682)). Critics are numerous. A review of social-science research concludes that the best peer-reviewed studies do not support mismatch for Black and Latino graduation rates and earnings ([BU scholarship record](https://scholarship.law.bu.edu/faculty_scholarship/298)), and a critic says Sander's own data do not support the hypothesis ([JBHE](https://www.jbhe.com/features/46_black_student_mismatch.html)).

The graduation evidence conflicts. Zachary Bleemer's Quarterly Journal of Economics paper finds that ending affirmative action pushed underrepresented minority (URM) applicants into lower-quality colleges ([Bleemer, QJE 2021](https://ouci.dntb.gov.ua/en/works/ldNgYBq7/)), and his working paper reports that URM degree attainment fell overall and in STEM, which he describes as inconsistent with mismatch ([Bleemer, CSHE](https://cshe.berkeley.edu/publications/affirmative-action-mismatch-and-economic-mobility-after-california%E2%80%99s-proposition-209)). An earlier review by Arcidiacono and Lovenheim reports that bans do not affect minority college graduation rates overall, but do reduce completion for lower-ranked students ([Arcidiacono & Lovenheim, Duke](https://public.econ.duke.edu/~psarcidi/aa2.pdf)). **The report follows Bleemer on overall degree attainment**, because his design is quasi-experimental, the review predates his 2021 paper, and the review itself also finds losses for lower-ranked students. The dispute is not fully resolved, however: Sander objected that Bleemer's underlying data are not public ([Manhattan Institute](https://manhattan.institute/article/does-affirmative-action-lead-to-mismatch)).

The bar-passage dispute is sharper. Sander and Steinbuch argue that large preferences cause underperformance, and that mismatch could fully explain half of the Black-white bar passage gap ([Sander & Steinbuch](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/URLs_Cited/OT2022/20-1199/20-1199-1.pdf)). Ayres, Brooks and Shelley argue that a second-choice analysis of the Bar Passage Study shows no mismatch effect on bar passage ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0144818821000569)), and a separate critique says coding the missing race data properly contradicts Sander's conclusion about Black students ([JLE comment](https://jle.aals.org/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1801&context=home)). **The evidence does not decide this question**, so the report treats the size of bar-passage mismatch as unresolved.

The stigma argument rests mainly on Thomas's testimony and opinions. Thomas argues that affirmative action began from a premise that Black people were "inferior" and leaves successful Black people with a stigma of inferiority ([HuffPost](https://www.huffpost.com/entry/clarence-thomas-affirmative-action_n_649e0e12e4b065a315b7e3e2/amp)), and he has said that the stigma of preferential treatment made it hard to find a job after Yale Law School ([ABC7 Chicago](https://abc7chicago.com/supreme-court-affirmative-action-clarence-thomas-ketanji-brown-jackson/13441662/)). A review of the stigma literature calls the account of stigma in Sander, Taylor and Thomas "theoretically impoverished and shallow" and not supported empirically ([BU scholarship record](https://scholarship.law.bu.edu/faculty_scholarship/298)). SFFA's claim that "dozens of polls" show nearly 70% of Americans agree with ending racial preferences ([SFFA anniversary statement](https://studentsforfairadmissions.org/celebrating-the-one-year-anniversary-of-students-for-fair-admissions-v-harvard-unc-and-the-end-of-race-based-affirmative-action/)) names no survey, so it cannot be checked.

## Courts, ballots and executive orders turned opposition into law

The legal path runs from Bakke to SFFA. In *Bakke* (1978), the Court struck down a fixed quota at UC Davis medical school but let race be one factor; there was no single majority opinion, and Justice Powell cast the deciding vote ([Oyez](https://www.oyez.org/cases/1979/76-811)). *Grutter* (2003) upheld a narrowly tailored diversity policy at Michigan Law School, 5-4, and O'Connor expected preferences would no longer be needed in about 25 years ([Ballotpedia, Grutter](https://ballotpedia.org/Grutter_v._Bollinger); [Wikipedia, Grutter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grutter_v._Bollinger)). In *SFFA* (2023), the Court held Harvard's and UNC's programs violated the Equal Protection Clause and applied strict scrutiny ([FindLaw, SFFA](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/us-supreme-court/20-1199.html)). Sources differ on the vote: FindLaw and the dissent's three named justices support 6-3, while Ballotpedia lists 6-2 ([Ballotpedia, SFFA](https://ballotpedia.org/Students_for_Fair_Admissions,_Inc._v._President_&_Fellows_of_Harvard)). The majority's reasoning, which said race could not be used negatively, rested on Grutter's diversity rationale being unmeasurable ([Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/20-1199)). Sotomayor's dissent argued that the ruling overruled decades of precedent ([Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Students_for_Fair_Admissions_v._President_and_Fellows_of_Harvard_College/Opinion_of_Justice_Sotomayor)).

Ballot measures carried the opposition into states. California's Proposition 209 (1996) eliminated state preferences in public employment, education and contracting ([CA Secretary of State](https://vigarchive.sos.ca.gov/1996/general/pamphlet/209analysis.htm)). It passed with **55% of the vote** per [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1996_California_Proposition_209), while [Fox News](https://www.foxnews.com/politics/californians-vote-prop-16-affirmative-action) gives 54%. Supporters argued that preferences amounted to reverse discrimination ([Schwartz Cohen Law](https://schwartzcohenlaw.com/understanding-prop-209-californias-affirmative-action-ban/)), and Peter Kirsanow argued that minority students were not succeeding because of mismatch and that graduation rates rose after the ban ([Fox News](https://www.foxnews.com/politics/californians-vote-prop-16-affirmative-action)). Bleemer's research found the ban shifted URM students from more selective UC campuses to less selective ones ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/1184461214)). Voters rejected repeal in 2020 by **56% to 44%** per [Ballotpedia](https://news.ballotpedia.org/2020/11/04/california-voters-reject-affirmative-action-proposition/), though [Federalist Society](https://fedsoc.org/commentary/fedsoc-blog/why-did-california-s-prop-16-fail-a-county-by-county-assessment) reports 57% to 43%. In *Schuette* (2014), the Court upheld Michigan's voter-approved ban, reasoning that voters may decide the policy through the democratic process ([Justia, Schuette](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/572/291)).

Federal policy changed most sharply in employment and contracting. Executive Order 11246, signed by Lyndon Johnson on September 28, 1965, required federal contractors to take affirmative action ([CEI](https://cei.org/studies/contradictory-to-our-countrys-laws/)). President Trump revoked it in Executive Order 14173 on January 21, 2025 ([Lexology, Littler](https://lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=7c7c7c87-ce8b-41a0-95b0-6b245012bf73)). A secondary compliance guide says a Labor Department rule removing the regulations takes effect October 26, 2026 ([Interviewer Signal](https://interviewersignal.com/blog/federal-hiring-law-changes-2026)), but the Federal Register text was not checked. In the military, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth banned race-based admissions at the academies in January 2025 ([Higher Ed Dive](https://www.highereddive.com/news/students-for-fair-admissions-drops-lawsuits-west-point-air-force-academy/757503/)).

Litigation since 2023 shows how far opponents have extended SFFA. SFFA dropped its West Point and Air Force suits after the Defense Department banned race-conscious admissions ([Higher Ed Dive](https://www.highereddive.com/news/students-for-fair-admissions-drops-lawsuits-west-point-air-force-academy/757503/)). The suit against UC's undergraduate and law school admissions continues ([Thompson Coburn, July 2026](https://www.thompsoncoburn.com/insights/higher-education-litigation-summary-july-2026/)), and a suit against UCLA's medical school is ongoing with the United States intervening ([Thompson Coburn, June 2026](https://www.thompsoncoburn.com/insights/higher-education-litigation-summary-june-2026/)). The Justice Department is reported to be investigating **15 medical schools** ([Gibson Dunn](https://www.gibsondunn.com/dei-task-force-update-august-11-2026/)), and it has issued formal findings against **at least eight professional schools** ([Voice of San Francisco](https://thevoicesf.org/pacific-legal-foundation-joins-lawsuit-challenging-university-of-washington-admissions/)). Whether SFFA reaches corporate programs is disputed: one commentary says the ruling does not reach them on its face ([Townhall](https://townhall.com/columnists/jay-rogers/2026/06/29/three-years-after-sffa-the-ruling-is-still-making-changes-n2678426)), while a Yale Law Journal note argues its zero-sum logic makes many race-conscious policies unconstitutional ([Yale Law Journal](https://yalelawjournal.org/note/reparative-action-admissions-preferences-after-sffa)).

## Race-neutral substitutes recover less racial diversity

Opponents have offered few formal alternatives beyond the policies that universities and states actually adopted, and the sources do not show a shared package. The main example is Texas's top-ten-percent rule, adopted in 1997 after affirmative action was struck down ([NBER Digest](https://www.nber.org/digest/jun20/results-texass-experiment-increasing-college-diversity)). Black, Denning and Rothstein found that it raised attendance, graduation and earnings for students from underrepresented high schools without reducing outcomes for those crowded out ([NBER Digest](https://www.nber.org/digest/jun20/results-texass-experiment-increasing-college-diversity)). On diversity, Kapor found that the plan raised enrollment of economically disadvantaged students but had few other diversity effects ([NBER Digest](https://www.nber.org/digest/202408/effects-texas-top-ten-percent-college-admissions-plan)), and Cortes and Klasik found little evidence it increased racial diversity ([Daily Texan](https://thedailytexan.com/2019/07/30/texas-top-10-rule-makes-little-difference-in-diversity-on-campus-study-says/)). Other studies are more favorable to race-neutral policies. A study across states found that affirmative action raised underrepresented minority enrollment by over 20%, UC's top-percent policy by less than 4%, and holistic review by about 7%, and that none of the common alternatives substantially changed socioeconomic composition ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S004727272300021X)). A UCLA Law Review essay concludes that socioeconomic preferences and percent plans are not effective substitutes for race-conscious measures ([UCLA Law Review](https://www.uclalawreview.org/workable-class-based-race-neutral-alternatives-leading-american-universities/)), though it is advocacy-leaning. The Century Foundation, which favors class-based preferences, reports that socioeconomic alternatives produce much higher socioeconomic diversity ([Century Foundation](https://tcf.org/content/report/achieving-better-diversity/)).

Post-2023 enrollment data show the gap in practice. **Black enrollment at some elite campuses fell to as low as 2% of the freshman class** ([OPB/AP](https://www.opb.org/article/2025/10/24/black-enrollment-affirmative-action-ban/)), and Harvard, Princeton and Amherst all saw large drops ([Brookings](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/the-complex-ramifications-of-students-for-fair-admissions-v-harvard/)). Across public flagships, Black and Hispanic first-year enrollment rose 6%, but **20 of 50 flagships** saw Black enrollment fall or stay flat ([Hechinger Report](https://hechingerreport.org/after-affirmative-action-takeaways-and-puzzles-from-college-admissions-data/)). These figures cover short windows, and some schools changed testing rules at the same time, so they do not isolate the effect of the alternatives.

Employment and contracting evidence splits by period and data. Miller found that contractors subject to Executive Order 11246 raised their African American employment share by **0.13 to 0.15 percentage points a year** relative to non-contractors ([DOL CLEAR](https://clear.dol.gov/study/persistent-effect-temporary-affirmative-action-miller-2014)). His data cover 1978 to 2004 and about 44,500 firms and establishments ([Berkeley Haas](https://newsroom.haas.berkeley.edu/magazine/fall-2018/the-enduring-effects-of-affirmative-action/)), and he attributes the persistence to "screening capital" ([Richmond Fed](https://www.richmondfed.org/publications/research/econ_focus/2017/q3/research_spotlight)). A Census study using linked employer-employee data for 2000–2014 finds **no effect on employment shares or hiring for any minority group**, with effects about 100 times smaller than Miller's ([Census CES WP 22-54](https://www2.census.gov/ces/wp/2022/CES-WP-22-54.pdf)). **The evidence does not decide this conflict**: the periods barely overlap, and Miller acknowledges that firms may choose to become contractors because compliance is easier for them ([DOL CLEAR](https://clear.dol.gov/study/persistent-effect-temporary-affirmative-action-miller-2014)). For the 2000–2014 period, the Census null is the only evidence reviewed, and the report treats it as the better-identified result for that period.

Contracting preferences change who wins more than they raise costs. A study of highway subcontracting requirements found they did not substantially raise prime contractors' bids or costs ([Cleveland Fed](https://www.clevelandfed.org/en/newsroom%20and%20events/publications/working%20papers/~/media/2024108be812481eb3d3219d036ef424.ashx)), and a study of California subcontracting goals found they raised disadvantaged-business usage significantly ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0167718711001093)). A study of the SBA 8(a) program found that participants' performance is driven by funding rather than mentorship, and that 8(a) firms are substantially more likely to go out of business ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11187-017-9860-z)). An advocacy group reports that after Illinois DOT eliminated DBE contract goals in 2002–03, the DBE share of contract value fell below 2% ([NAACP LDF](https://www.naacpldf.org/what-is-the-disadvantaged-business-enterprise-program/)), a single-state figure that has not been verified.

## Opinion splits by question wording, party and education

Public opinion depends on how the question is asked. The table compares the most recent national readings found.

| Survey | Question | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Verasight, May 13–18, 2026 (n=1,000) | Programs to improve access to higher education for Black people, women and other minorities | 58% favor, 26% oppose, 16% don't know ([Verasight](https://data.verasight.io/politics/a-majority-of-americans-favor-affirmative-action-in-higher-education/)) |
| Pew, March 27–April 2, 2023 (n=5,079) | Selective colleges taking race and ethnicity into account | 33% approve, 50% disapprove, 16% not sure ([Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/fact-sheet/topic-affirmative-action-in-college-admissions/)) |
| Gallup, 2018 | Favor or oppose affirmative action programs for racial minorities | 61% favor, 30% oppose ([Gallup](https://news.gallup.com/opinion/polling-matters/317006/affirmative-action-public-opinion.aspx)) |
| Gallup, 2016 | Basis for admissions | 70% solely on merit, 26% race considered for diversity ([Gallup](https://news.gallup.com/poll/193508/oppose-colleges-considering-race-admissions.aspx)) |

Within the 2023 Pew survey, **74% of Republicans and Republican leaners disapproved**, against 29% of Democrats ([Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/fact-sheet/topic-affirmative-action-in-college-admissions/)). By race, 57% of White adults and 52% of Asian adults disapproved, compared with 39% of Hispanic adults and 29% of Black adults ([Just the News](https://justthenews.com/government/courts-law/half-americans-oppose-affirmative-action-ahead-scotus-decision-practice-poll)). Among Asian Americans who had heard of affirmative action, **76% said colleges should not use race or ethnicity** in admissions ([Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/race-and-ethnicity/2023/06/08/asian-americans-hold-mixed-views-around-affirmative-action/)). College graduates were split almost evenly, at 45% approve and 47% disapprove ([Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2023/06/08/more-americans-disapprove-than-approve-of-colleges-considering-race-ethnicity-in-admissions-decisions/)).

The reasons respondents give cluster around fairness and merit. In 2023, **49% said considering race makes admissions less fair and 20% said it makes them fairer** ([Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2023/06/16/americans-and-affirmative-action-how-the-public-sees-the-consideration-of-race-in-college-admissions-hiring/)), and 78% of those who disapprove said race consideration makes admissions less fair ([Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2023/06/08/perceived-impacts-of-factoring-race-and-ethnicity-into-college-admissions/)). Gallup found 67% to 70% chose "solely on merit" across four readings over 13 years ([Gallup](https://news.gallup.com/opinion/polling-matters/243965/harvard-affirmative-action-case-public-opinion.aspx)), and found that people most familiar with admissions were no more likely than others to accept race-sensitive admissions ([Gallup](https://news.gallup.com/poll/193508/oppose-colleges-considering-race-admissions.aspx)). Wording matters: Cato, summarizing YouGov and Pew, reports about 7 in 10 opposed when affirmative action was described without its purpose, and about 5 to 6 in 10 when its intent was mentioned ([Cato Institute](https://www.cato.org/blog/americans-say-they-affirmative-action-yet-oppose-racial-preferences-college-admissions)).

Trends are hard to read because wording changed. Gallup's broad-concept support rose from less than half in 2001 to 61% in 2018 ([Gallup](https://news.gallup.com/poll/247046/americans-support-affirmative-action-programs-rises.aspx)), and Gallup found 62% favor in 2021 ([Gallup](https://news.gallup.com/poll/355958/gender-disparities-views-women-equality-persist.aspx)). Pew's 2014 reading found 63% saying programs to increase Black and minority student numbers were a good thing ([Deseret News](https://www.deseret.com/2019/3/4/20667415/is-this-the-beginning-of-the-end-of-affirmative-action-on-college-campuses/)), but the 2023 admissions question is different, so the apparent drop should not be read as a like-for-like trend. The public also distinguishes admissions from other uses: Gallup found **72% opposed preference for Black Americans in hiring and promotion** ([Gallup](https://news.gallup.com/opinion/polling-matters/317006/affirmative-action-public-opinion.aspx)). Gallup found 68% called the 2023 ruling "mostly a good thing," with Black adults split 52% to 48% ([The Hill](https://thehill.com/homenews/education/4411246-majority-support-ban-race-college-admissions-poll/)). No Pew or Gallup reading from 2025 or 2026 on admissions was found.

## Conclusion

The main reasons for opposition are principled and easy to state: colorblindness, individual fairness and merit. These reasons have won in the courts, in state ballot measures and in federal executive action, and they now drive litigation against K-12 schools, medical schools and scholarships. The reasons that depend on empirical claims are weaker. Mismatch, the argument that preferences harm their beneficiaries, is contested on law-school bar passage and on California graduation data, and the stigma claim rests largely on testimony and opinion. Opponents' strongest evidence is not that race-neutral alternatives fail to reduce racial gaps, which they clearly do not fully prevent, but that preferences are unfair and that their harms are real but hard to measure.

The outcome evidence will not settle the debate on its own. The disputes turn on data access (Sander's objections to Bleemer's data), on period (Miller's 1970s–90s gains against the 2000–2014 null), and on methods (the Harvard personal-rating analysis and the bar-passage coding). Post-2023 enrollment and employment data over several years are the most useful next evidence, and the pending cases on UC, UCLA, medical schools and the Montgomery County K-12 case will decide the legal scope. Public opinion is more divided than the headline numbers suggest, with **half of Americans disapproving of race consideration in 2023 but a clear majority supporting programs described as improving access**. The wording question, not a settled majority, is what the public record shows.
