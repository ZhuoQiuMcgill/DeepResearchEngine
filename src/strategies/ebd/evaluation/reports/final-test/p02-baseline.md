# Insulin Shifted Diabetes From Starvation to Replacement Therapy

Insulin is credited to Frederick Banting, Charles Best, John Macleod and James Collip at the University of Toronto in 1921, not to Alexander Fleming, whose documented discoveries are lysozyme (1922) and penicillin (1928) ([Nobel Prize, Fleming facts](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/medicine/1945/fleming/facts/); [National Library of Scotland](https://digital.nls.uk/scientists/biographies/alexander-fleming/discoveries.html)). Before insulin, the leading treatment was severe food restriction, and **50% of type 1 patients died within 20 months of diagnosis** ([JAMA](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/article-abstract/2088833)). Insulin changed this within months: it was first given to a patient in January 1922, and **Eli Lilly began selling the first commercial product, Iletin, in 1923** ([ACS](https://www.acs.org/content/dam/acsorg/education/whatischemistry/landmarks/insulin/insulin-booklet.pdf)). Survival and acute emergency outcomes improved, but early preparations were impure and dosing was imprecise, so hypoglycaemia remained a major risk. The later shifts came from recombinant human insulin, which the **FDA approved in October 1982**, and from analogues, pumps and continuous glucose monitoring (CGM). Access remains uneven: WHO reports that **about one in two people needing insulin for type 2 diabetes does not get it** ([WHO, 2021 report](https://www.who.int/news/item/12-11-2021-new-who-report-maps-barriers-to-insulin-availability-and-suggests-actions-to-promote-universal-access)).

## Fleming's documented work is penicillin, not insulin

The question's premise does not match the record. Fleming shared the **1945 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for penicillin** with Ernst Boris Chain and Howard Florey ([Nobel Prize, Fleming facts](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/medicine/1945/fleming/facts/)). The 1923 prize for insulin went to Banting and Macleod, and Best and Collip were not named ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0168822721001789); [Diabetes UK](https://www.diabetes.org.uk/our-research/about-our-research/our-impact/discovery-of-insulin)). No source found links Fleming to insulin research or to the Toronto work. The name "insulin" was proposed earlier by Edward Sharpey-Schafer, which is a naming claim, not a discovery ([McGill](https://www.mcgill.ca/oss/article/history/learning-movies)). Credit within the Toronto group is also contested: one 2026 account describes Banting proposing the approach, Best running early experiments, Macleod providing expertise, and Collip producing the purified extract ([GreekReporter](https://greekreporter.com/2026/11/14/insulin-discovery-nobel-controversy/)), while Wikipedia records Banting's view that Macleod's role was negligible ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Macleod_(physiologist))).

## Starvation diets were the only option before 1922

From 1915, Allen and Joslin promoted repeated fasting and prolonged undernourishment, aimed at symptom relief and longer life rather than cure ([PMC3062586](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3062586/)). Allen's regimen limited the body's glucose supply to slow diabetic ketoacidosis, keeping patients just above metabolic collapse ([ScienceInsights](https://scienceinsights.org/how-did-diabetics-survive-before-insulin/)). Adults fared relatively well on it, but children hovered between death from diabetes and death from starvation ([Diabetologia](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00125-008-1203-9)). Joslin's 1917 report on 59 children diagnosed before age 10 found **38 died within an average of 1.4 years** ([Diapedia](https://www.diapedia.org/type-1-diabetes-mellitus/2104085199/natural-history)). Estimates of survival vary: one source gives **one to three years** for most type 1 patients even with the best pre-insulin care ([Biology Insights](https://biologyinsights.com/how-did-diabetics-survive-before-insulin/)), while JDCA gives a maximum of six years on the Allen diet ([JDCA](https://www.thejdca.org/publications/report-library/archived-reports/2024-reports/t1d-history-a-burden-carried-for-3-millenia.html)). These figures come from reviews and secondary summaries rather than a single cohort, so they should be read as approximate.

## Insulin reached patients within two years, but supply was impure

The Toronto team began work under Macleod's direction on **17 May 1921**, and Macleod announced the findings, including the term "insulin", on **3 May 1922** ([Canadian Encyclopedia](https://thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/the-discovery-of-insulin)). The first human use is dated inconsistently. One account gives **11 January 1922** ([Uplevel](https://www.uplevel.bio/articles/insulin-discovery-toronto-1921)), another gives **23 January 1922** for the 14-year-old Leonard Thompson, who responded to purified extract prepared by Collip ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2213858720303375)), and a University of Toronto heritage exhibit lists **20 December 1921** ([U of T Heritage](https://heritage.utoronto.ca/exhibits/insulin)). No source reconciles these dates. Thompson, who weighed 65 pounds on Allen's diet, regained weight and energy within weeks ([Biology Insights](https://biologyinsights.com/how-did-diabetics-survive-before-insulin/)). Supply, not clinical benefit, was the limit in 1922: only a few critically ill patients could be treated ([PMC7773348](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7773348)). By early 1923, Lilly was reportedly able to meet world demand, and the Toronto Insulin Committee gave it exclusive US rights until 1924 ([PMC7773348](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7773348)). Insulin was released on prescription on **15 October 1923** ([Encyclopedia of Indianapolis](https://indyencyclopedia.org/insulin/)).

Early product quality was poor. Doses required **3 to 7 mL** injections, which caused painful abscesses, and hypoglycaemia was frequent ([JCI](https://www.jci.org/articles/view/142239)). Supply moved from dog pancreas to cattle pancreas in November 1921 ([Practical Diabetes](https://wchh.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/pdi.2347)). Lipodystrophy affected **33%–55% of patients** in one review, though that figure may not describe 1920s products ([PMC8370559](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8370559/)). Early insulins lasted about six hours, which meant several injections a day ([PMC6205949](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6205949/)).

## Survival rose, but complications and hypoglycaemia remained

The gain in acute survival is clearest for ketoacidosis. Sources report DKA mortality as close to **100%** in the 1920s before insulin ([Medscape](https://www.medscape.com/viewarticle/846124)), **29%** by the 1930s and under 10% by the 1950s ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diabetic_ketoacidosis)), and **12% in 1940 and 1.6% by 1945** with high-dose insulin ([PMC13284666](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13284666/)). These figures differ by period and population, and one textbook still reports **10% to 30%** mortality after insulin was introduced ([Cornell](https://confluence.cornell.edu/download/attachments/372902373/Chapter%208%20Diabetic%20Ketoacidosis%20Endocrinology%20Book.pdf?version=1&modificationDate=1591134261000&api=v2)). Insulin therefore changed the time course of type 1 diabetes without eliminating early death. In the 1930s, delayed kidney, eye and heart complications appeared, and **only one in two patients who started insulin before age 20 would live to 55** ([Diapedia](https://www.diapedia.org/type-1-diabetes-mellitus/2104085199/natural-history)).

Trial evidence later showed that tighter control reduced these complications. In the DCCT, intensive therapy reduced early microvascular disease by **35–76%** ([Diabetes Care](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/37/1/9/31789/The-Diabetes-Control-and-Complications-Trial)), although an earlier summary gives **26–63%** ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/neuroscience/diabetes-control-and-complications-trial)); both ranges are reported here because the sources differ. The cost was a **threefold increase in hypoglycaemia**, which the same overview did not link to cognitive or quality-of-life decline ([Diabetes Care](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/37/1/9/31789/The-Diabetes-Control-and-Complications-Trial)). In EDIC, the follow-up of that cohort, cardiovascular events fell **58%** after a mean of 18 years ([Diabetes Care](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/37/1/9/31789/The-Diabetes-Control-and-Complications-Trial)). In type 2 diabetes, UKPDS showed that intensive therapy with sulfonylureas or insulin reduced microvascular complications, with weight gain and hypoglycaemia as the main costs ([Wiki Journal Club](https://www.wikijournalclub.org/wiki/UKPDS_33)). Because these trials combined insulin with other measures, they do not isolate insulin's effect alone.

## Recombinant insulin and newer tools reshaped dosing and guidelines

Until 1982, human-use insulin came from slaughterhouse animals. One account states that **about 8,000 pounds of pancreas from roughly 23,500 animals** were needed for one pound of insulin ([ThriveMed](https://www.thrivemed.us/thrivecityblog/when-bacteria-started-making-human-insulin-1982)). Lilly filed for approval on **14 May 1982**, and the FDA approved Humulin, made in engineered E. coli, on **28 October 1982** ([Clinical Times](https://theclinicaltimes.com/landmarks/1982-recombinant-human-insulin-humulin-approval); [BioSpace](https://www.biospace.com/first-recombinant-insulin-marks-36th-year-of-dna-technology)). The transition was gradual, and no retrieved source gives a date when animal insulin disappeared from any national market. Analogues followed: a meta-analysis in type 1 diabetes found a small HbA1c difference (about **−0.07%**) but reduced nocturnal and severe hypoglycaemia for long-acting analogues ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19267715/)). Evidence is mixed for short-acting analogues. One analysis found HbA1c about **0.4% lower** in type 2 diabetes ([Wiley](https://dom-pubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1463-1326.2008.00934.x)), while an earlier review found only small HbA1c advantages and no hypoglycaemia benefit ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0168822706004864)). A 2026 review in children found no clear difference on severe hypoglycaemia, but rated the evidence very uncertain ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42134835/)).

Monitoring technology has shown measurable but modest gains. In the DIAMOND trial, HbA1c fell **1.0% with CGM versus 0.4% with usual care** over 24 weeks ([JAMA](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/2598770)). In a 153-participant pump trial, time below 3.9 mmol/L was **19 versus 31 minutes per day** in the sensor-on arm, and HbA1c returned to baseline after sensing stopped ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22965294/)). Current guidance reflects this. ADA 2026 states that the central principle for type 1 diabetes is some form of insulin, and it recommends CGM for adults on insulin ([ADA Section 9](https://dx.doi.org/10.2337/dc26-S009); [DiabetesontheNet](https://diabetesonthenet.com/diabetes-primary-care/factsheet-2026-ada-standards/)). It also recommends GLP-1-based combinations for adults with type 2 diabetes on insulin ([Guideline Central](https://www.guidelinecentral.com/guideline/14119/)). NICE's February 2026 update to NG28 recommends basal insulin as the first insulin option and suggests considering a switch to a biosimilar at routine reviews ([DiabetesontheNet](https://diabetesonthenet.com/diabetes-primary-care/factsheet-nice-ng28-feb-2026/)). Sources differ on how far SGLT2 inhibitors have moved up in NICE's pathway, with one summary saying they moved to first line for most patients ([Clinilink](https://clinilink.co.uk/nice-ng28-update-2026-key-changes-in-type-2-diabetes-management/)). The NICE text itself was not opened, so its wording should be checked at source. Global access is the main unresolved gap: WHO reports that **59% of adults with diabetes aged 30 and over were not taking medication** in 2022 ([WHO](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/diabetes)).

## Conclusion

The discovery of insulin changed diabetes treatment from near-starvation to replacement therapy, and the first effects were fewer deaths from ketoacidosis and a much longer course of type 1 disease. Those gains depended on purification, reliable supply and, later, recombinant production. The pattern of change matters for interpreting the question: the credit belongs to the Toronto group, while Fleming's documented contribution lies in antibiotics.

The gains are not complete. Hypoglycaemia remains the main cost of intensive insulin therapy, and analogues and devices have so far shown modest HbA1c gains and reduced hypoglycaemia rather than proven reductions in mortality. Several figures, including the first-use date, the pre-insulin survival range and the DCCT effect size, depend on secondary or conflicting sources. The most useful next step is to check primary records, especially the Nobel laureate pages, the NICE NG28 text and the original DKA mortality series.
