# Funding restraint has slowed NHS recovery

Funding for the NHS has grown more slowly than its historical average for most of the period since 2010, and the gains of recent years have been partly offset by short-term and unplanned decisions. Real-terms growth was about 1.3–1.7% a year through the 2010s, against a long-run average of about 3.6% ([IFS](https://ifs.org.uk/books/pressures-nhs); [IFS](https://ifs.org.uk/publications/implications-nhs-workforce-plan)). The COVID-19 years brought a spike of about 4.7% a year, which was then unwound ([IFS](https://ifs.org.uk/publications/implications-nhs-workforce-plan); [IFS](https://ifs.org.uk/publications/past-and-future-uk-health-spending)). The June 2025 Spending Review set NHS England day-to-day growth at 3.0% a year in real terms, and the IFS says this is below the historic average and may not fund the workforce plan ([HM Treasury](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/68627094354985706f111adc/E03349913_HMT_Spending_Review_June_2025_TEXT_CS_Accessible__002_.pdf); [IFS](https://ifs.org.uk/collections/spending-review-2025)). The extra money has bought visible gains on elective waiting lists, mainly through record removals and one-off funding, while A&E, cancer and diagnostics have changed little ([Institute for Government](https://www.instituteforgovernment.org.uk/publication/performance-tracker-2026/nhs)). Capital is now being squeezed, the estates backlog has reached £17.3bn, and most trusts depend on non-recurrent support to balance their books. The causal evidence linking funding to outcomes remains thin and largely observational.

## Real-terms growth has lagged need since 2010

The headline trajectory is one of restraint followed by a temporary surge. The IFS puts UK health spending growth at **1.6% a year in real terms between 2009–10 and 2019–20**, lower than any previous decade in NHS history ([IFS](https://ifs.org.uk/books/pressures-nhs)). Health was protected relative to other departments, so the flat period reflects relative protection rather than generous growth ([IFS](https://ifs.org.uk/health-spending)). The COVID-19 response then added unplanned money. Spending in 2020–21 and 2021–22 ran **10.6% and 9.0% above original plans** ([IFS](https://ifs.org.uk/publications/nhs-funding-resources-and-treatment-volumes)), and the Department of Health and Social Care spent **£63 billion on COVID-19 support in 2020–21** ([IFS](https://ifs.org.uk/books/pressures-nhs)). That money has since been unwound.

| 2010s average | 1.7% a year | [IFS](https://ifs.org.uk/publications/implications-nhs-workforce-plan) |
| 2019–20 to 2022–23 (COVID period) | 4.7% a year | [IFS](https://ifs.org.uk/publications/implications-nhs-workforce-plan) |
| 2025–26 to 2028–29 (NHS England day-to-day, Spending Review) | 3.0% a year | [HM Treasury](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/68627094354985706f111adc/E03349913_HMT_Spending_Review_June_2025_TEXT_CS_Accessible__002_.pdf) |
| 2025–26 to 2028–29 (DHSC total, OBR) | 2.4% a year | [OBR](https://obr.uk/efo/economic-and-fiscal-outlook-november-2025/) |

The sources disagree on the growth rate in the current period, and the differences matter. The Health Foundation puts DHSC total budget growth at **2.8% a year** for 2025/26 to 2028/29 ([Health Foundation](https://www.health.org.uk/features-and-opinion/blogs/budget-2025-preview)), while the OBR's November 2025 forecast gives **2.4%** for a similar period. The NHS Confederation says the autumn figure fell from 2.7% in June, which it attributes to a lower OBR inflation projection ([NHS Confederation](https://www.nhsconfed.org/publications/autumn-budget-2025)). The gap largely reflects different periods and definitions, and the 3% NHS England figure measures a narrower day-to-day budget. Against need, the picture is clearer: the Health Foundation estimates that a **2.5% a year** real increase would be needed to sustain improvement assuming 1% productivity growth ([Health Foundation](https://www.health.org.uk/reports-and-analysis/analysis/spending-review-2025-priorities)), and the IFS estimates the workforce plan requires about **3.6% a year** ([IFS](https://ifs.org.uk/publications/past-and-future-uk-health-spending)). Both sources use a GDP deflator, which the HFMA and IFS argue understates NHS-specific cost pressures ([HFMA](https://www.hfma.org.uk/articles/spending-review-2025-sense-perspective); [IFS](https://ifs.org.uk/sites/default/files/2022-12/NHS-funding-resources-and-treatment-volumes-Institute-for-Fiscal-Studies.pdf)).

Public health has borne a different kind of cut. The public health grant will have **fallen 27% in real terms per person between 2015/16 and 2025/26** ([Health Foundation](https://www.health.org.uk/reports-and-analysis/analysis/spending-review-2025-priorities)). Funding has also been uneven across England, with **planned NHS spending 32% higher per person** in Cheshire and Merseyside than in the lowest-funded area ([IFS](https://ifs.org.uk/publications/past-and-future-uk-health-spending)).

## Waiting lists improved through short-term levers, not sustained capacity

The clearest recent gain is on elective waiting times, but its source is contested. The waiting list was **7.11 million in March 2026**, down 515,000 from July 2024, and 18-week performance reached **65.3%**, the first time the interim target was met ([NHS England](https://www.england.nhs.uk/2026/05/health-service-hits-18-week-target-amid-half-million-waiting-list-drop/); [Health Foundation](https://www.health.org.uk/reports-and-analysis/analysis/progress-but-is-it-enough-what-the-latest-nhs-waiting-times-mean-for)). Since then performance has slipped. The list was **7.38 million in September 2026**, with about 2.52 million waiting over 18 weeks ([Going Private](https://goingprivateuk.co.uk/nhs-waiting-list-september-2026/)).

| Within 18 weeks | 65.3% (March 2026); 65.2% (August 2026) | [Health Foundation](https://www.health.org.uk/reports-and-analysis/analysis/progress-but-is-it-enough-what-the-latest-nhs-waiting-times-mean-for); [Institute for Government](https://www.instituteforgovernment.org.uk/publication/performance-tracker-2026/nhs) |

| Diagnostic waiting list | 1.92 million (July 2026), against 1.01 million in December 2019 | [House of Commons Library](https://researchbriefings.files.parliament.uk/documents/CBP-7281/CBP-7281.pdf) |

The sources disagree on what drove the gain. The Health Secretary called the March fall the biggest single-month cut in 17 years ([NHS England](https://www.england.nhs.uk/2026/05/health-service-hits-18-week-target-amid-half-million-waiting-list-drop/)). The Institute for Government attributes much of it to **removals**, with 21.8 million removals against 21.5 million additions in 2025/26, and to a £120 million sprint fund in early 2026 ([Institute for Government](https://www.instituteforgovernment.org.uk/publication/performance-tracker-2026/nhs)). It also projects **79% within 18 weeks by March 2029** against the 92% target, and 72% if recent trends persist ([Institute for Government](https://www.instituteforgovernment.org.uk/publication/performance-tracker-2026/nhs)). The Public Accounts Committee concluded in November 2025 that the elective programmes **added capacity but did not reduce waiting times as intended** ([Parliament, PAC report HC 820](https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/cm5901/cmselect/cmpubacc/820/report.html)). The NAO found that community diagnostic centres met their test-volume goals ([NAO](https://www.nao.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/nhs-englands-management-of-elective-care-transformation-programme.pdf)). Taken together, the evidence suggests that money bought throughput on the elective list, but not the wider capacity needed for sustained gains.

Other areas have changed little. Major A&E attendances staying longer than four hours were **38.9% in August 2026**, which the Commons Library describes as worse than before 2022 ([House of Commons Library](https://researchbriefings.files.parliament.uk/documents/CBP-7281/CBP-7281.pdf)). Ambulance response times have improved since 2024, though the 2025/26 Category 2 target was narrowly missed ([Institute for Government](https://www.instituteforgovernment.org.uk/publication/performance-tracker-2026/nhs)). The Institute for Government concludes that the focus on elective care left these areas with little improvement. Acute trusts received 1.6% more in real terms in 2025/26, while community trust spending fell 6.3% ([Institute for Government](https://www.instituteforgovernment.org.uk/publication/performance-tracker-2026/nhs)).

## Strain shows in capital, trust deficits and the workforce

Capital has been the first casualty of revenue pressure. The 2025/26 estates backlog in England reached **£17.3bn, up 9.1% in a year**, and the high-risk element rose to £3.7bn ([HFMA](https://www.hfma.org.uk/articles/nhs-england-estates-maintenance-backlog-rises-91-ps173bn)). Capital rose in 2024/25 and 2025/26, but the Autumn 2026 Budget applied a **1% cut to departmental capital** for 2027/28 to 2029/30 to fund defence, with health capital shown falling from £13.8bn to £13.5bn ([Carnall Farrar](https://www.carnallfarrar.com/insights-uk-budget-2026-nhs/)). The sources disagree on the direction of real-terms capital. The Health Foundation projects DHSC capital rising **0.7% a year in real terms** to 2028/29 ([Health Foundation](https://www.health.org.uk/reports-and-analysis/analysis/health-care-funding)), while the NHS Alliance and HFMA describe a flat real-terms settlement ([The NHS Alliance](https://thenhsalliance.org/resources/on-the-day-briefing-spending-review-2025)). The government's claim of a 20%-plus real rise for the DHSC capital budget overall is not reconciled with these readings. The consequences are visible: clinical service incidents caused by estates failures rose by more than 60% in a single year, according to the NHS Alliance ([HFMA](https://www.hfma.org.uk/articles/nhs-england-estates-maintenance-backlog-rises-91-ps173bn)), and in June 2026 several trusts declared critical incidents after heat caused cooling, diagnostic and IT failures ([Digital Health](https://www.digitalhealth.net/2026/06/nhs-hospitals-declare-critical-incidents-as-heat-causes-it-failures/)).

Trust finances now carry the risk. From 2026/27, each trust must **break even on revenue each year** unless exceptional arrangements apply ([NHS England](https://www.england.nhs.uk/long-read/nhs-finance-business-rules-from-2026-27-guidance-for-integrated-care-boards-and-nhs-trusts/)). UNISON's analysis of 202 provider trusts found **deficits above £1.1bn in 2024/25**, offset by surpluses, for an overall deficit of £818m ([UNISON](https://unison.org.uk/press/2026/04/more-20000-nhs-posts-be-axed-over-ps11bn-budget-deficit)). Some local systems are already off plan. The Nottingham and Nottinghamshire system recorded a **£93.1m deficit for April–September 2026, £47.1m behind plan**, with efficiency savings £7.5m short ([Westbridgford Wire](https://westbridgfordwire.com/nottinghamshire-nhs-deficit-reaches-93-1-million-at-halfway-point-of-financial-year/)). Shrewsbury and Telford's breakeven plan relied on **£45.1m of non-recurrent funding** ([SaTH 2025-26 Annual Report](https://www.sath.nhs.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/SaTH-annual-report-and-accounts-signed.pdf)). The Institute for Government notes that trusts have run deficits in every year from 2013/14 to 2024/25 except 2020/21 and 2021/22 ([Institute for Government](https://www.instituteforgovernment.org.uk/publication/performance-tracker-2026/nhs)).

The workforce picture is mixed. The overall vacancy rate fell from **just under 10% in September 2022** to about **6.5–6.7%** in 2026 ([NHS Pay Review Body](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/698df41175466636847f6a93/NHSPRB_39th_Report_2026.pdf); [NHS England Digital](https://digital.nhs.uk/data-and-information/publications/statistical/nhs-vacancies-survey/april-2015---june-2026-experimental-statistics)). DHSC attributes part of this fall to workforce plans being **adjusted to trusts' financial position**, not only to stronger recruitment ([DHSC and NHS England](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6ad21fdaa89985bb970267dc/nhs-pay-review-body-written-evidence-2027-to-2028.pdf)). Total FTE growth has slowed to near zero, with the July 2026 headcount **0.3% lower** than a year earlier ([NHS Workforce Statistics, July 2026](https://digital.nhs.uk/data-and-information/publications/statistical/nhs-workforce-statistics/july-2026)). Pay settled more calmly for Agenda for Change staff, with the **3.3% award** announced on 12 February 2026 and paid on time in April ([NHS Employers](https://www.nhsemployers.org/news/nhs-prb-pay-award-announcement-2026)). Resident doctors took **21 days of strikes** in England between July 2025 and April 2026 before accepting a deal averaging 6.6% ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/resident-doctors-agree-deal-with-government-to-end-strikes); [Daybreak Wire](https://daybreakwire.com/health/england-resident-doctors-accept-pay-deal-end-strikes/)).

Costs have shifted rather than fallen. Agency spending dropped from **£3.46bn in 2022/23 to £2.07bn in 2024/25** after reduction targets were set ([House of Commons Library](https://researchbriefings.files.parliament.uk/documents/CBP-10539/CBP-10539.pdf)). The Recruitment and Employment Confederation argues that bank shifts often cost more than agency cover, and that bank spending exceeded agency spending overall between 2020 and 2025 ([Healthcare Management](https://www.healthcare-management.uk/nhs-bank-shifts-cost-agency-cover-rec-claims)). The government says the NHS saved £1bn in 2024/25 by using bank rather than agency staff ([Personnel Today](https://www.personneltoday.com/hr/rec-says-bank-staff-at-nhs-as-expensive-as-agency-workers/)). These claims cannot both be fully correct on the evidence found. Training is constrained as well. Specialty applications rose from about 12,000 for 9,000 places in 2019 to **over 30,000 for near 10,000 places in 2025** ([GOV.UK](https://gov.uk/government/news/government-to-prioritise-uk-medical-graduates-for-training-places)). The Medical Training (Prioritisation) Act cut the share of accepted places going to non-priority applicants **from 28% in 2025 to 1.8% in 2026** ([Nuffield Trust](https://www.nuffieldtrust.org.uk/news-item/new-law-has-dramatically-reduced-overseas-doctors-chances-of-securing-nhs-specialty-training-places)). That changed who gets posts without creating more of them.

## Devolved budgets diverge under the Barnett formula

For the devolved nations, the link between English health spending and local NHS budgets runs through the Barnett formula, which **adjusts block grants** allocated by the Treasury ([Institute for Government](https://www.instituteforgovernment.org.uk/article/explainer/barnett-formula)). The devolved governments then decide how much to spend on health ([House of Commons Library](https://researchbriefings.files.parliament.uk/documents/SN00724/SN00724.pdf)). Budget 2026 added **£1.3 billion** to the devolved block grants through Barnett over the Spending Review period, including **£600m for Scotland** and **£450m for Wales** ([HM Treasury](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6ae20b85460c1346f7be784e/E03668986_HMT_Budget_2026_ELAY__2_.pdf)). Those additions do not guarantee matching NHS growth. Scotland's health and social care resource budget is planned to rise **just 0.2% in real terms in 2026–27**, and the territorial NHS budget by 0.4% a year ([IFS](https://ifs.org.uk/news/scottish-budget-and-spending-review-signal-big-shifts-health-and-investment-spending-reliance)). Over the last 15 years, health spending per person rose **18% in real terms in Scotland, against 28% in England** ([IFS](https://ifs.org.uk/publications/public-service-spending-and-performance-scotland)).

Northern Ireland faces a specific risk. Its Fiscal Council warned of a **"cliff-edge" drop in funding in 2026–27** without changes to the formula and proposed moving funding towards relative need ([NI Fiscal Council](https://www.nifiscalcouncil.org/files/nifiscalcouncil/documents/2024-02/NI%20public%20finances%20and%20the%20UK%20Government's%20financial%20support%20package%20for%20the%20restored%20Executive%20-%20%20web%20version%2015.02.24.pdf)). A Lords minister claimed the Executive receives at least 24% more per person than equivalent UK spending, but this is a ministerial claim, not an independent assessment ([Hansard](https://hansard.parliament.uk/Lords/2025-11-12/debates/080ECCA4-FFBE-4074-96DF-E4695343305E/BarnettFormulaWales)). The joint review of the Barnett formula was due in early 2026, and no outcome was found. Wales spent **£2,632 per head on NHS services in 2020–21** ([Welsh Government](https://www.gov.wales/nhs-expenditure-programme-budgets-april-2020-march-2021)). The sources found no comparable 2024–26 waiting-time or funding figures for the devolved nations, so the performance effects there cannot be judged.

## Outcome evidence is suggestive, not conclusive

The strongest studies linking austerity to patient harm are observational. A BMJ Open study from UCL linked health and social care cuts since 2010 to **nearly 120,000 excess deaths** in England, but the authors state that no firm cause-and-effect conclusion can be drawn ([UCL](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2017/nov/austerity-linked-120000-extra-deaths-england)). The Health Foundation criticised the underlying time-trend projections for assuming past trends continue and lacking patient-level data ([Health Foundation](https://www.health.org.uk/blogs/did-funding-cuts-in-health-and-social-care-budgets-lead-to-excess-mortality-in-england-the-devil-is-in-the-detail)). A 2024 systematic review found consistent harmful effects of macroeconomic austerity on mortality, but rated the certainty of evidence as **low** ([PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11437704/)). A time-series study of NHS allocation from 2007 to 2014 found that funding increases to more deprived areas were associated with reduced male amenable-mortality inequality, though the authors note that the 2012 commissioning reorganisation stalled the effect ([PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6352397/)). A separate analysis concluded that austerity was not directly responsible for the mortality increase, a view that conflicts with the excess-death studies ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/337356239_Austerity_in_the_UK_and_poor_health_were_deaths_directly_affected)).

More recent evidence is incident-based. The NHS Productivity Commission reports that productivity **fell sharply in 2020** and has not returned to its original path ([NHS Productivity Commission](https://www.health.org.uk/sites/default/files/upload/publications/2026/NHS%20Productivity%20Commission_report_FINAL_2.pdf)). The ONS reports healthcare productivity **up 0.7% in the year to June 2026**, but this follows a sharp pandemic fall, and the ONS advises against direct comparison with NHS England's own productivity series ([ONS](https://www.ons.gov.uk/economy/economicoutputandproductivity/publicservicesproductivity/bulletins/publicserviceproductivityquarterlyuk/apriltojune2026); [ONS](https://www.ons.gov.uk/economy/economicoutputandproductivity/publicservicesproductivity/bulletins/publicserviceproductivityquarterlyuk/januarytomarch2026)). Several sources say that more money has not produced proportionate activity. The Nuffield Trust argues that since the pandemic the NHS delivers less acute activity despite more staff and multiple injections of new money, often short-term ([Nuffield Trust](https://www.nuffieldtrust.org.uk/news-item/productivity-in-the-nhs-what-s-getting-in-the-way)). No source isolates how much of the productivity change reflects funding rather than coding, case-mix or workforce factors.

## Conclusion

The evidence points to a funding settlement that has protected the NHS relative to other departments, but has been too slow to rebuild capacity and too unstable to support planning. The main effects are visible in waiting lists, where the gains came from a one-off surge in removals and short-term money, and in the estate, where the £17.3bn backlog is growing even as capital has been flat or falling in real terms. Trusts face a breakeven rule with less room for non-recurrent support, which makes the next two years a test of whether financial discipline and service demand can be reconciled.

For readers judging whether funding changes have worked, the main uncertainty is attribution. The sources agree that the funding path shaped performance, but they do not quantify how much. Future assessments should watch the 2027 Spending Review, the outcome of the Barnett review, and whether the waiting-list improvement holds once one-off money ends. The strongest unresolved question is whether the workforce gains in vacancies reflect better recruitment or smaller funded establishments, which the current data cannot distinguish.
