# Income Shocks and Debt Drive Bankruptcy Filings

The most consistent finding is that **job loss and other income drops are the main trigger for personal bankruptcy**. Keys's US panel work finds households are **2.5 times as likely to file in the year after a job displacement**, with elevated risk lasting two to three years ([Keys, Federal Reserve FEDS 2010-24](https://www.federalreserve.gov/pubs/feds/2010/201024/201024pap.pdf)). Medical problems matter, but estimates of their role are sharply contested: survey-based studies of filers attribute about two-thirds of filings to medical causes, while a causal design linking hospital records to credit reports finds hospital admissions trigger **fewer than 5%** of bankruptcies ([HealthPopuli](https://www.healthpopuli.com/2019/03/18/medical-issues-are-still-the-1-contributor-to-bankruptcy-in-the-u-s/); [AEA](https://pubs.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/aer.20161038)). Divorce shows weaker and more ambiguous links. The gap in medical estimates largely reflects how causation is defined, and the best evidence suggests medical events matter mainly through lost income. Shocks alone rarely produce filings without pre-existing debt, so household debt levels, the legal framework and the economic cycle shape when people file. The latest official US data show filings rising again: non-business filings were **up 11.4% to 594,057** in the 12 months to 30 September 2026 ([uscourts.gov](https://www.uscourts.gov/data-news/judiciary-news/2026/10/22/bankruptcy-filings-increase-114-percent)).

## Job loss is the most consistent trigger, with effects lasting years

Panel and event-study work points to job loss as the adverse event with the clearest link to filing. In the NLSY79 cohort, Keys estimates about **10 extra bankruptcies per 1,000 job losses** in the year after displacement, and in county-level data 1,000 additional job losses are associated with **8 to 11 additional bankruptcies**, rising to **48 per 1,000 in high-unemployment years** ([Keys, FEDS 2010-24](https://www.federalreserve.gov/pubs/feds/2010/201024/201024pap.pdf)). Manufacturing losses are about three times as likely to lead to bankruptcy as non-manufacturing losses, and the effect is roughly seven times larger for Chapter 7 filings ([Keys, FEDS 2010-24](https://www.federalreserve.gov/pubs/feds/2010/201024/201024pap.pdf)). Aggregate evidence points the same way: a state-level US panel finds a 10% rise in unemployment is associated with a **14.4% rise in personal bankruptcies**, though this is a macro association rather than a household effect ([Platt, Demirkan and Platt, SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1524484)). British data reach a similar conclusion, with becoming unemployed the dominant adverse event in the filing decision ([Gumus, IJFE](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ijfe.2859)).

Divorce looks different. In the NLSY, filing probability begins rising one to two years before divorce, and Keys concludes divorce is not a proximate cause. In the three years before filing, **19.5% of filers had a divorce versus 8.4% of non-filers**, which fits pre-existing financial strain better than a trigger ([Keys, FEDS 2010-24](https://www.federalreserve.gov/pubs/feds/2010/201024/201024pap.pdf)). PSID-based studies are weaker and more sensitive to specification. Keys shows that dropping post-treatment controls roughly triples the unemployment coefficient in the Fay, Hurst and White replication ([Keys, FEDS 2010-24](https://www.federalreserve.gov/pubs/feds/2010/201024/201024pap.pdf)). These are single-cohort results, so they may not generalise.

## Medical causes: self-reports versus causal estimates

The medical estimates diverge most sharply. Himmelstein et al. (2019) report that **medical problems contributed to 66.5% of bankruptcies**, with 58.5% citing medical bills and 44.3% illness-related income loss ([HealthPopuli](https://www.healthpopuli.com/2019/03/18/medical-issues-are-still-the-1-contributor-to-bankruptcy-in-the-u-s/)). The Dobkin et al. design, which links California hospital records to credit reports, finds that for insured non-elderly adults admissions raise out-of-pocket spending, unpaid bills, bankruptcy, and lower earnings and credit access, yet admissions still account for **under 5% of all bankruptcies** in their sample ([AEA](https://pubs.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/aer.20161038)). The uninsured show much larger effects, with a **1.4 percentage point rise** in bankruptcy after admission ([Dobkin et al., HBS](https://www.hbs.edu/ris/download.aspx?name=Kluender_Economic+ReviewFeb2018.pdf)). Dobkin and colleagues argue that earlier estimates of about 60% rest on survey data of filers ([Cato Institute summary](https://www.cato.org/blog/study-medical-expenses-cause-close-4-personal-bankruptcies-not-60)).

Neither side is fully settled. Fay, Hurst and White found no significant health effect in their main PSID specification ([Health Affairs 2006 PDF](https://minnesota.publicradio.org/features/2009/06/newscut_singlepayer/BankruptcyDranoveMillensonhlthaffFeb2006.pdf)), and Keys's disability estimate is small, about **7 extra bankruptcies per 1,000 cases** ([Keys, FEDS 2010-24](https://www.federalreserve.gov/pubs/feds/2010/201024/201024pap.pdf)). Finkelstein suggests that health shocks work largely by costing people their jobs, which then drives filing ([ScienceDaily, MIT](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2018/03/180321173955.htm)). That reading reconciles the two camps: illness is a common source of lost income, even where hospital bills alone are not enough to cause a filing. Critics also argue that Dobkin et al. excluded people hospitalised in the three years before the study window, a high-risk group. This is a critic's claim, not an established finding ([PNHP](http://pnhp.org/news/again-medical-bankruptcy-is-not-a-myth/)).

## What filers say: income loss tops survey reasons

Survey data on stated reasons consistently rank income loss first, though percentages are multi-coded and vary by wave and wording. The table summarises the main series.

|---|---|---|---|---|

| CBP Phase II, 1991 filers | 67.5% cite lower labour income | 20% (third most cited) | Not reported | [Cleveland Fed WP 2409](https://www.clevelandfed.org/-/media/project/clevelandfedtenant/clevelandfedsite/publications/working-papers/2024/wp2409.pdf); [Missouri law repository](https://pdf.core.ac.uk/corefilesystem/pdf/3531/232601312.pdf) |

| Canada, Consumer Debtor Profile 2024 | 45% | 20% | 11% | [ISED/OSB 2024](https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/office-superintendent-bankruptcy/en/statistics-and-research/canadian-consumer-debtor-profile-2024) |

The US and Canadian patterns agree that income loss leads and medical reasons come second. The Canadian official profiles define loss of income and medical reasons broadly and state that each may be one factor in insolvency, so they are self-reported contributors rather than adjudicated causes. Other studies rank differently: a PSID survey found 23% of bankrupts cited job loss, 20% illness and 17% divorce ([White, NBER WP 13265](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w13265/w13265.pdf)). Sources also conflict on the 55% figure. Himmelstein et al. (2005) reported 55% of filers cited illness, injury or medical bills, not job loss ([White, NBER WP 13265](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w13265/w13265.pdf)), so it should not be read as a job-loss share. Some Canadian studies rank credit overextension first: a UBC-hosted study finds overextension of credit at 22% as the primary cause ([UBC Allard Institute](https://commons.allard.ubc.ca/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1314&context=fac_pubs)), and a licensed trustee's client sample reports 62% for over-extension and mismanagement against 31% for job-related causes ([Hoyes Michalos](https://www.hoyes.com/press/joe-debtor/causes-of-insolvency/)). That single-firm sample is not an official survey, and the coding frameworks differ.

## Debt, law and the cycle shape when people file

Income shocks explain who gets pushed toward filing, but debt and rules explain timing and volume. Aggregate US household debt reached **$19.1 trillion in Q3 2026**, and credit card balances were **$1.285 trillion**, up $52 billion over the year ([NY Fed press release](https://www.newyorkfed.org/newsevents/news/research/2026/20261110)). The sources show co-movement between debt and filings over time, but none gives a debt-to-income series tied to filing rates. Ausubel argues that credit card defaults and personal bankruptcy filings moved together, and that lenders with market power extended credit to marginal borrowers ([Ausubel, ABLJ](https://www.ausubel.com/creditcard-papers/ablj.pdf)). Zywicki disputes that issuers were simply reckless and notes that issuers often waive penalty charges ([Zywicki, GMU](https://www.law.gmu.edu/assets/files/publications/working_papers/00-22.pdf)). That lending-practice debate remains unresolved, and no source measures the effect of minimum payments or penalty fees on filings.

Legal rules have a clear effect on volume. Gross and Kluender estimate that BAPCPA reduced filings in the two years after implementation, and NBER summarises a decline of **more than a million** filings, steeper for Chapter 7 ([NBER Digest](https://www.nber.org/digest/dec19/bankruptcy-reform-2005-sharply-reduced-filings)). The sources disagree on mechanism. A New York Fed staff report finds that higher filing costs, not the income means test, were the main channel ([NY Fed Staff Report 725](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr725.pdf)). Reform also shifted filers toward Chapter 13: the means test raised the Chapter 13 share by about **3 percentage points** in treated states ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S016517651400264X)). Macroeconomic conditions matter with lags. The Chicago Fed notes that filings rise in recessions but respond with a lag, and that they rose 20% in 1985 during expansion ([Chicago Fed, 1991](https://www.chicagofed.org/publications/chicago-fed-letter/1991/december-52)). A commercial blog reports that filings after 2008 peaked in 2010, more than a year after the recession ended, though this is a secondary source ([PX Media](https://www.pxmediainc.com/bankruptcy-trends-before-and-during-a-recession/)).

Whether structural pressure or individual choices dominate remains unresolved. Warren's *Two-Income Trap* argues that dual-income families have less discretionary income than single-income families did a generation earlier ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Two-Income_Trap)), but a Volokh Conspiracy analysis argues that tax burdens explain more than housing costs ([Volokh Conspiracy](https://volokh.com/2007/08/06/evaluating-the-two-income-trap-hypothesis/)). That critique is a blog analysis, not peer-reviewed work. Keys's structural model treats filing as both strategic and a response to adverse events: households with large shocks may file immediately, while others borrow and wait ([Keys, FEDS 2010-24](https://www.federalreserve.gov/pubs/feds/2010/201024/201024pap.pdf)).

## Filings are rising from a lower base

Recent counts show a sharp rise, but levels remain well below the early-2000s peak. US filings reached **more than 2 million in 2005**, one for every 55 households ([Debt.org](https://www.debt.org/bankruptcy/statistics/)). After BAPCPA took effect, the CRS documents a sharp fall in 2006 ([CRS, RS20777](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/RS20777)). Filings then reached about **1.5 million in 2010** ([CRS](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/RS20777)), though a secondary source puts the September 2010 12-month peak near 1.6 million ([The World Data](https://theworlddata.com/bankruptcy-statistics-in-us/)); the difference reflects measurement window and definition, and the CRS figure is the more authoritative. The 12 months to 30 June 2026 recorded **608,511 total filings, up 12.2%** ([uscourts.gov](https://www.uscourts.gov/data-news/judiciary-news/2026/07/28/bankruptcies-rise-122-percent)). Canada's consumer bankruptcies rose **8.4% to 31,681** in the 12 months to June 2026 ([ISED/OSB, June 2026](https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/office-superintendent-bankruptcy/en/statistics-and-research/insolvency-statistics-canada-june-2026)). A separate OSB figure of 5.9% for all insolvencies in the same period ([ISED/OSB via notes](https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/office-superintendent-bankruptcy/en/statistics-and-research/insolvency-statistics-canada-june-2026)) likely reflects a broader measure, though the sources do not say so explicitly. In England and Wales, **11,894 personal insolvencies** were recorded in September 2026, 9% above the same month a year earlier ([R3](https://www.r3.org.uk/news/latest-insolvency-statistics-demonstrate-need-for-budget-certainty-says-r3/)). One aggregator's headline describes the same month as a decrease, which contradicts its own figures, so R3's wording is preferred. UK monthly figures are also distorted by backlog clearance after a system change ([GOV.UK, February 2026](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/individual-insolvencies-february-2026/commentary-individual-insolvency-statistics-february-2026)).

Demographics show who files. Single women make up about **33% of filers**, and the median age is **49**; the 65-and-over group is the fastest-growing segment ([Debt.org](https://www.debt.org/bankruptcy/statistics/)). Black households file at **more than twice their population share**, according to CBP data for 2013–2019 cases ([Fredrikson](https://www.fredlaw.com/the-restructuring-report/new-analysis-of-consumer-bankruptcy-filers)). Measurement limits apply throughout. Surveys undercount filers: Keys reports that the PSID's bankruptcy rate is roughly half the national rate ([Keys, FEDS 2010-24](https://www.federalreserve.gov/pubs/feds/2010/201024/201024pap.pdf)). Scholars also disagree on what counts as medical debt; a $5,000 threshold cut the share of medical bankruptcies by about seven percentage points ([Missouri law repository](https://pdf.core.ac.uk/corefilesystem/pdf/3531/232601312.pdf)).

## Conclusion

The evidence supports a combined account. People file most often after losing income, and job loss has the most consistent, sizeable association with filing. Medical problems operate mainly through lost work and then unpaid bills, which explains why survey estimates of two-thirds and causal estimates under 5% can both be partly right: they measure different things. Divorce and other personal events look less decisive once pre-existing financial strain is accounted for. Debt levels and legal rules determine how many shocks become filings, and the 2005 reform shows that procedural costs can reduce volume substantially.

For policy, the implication is that income protection, including unemployment and disability insurance, may do more to reduce filings than measures aimed only at medical bills. Medical-debt policy alone would address only part of the pathway. For analysts, the main gap is a current, causal decomposition of filings by trigger, which the sources do not provide. Survey data are dated and multi-coded, and the best-identified designs cover a single cohort or state. Current official data show rising filings in the US, Canada and England and Wales, but no source yet establishes whether the 2026 rise is driven mainly by job losses, medical costs or accumulated debt.
