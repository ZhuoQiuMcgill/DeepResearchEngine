# Insulin Turned Fatal Diabetes Into Managed Disease

Alexander Fleming did not discover insulin. The 1923 Nobel Prize for insulin went to Frederick Banting and John Macleod, working with Charles Best and James Collip at the University of Toronto ([NobelPrize.org, 1923 presentation speech](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/medicine/1923/ceremony-speech/)). Fleming's Nobel was for penicillin, shared with Ernst Chain and Howard Florey in 1945 ([NobelPrize.org, 1945](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/medicine/1945/florey/facts/)). Before insulin, treatment was restrictive diet, and children with type 1 diabetes usually died within a few years of diagnosis ([Biology Insights](https://biologyinsights.com/how-did-diabetics-survive-before-insulin/)). After insulin, the change was largest for type 1: a Pittsburgh registry reported 10-year survival above 90% for patients diagnosed in the 1930s ([Diabetes Care, Allegheny registry](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/24/5/823/23641/Mortality-Trends-in-Type-1-DiabetesThe-Allegheny)). Type 2 treatment changed later, through oral drugs first and insulin as an add-on, and the evidence for its mortality benefit remains mixed. Several figures in this history come from secondary summaries, so the exact survival numbers should be read as estimates.

## Fleming's documented work lay in antibacterial substances, not insulin

Fleming's recognised discoveries were **lysozyme, an antimicrobial substance found in tissues and secretions, reported in 1922**, and **penicillin, dated to September 1928** ([Royal College of Physicians](https://history.rcp.ac.uk/inspiring-physicians/sir-alexander-fleming); [National Museum of Australia](https://digital-classroom.nma.gov.au/defining-moments/nobel-prize-awarded-developing-penicillin)). In his 1945 Nobel speech, Fleming himself linked the lysozyme work to penicillin ([Nobel banquet speech, 1945](https://krishna.gs.washington.edu/content/members/cholilee/sequencing_files/alumni_akash/vol7/fun/1945_Fleming_penicillinspeech.html)). No source places him in insulin or pancreatic-extract work. The name also appears in diabetes literature, but a different person is meant: a **1986–98 FDA reviewer, now CEO of Kinexum, who oversaw clinical review of early biotech products including human insulin** ([Diabetes Technology Society](https://www.diabetestechnology.org/ddc/bios/fleming.html)). That was regulatory work decades after 1922.

## Who found insulin, and the priority dispute

The standard account credits the Toronto team. **Banting and Best began experiments under Macleod on 17 May 1921** ([Canadian Encyclopedia](https://thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/the-discovery-of-insulin)), and Diabetes UK states that insulin was isolated at Toronto in 1921 by Banting, Best and Macleod, and purified by Collip ([Diabetes UK](https://www.diabetes.org.uk/our-research/about-our-research/our-impact/discovery-of-insulin)). A 2022 review concludes that **Zülzer (1908) and Paulescu (1921) meet the priority rule for the antidiabetic hormone** ([PMC9852216](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9852216)), and another review argues that their work and Collip's purification should be weighed in any credit allocation ([ScienceDirect pancreatic extracts review](https://www.elsevier.es/en-revista-avances-diabetologia-326-articulo-from-pancreatic-extracts-artificial-pancreas-S1134323010650132)). This report follows the Toronto attribution, because the Nobel committee made that award and the clinical treatment of patients depended on Toronto's repeatable preparation; a 2021 timeline says Paulescu's results preceded Toronto's publications but did not become a usable human treatment ([historiamedica](https://historiamedica.org/timeline/insulin-therapy/)). The dispute is really about whether "discovery" means the first animal result or the first therapy, and the evidence does not settle that definition.

Even within Toronto, credit is contested. The 1923 Nobel went only to Banting and Macleod, which caused lasting resentment, and the two laureates shared their halves with Best and Collip ([Science History Institute](https://www.sciencehistory.org/education/scientific-biographies/frederick-banting-charles-best-james-collip-and-john-macleod/); [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2666970621000494)). The first human patient, Leonard Thompson, received an extract on 11 January 1922, and a second, Collip-purified dose on 23 January restored his blood glucose ([Breakthrough T1D Australia](https://breakthrought1d.org.au/what-is-t1d/history-type-1-diabetes/)).

## Before insulin, diabetes meant starvation and early death

Before 1922, the leading treatment was restrictive dieting. Between 1915 and 1922 fasting and prolonged undernourishment were promoted to relieve symptoms and extend life, not to cure disease ([Nutrition & Diabetes](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3062586/)). Some patients starved to death on these regimens, and the Springer review of the period concludes that fasting probably extended life little for children, given very high child mortality in a reanalysis of the Rockefeller series ([Springer, Nutrition & Diabetes](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/1475-2891-10-23)). Survival estimates for children vary: one secondary summary gives "months, or at most one to three years" ([ScienceInsights](https://scienceinsights.org/how-did-diabetics-survive-before-insulin/)), and the Allegheny registry paper describes onset before insulin as almost certain death ([Diabetes Care](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/24/5/823/23641/Mortality-Trends-in-Type-1-DiabetesThe-Allegheny)). Type 1 and type 2 were not yet distinguished in this era ([Nutrition & Diabetes](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/50374332_Why_were_starvation_diets_promoted_for_diabetes_in_the_pre-insulin_period)). The Nobel presentation speech of 1923 already cautioned that insulin cannot cure diabetes ([NobelPrize.org](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/medicine/1923/ceremony-speech/)).

## Insulin transformed type 1 survival but not its long-term burden

Insulin became purchasable in 1923, and in 1926 the UK Medical Research Council recommended daily injections timed around meals ([Breakthrough T1D Australia](https://breakthrought1d.org.au/what-is-t1d/history-type-1-diabetes/)). Survival gains followed. In the Allegheny County registry, **cumulative survival was 98.0% at 10 years, 92.1% at 20 years and 79.6% at 30 years** ([Diabetes Care](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/24/5/823/23641/Mortality-Trends-in-Type-1-DiabetesThe-Allegheny)). In a nationwide cohort, the standardised mortality ratio at 20 years fell from **3.5 for those diagnosed in 1970–74 to 1.9 for those diagnosed in 1985–89**, though the authors also flagged rising risk for people diagnosed at ages 15–29 ([PMC3169676](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3169676/)). The Pittsburgh cohort reported a **4–6 year life-expectancy loss for those diagnosed in the late 1960s and 1970s, against more than 17 years for those diagnosed in the 1950s and early 1960s**, and linked the improvement to better glucose monitoring and insulin administration ([Diabetes](https://diabetesjournals.org/diabetes/article/61/11/2987/33742/Improvements-in-the-Life-Expectancy-of-Type-1)). Even so, a Finnish study found life expectancy at age 20 remained **almost 10 years below the general population in 2017** ([Diabetes Research and Clinical Practice](https://www.diabetesresearchclinicalpractice.com/article/S0168-8227(23)00055-4/fulltext)).

For type 2, the change came later and is harder to measure. Sulfonylureas, used since the 1950s, were the first widely used oral drugs ([American Diabetes Association](https://diabetes.org/health-wellness/medication/oral-other-injectable-diabetes-medications)), and metformin became the preferred first agent after the UKPDS trial ([Springer, metformin overview](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00125-017-4318-z)). In UKPDS, intensive control with sulfonylurea or insulin reduced microvascular complications by about 25% over a median of 10 years, but the Lancet's legacy report found non-significant reductions in cardiovascular events and mortality ([The Lancet](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lancet/article/PIIS0140-6736(24)01395-3/abstract)). Oxford's summary of the same trial reports 11% fewer deaths, which conflicts with the Lancet's non-significant finding ([Oxford RDM](https://www.rdm.ox.ac.uk/news/intensive-blood-glucose-control-for-people-with-type-2-diabetes-when-they-are-first-diagnosed-reduces-diabetic-complications-and-prolongs-life)). Observational comparisons of insulin users with others, such as a Taiwanese cohort reporting much higher mortality, are confounded by the severity of disease that prompted insulin use ([PMC7939219](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7939219/)).

## Modern insulin is more precise, but access remains uneven

Insulin production moved quickly after 1922. Connaught produced about 250,000 units by the benzoic acid method for clinical use in fall 1922 ([Connaught Laboratories](https://connaught.research.utoronto.ca/about/history/article3)), and Eli Lilly's Iletin went on general sale on 15 October 1923 ([Encyclopedia of Indianapolis](https://indyencyclopedia.org/insulin/)). **Recombinant human insulin was produced by Genentech scientists in 1978** ([PMC6205949](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6205949/)), and **analogue insulin lispro was first approved in 1996** ([e-DMJ](https://www.e-dmj.org/journal/view.php?number=2586)). Device dates are less certain: sources give 1954 and 1985 for the first pen, and 1999 or 2008 for CGM approval ([Novo Nordisk](https://www.novonordisk.com/about/insulin-100-years.html); [Beyond Type 1](https://beyondtype1.org/the-evolution-of-diabetes-technology/); [PMC6205949](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6205949/)). The **2026 ADA Standards recommend automated insulin delivery for all adults with type 1 or type 2 diabetes on insulin** ([Guideline Central](https://www.guidelinecentral.com/guideline/14119/)).

Prices and access diverge sharply. A RAND analysis of 2017–2022 data found **US gross prices more than nine times higher than those in 33 comparison countries** ([RAND](https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RRA788-2.html)), and after rebates the US price remained **2.3 times higher** ([EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1033191)). In March 2023, Lilly, Novo Nordisk and Sanofi announced **list-price cuts of 65% to 80%** ([JAMA Network Open](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2806020); [CNN](https://www.cnn.com/2024/01/01/politics/insulin-price-cap/index.html)). Manufacturers say list prices "do not reflect the actual, net prices" ([Arizona Republic](https://www.pressreader.com/usa/the-arizona-republic/20230321/281629604512422)), so the 2022-based comparisons probably overstate today's gap, though no source quantifies this. By January 2026, **29 states and DC had enacted out-of-pocket caps for state-regulated commercial plans** ([PMC13626181](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13626181)), and **Medicare Part D caps insulin at $35 per month**, but there is no federal cap for commercial insurance ([Covered USA](https://coveredusa.org/en/drug/insulin-35-cap-explained)). A bipartisan bill to extend the $35 cap to commercial plans is pending, not enacted ([Senate press release](https://www.kennedy.senate.gov/2026/05/13/2026-5-kennedy-introduces-bill-to-lock-in-35-insulin-price-cap-for-all-americans-my-bill-saves-money/)).

Globally, the WHO reports that **about 60 million people with type 2 diabetes need insulin but only about half can access it**, and fewer than half of low-income countries have general public-sector availability ([WHO, 2020](https://www.who.int/news-room/detail/25-09-2020-changing-the-game-to-improve-availability-and-affordability-of-quality-assured-insulin-and-associated-devices)). The Access to Medicine Foundation found that **only 29 of 108 countries in scope register all WHO Essential Medicines List insulins**, and only one of those is low-income ([Access to Medicine Foundation](https://accesstomedicinefoundation.org/news/how-the-dominant-global-insulin-producers-must-scale-up-access-efforts-to-reach-people-with-diabetes-worldwide)). Advocacy group T1International argues "affordable" should mean **5% or less of income on insulin and testing supplies**, a standard WHO has not adopted ([T1International](https://www.t1international.com/blog/2023/11/10/advocating-define-affordable-people-diabetes/)). Most cross-country price and availability data date from 2019–2022, so current international prices remain unmeasured.

## Conclusion

The question's premise is wrong, and the correction matters: the insulin story belongs to Banting, Best, Macleod and Collip, while Fleming's place in medical history rests on penicillin. The contested part is priority, and the evidence supports a split verdict: Paulescu and Zülzer have a credible claim to the first animal results, while Toronto's claim rests on a usable therapy that changed patient outcomes.

The clearest effect of insulin was on type 1 diabetes, where it converted a condition that killed children within years into one that allows survival for decades, though with a persistent excess mortality. Type 2 changed in a different way, with oral drugs and later insulin intensification, and the mortality benefit of treatment remains contested. Access is now the central problem. Price cuts and US caps have reduced costs for some patients, but the gaps for uninsured people, commercially insured patients in some plans, and low-income countries remain large, and the most recent international price data are several years old.
