# Darwin Linked Trait Variation to Conditions and Selection

Darwin did not fully explain why individuals differ. He treated individual variation as a fact of nature, saying it **mainly depends on changed conditions of life, but is "governed by infinitely complex and unknown laws"** ([Variation, 1875 2nd ed. vol. 2, Darwin Online](https://darwin-online.org.uk/converted/published/1875_Variation_F880/1875_Variation_F880.2.html)). He then explained how such variation diverged over generations through natural selection, which preserves favourable variations, and through a principle of divergence that turns small differences into varieties and then species ([Origin of Species Ch. 4, Marxists.org](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/darwin/works/origins/ch04.htm)). Sexual selection covered traits such as ornaments and weapons that seemed to have no survival value ([Wikisource, *Descent* ch. VIII](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Descent_of_Man_(Darwin)/Chapter_VIII)). For inheritance, Darwin relied on blending and on inherited use effects, and in 1868 he proposed pangenesis as a provisional mechanism ([ASU, "Charles Darwin's Theory of Pangenesis"](https://embryo.asu.edu/pages/charles-darwins-theory-pangenesis)). Later genetics replaced blending and pangenesis with particulate inheritance, although historians still dispute how central blending was to Darwin's own problem ([Springer, "Darwin and the White Shipwrecked Sailor"](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10739-024-09770-y)). The most important point for a reader is that Darwin's answer had two layers: an open account of where variation starts, and a more developed account of how selection and heredity sort and carry it forward.

## Darwin located variation's source in conditions and unknown laws

Darwin's explanation of the origin of variation was deliberately partial. In the 1859 *Origin*, he pointed to slight "individual differences" among offspring of the same parents and among individuals in one locality ([Origin of Species (1859), vliz.be](https://www.vliz.be/docs/Zeecijfers/Origin_of_Species.pdf)). He described these as "endless slight peculiarities" that cannot be accounted for by inheritance from either parent or a more remote ancestor ([Origin of Species Ch. 1, Marxists.org](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/darwin/works/origins/ch01.htm)). His working idea was that such variations are "the indefinite effects of the conditions of life on each organism," comparable to how a chill affects different people differently ([Origin of Species Ch. 1, sacred-texts.com](https://sacred-texts.com/aor/darwin/origin/or01.htm)).

Darwin's causal scheme separated direct and indirect effects. The 1872 sixth edition says changed conditions are of the highest importance, acting both directly on the organisation and indirectly by affecting the reproductive system ([On the Origin of Species, 6th ed., UNAM](https://labgenmol-fo-unam.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/04/the-origin-of-species_charles-darwin.pdf)). He added further causes: correlated growth, which he thought was probably the most important of the unknown laws, plus use and disuse ([On the Origin of Species, 6th ed., UNAM](https://labgenmol-fo-unam.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/04/the-origin-of-species_charles-darwin.pdf); [Origin of Species Ch. 1, Marxists.org](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/darwin/works/origins/ch01.htm)). In *Variation* he wrote that causes act on the mature organism, the embryo, and both sexual elements before fertilisation ([Variation, Gutenberg vol. 2](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/28897/pg28897-images.html)).

Darwin's own admissions of ignorance were consistent. The *Origin*'s chapter on the laws of variation concludes that "Our ignorance of the laws of variation is profound," a line that also appears in the 1872 edition ([Origin of Species Ch. V (1859), Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/On_the_Origin_of_Species_(1859)/Chapter_V); [Origin of Species Ch. V (1872), Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Origin_of_Species_(1872)/Chapter_V)). In 1868 he wrote that why some animals and plants varied more under domestication than others "we do not know" ([Variation, 1868 vol. 2, Darwin Online](https://darwin-online.org.uk/converted/published/1868_Variation_F878/1868_Variation_F878.2.html)). He also used "chance" for the origin of individual variations but called it "a wholly incorrect expression" that signalled ignorance of the cause ([Origin of Species Ch. V (1859), Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/On_the_Origin_of_Species_(1859)/Chapter_V); [LSU Scholarly Repository](https://repository.lsu.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1009&context=prs_pubs)). Winther's reading of the *Variation* book describes Darwin as an externalist about causes, while Darwin also distinguished direct from indirect effects ([Winther, JHB 2000, PhilArchive](https://philarchive.org/archive/WINDOV)). The wording shifted from "mainly caused" in 1868 to "mainly depends on changed conditions... governed by... unknown laws" in 1875, so the admission of ignorance became more explicit over revisions ([Variation, 1868 vol. 2](https://darwin-online.org.uk/converted/published/1868_Variation_F878/1868_Variation_F878.2.html); [Variation, 1875 2nd ed.](https://darwin-online.org.uk/converted/published/1875_Variation_F880/1875_Variation_F880.2.html)).

## Domestication showed how much variation exists

Darwin used domestication as his main evidence that variation is large and conditional. He argued that breeders could shape breeds by selecting differences too slight for an untrained eye to notice, and that adding these up over time produced great results ([Origin of Species (1859), Hanover Historical Texts](https://history.hanover.edu/courses/excerpts/111dar.html)). His examples included strawberries, where gardeners bred from plants with slightly better fruit, and pigeons, where many slight differences were rejected as faults ([Origin of Species Ch. 1, Friends of Darwin](https://friendsofdarwin.com/docs/origin-1/chapter-01/)). Domestic variability, he concluded, "results from the conditions to which the parents have been exposed" ([Variation, 1868 vol. 2](https://darwin-online.org.uk/converted/published/1868_Variation_F878/1868_Variation_F878.2.html)).

Darwin's range of claims was not uniform. Some domesticated animals varied very little, as with rabbits and ferrets that bred freely in hutches ([Origin of Species (6th ed. text), vliz.be](https://www.vliz.be/docs/Zeecijfers/Origin_of_Species.pdf)). He also held that variation is limited in nature, because individuals are never exposed to completely uniform conditions, and that if they were, variability would disappear ([Variation, 1868 ed. vol. 2, Gutenberg](https://dev.gutenberg.org/files/3332/3332-h/3332-h.htm)). Darwin described the amount of variation as endless in its details, and he stressed that similar varieties can arise under very different conditions while dissimilar ones arise under apparently identical conditions ([Origin of Species Ch. I (1859), Skedbooks](https://skedbooks.com/books/the-origin-of-species-by-means-of-natural-selection-or/chapter-i-variation-under-domestication/); [Origin of Species Ch. V, sacred-texts.com](https://www.sacred-texts.com/aor/darwin/origin/or05.htm)). This made the direction of variation hard to predict from conditions alone.

## Natural selection and divergence sorted variation into varieties and species

Selection was Darwin's main answer to how variation becomes differences between groups. In the 1859 text, a useful variation gives its bearer the best chance of being preserved in the struggle for life, and strong inheritance then makes such individuals produce similar offspring ([Gutenberg, *On the Origin of Species*, ch. IV](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/1228/pg1228-images.html)). The chapter states the principle as "the preservation of favourable variations and the rejection of injurious variations" ([Wikisource, *Origin* (1859) ch. IV](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/On_the_Origin_of_Species_(1859)/Chapter_IV)). The phrase "survival of the fittest" was added from the fifth edition in 1869, and Darwin kept "natural selection" as the main term ([Darwin Project, "Survival of the fittest"](https://www.darwinproject.ac.uk/commentary/survival-fittest); [Darwin Online, Freeman introduction](https://darwin-online.org.uk/EditorialIntroductions/Freeman_OntheOriginofSpecies.html)). Wallace had urged the phrase earlier ([Darwin Project, "Rewriting Origin"](https://www.darwinproject.ac.uk/letters/darwins-works-letters/rewriting-origin-later-editions)). Britannica notes that Darwin preferred "survival of the fitter," because success is relative to the struggle for existence ([Britannica, "survival of the fittest"](https://britannica.com/topic/survival-of-the-fittest)).

Darwin's struggle-for-existence examples were mainly about reproduction and competition. He argued that the struggle is most severe between varieties and individuals of the same species, and that competition is strongest among allied forms that fill nearly the same place in nature ([Gutenberg, *Origin of Species* ch. III](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1228/1228-h/1228-h.htm)). He used the fly example, in which one fly lays hundreds of eggs and another one, to show that fecundity alone does not determine how many can be supported ([Gutenberg, *Origin* (1228-h), ch. III](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1228/1228-h/1228-h.htm)). Isolation appears as a circumstance that affects selection's results ([Marxists.org, *Origin* ch. 4](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/darwin/works/origins/ch04.htm)). The Galápagos cases are usually reported through secondary summaries ([SparkNotes, *Origin* isolation](https://www.sparknotes.com/lit/origin/idea-isolation/); [Christ's College Cambridge, "Speciation"](https://darwin200.christs.cam.ac.uk/speciation)), so their direct role in the 1859 argument is less well documented.

Divergence was the second principle. Darwin argued that more living beings can be supported in the same area the more they diverge in structure, habits and constitution, so the most divergent variations tend to be preserved ([Marxists.org, *Origin* ch. 4](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/darwin/works/origins/ch04.htm); [TalkOrigins, *Origin* ch. 4](https://talkorigins.org/faqs/origin/chapter4.html)). Lesser differences between varieties tend to grow into greater differences between species ([Gutenberg, *Origin* (1228-h)](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1228/1228-h/1228-h.htm)). This, together with extinction of intermediates, was Darwin's explanation for the grouping of organisms. Scholarly readings disagree about the logic. Uchii argues that Darwin said two contradictory things about whether divergence follows from natural selection ([Uchii, "Darwin's Principle of Divergence"](https://philsci-archive.pitt.edu/1781/1/PrDiv.pdf)).

## Sexual selection explained traits that survival alone could not

Darwin needed a second agency for traits that seemed to have no survival value. In the 1859 text, sexual differences in structure, colour or ornament were mainly due to sexual selection, which gives males advantages in weapons, defence or charms ([Wikisource, *Origin* (1859) ch. IV](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/On_the_Origin_of_Species_(1859)/Chapter_IV)). Darwin called it "less rigorous" than natural selection, because it does not entail death but gives fewer offspring to less favoured males ([Darwin Online, *Origin* 1st ed.](https://darwin-online.org.uk/converted/published/1859_Origin_F373/1859_Origin_F373.html)). The *Descent* (1871) developed the theory at length. It treated female choice as a real process, with females generally accepting one male in preference to others ([Wikisource, *Descent* ch. VIII](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Descent_of_Man_(Darwin)/Chapter_VIII)). The examples included the peacock's train, male spurs found only in males of one species, and bright male colours ([Darwin Online, *Descent* 2nd ed. (1874)](https://darwin-online.org.uk/converted/published/1874_Descent_F944/1874_Descent_F944.html); [Darwin Online, *Descent* vol. 2 (1871)](https://darwin-online.org.uk/converted/published/1871_Descent_F937/1871_Descent_F937.2.html)). Antlers were treated as weapons that decide access to females ([PMC, "Explaining sex"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12837013/)).

Darwin's own position included admitted costs. He suggested that the long peacock tail could be inconvenient to females during incubation, and he admitted that the tail could be slightly harmful to the male ([Darwin Online, *Descent* vol. 2 (1871)](https://darwin-online.org.uk/converted/published/1871_Descent_F937/1871_Descent_F937.2.html); [Science News, "A Most Private Evolution"](https://www.sciencenews.org/article/most-private-evolution)). Later empirical work disputes whether the peacock train reliably signals male condition, which is an assessment after Darwin rather than his own view ([PMC, "Explaining sex"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12837013/)). Secondary sources note that Darwin placed more weight on sexual selection than many of his contemporaries while still treating it as relatively weak ([Wikipedia, "Sexual selection"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sexual_selection)).

## Heredity carried variation, and its mechanisms were revised

Darwin's account of inheritance was the weakest link, and it was where his explanation was later replaced. He treated inheritance as blending, so offspring took an intermediate form of parental traits ([ScienceDirect, "Blending Inheritance – an overview"](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/biochemistry-genetics-and-molecular-biology/blending-inheritance)). He also accepted that the effects of use and disuse could be inherited. In Chapter V of the *Origin*, use strengthens and enlarges parts, disuse diminishes them, and such changes are inherited ([Arxiv 2305.00491](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.00491)). In 1868 Darwin offered pangenesis to explain how these acquired changes reached the next generation. Cells were said to throw off gemmules that gather in the reproductive elements ([Vision.org, "Darwin's Gemmules"](https://www.vision.org/darwins-gemmules-177)). Darwin called this a provisional hypothesis, and it had no experimental support ([ASU, "Charles Darwin's Theory of Pangenesis"](https://embryo.asu.edu/pages/charles-darwins-theory-pangenesis)).

Fleeming Jenkin's 1867 review gave the classic objection. Jenkin argued that under blending, a rare beneficial variation would be diluted away before selection could act ([Wikipedia, "Blending inheritance"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blending_inheritance); [Utah reading guide, "Jenkin's 'swamping' argument"](https://content.csbs.utah.edu/~rogers/tch/evidevol/readings/swamping.pdf)). Darwin responded by adding a passage in a later edition of the *Origin* ([Wikipedia, "Swamping argument"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swamping_argument)). Revisionist historians argue that Jenkin's real problems concerned intercrossing and reversion, and that Darwin was probably mostly unimpressed by the review ([PubMed 38656677](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38656677/)). The two accounts conflict, and the classic version should be read with that caveat.

Mendel's particulate model replaced blending. Mendel's principles were rediscovered around 1900 by de Vries and Correns ([graphsearch.epfl.ch](https://graphsearch.epfl.ch/en/concept/665725/concepts?page=1)). Weismann's germ plasm theory, framed against pangenesis, removed the gemmule mechanism ([MDPI, "What Is Lost in the Weismann Barrier?"](https://www.mdpi.com/2221-3759/8/4/35)). Whether Darwin knew of Mendel's work is disputed. One source says Darwin was "almost certainly unaware" of it ([Nature Ecology & Evolution](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41559-022-01834-8)), while another reports evidence that Mendel read the *Origin*, a claim that is contested ([ScienceDirect, "Darwin and Mendel"](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0065266018300063)).

Some reassessments give pangenesis-related ideas a partial vindication. A review argues that pangenesis should not be dismissed as Darwin's "biggest mistake," citing evidence for heritable changes induced by blood transfusion, graft hybridization and xenia ([PubMed 30037392](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30037392/)). A 2022 article argues Darwin accepted inheritance of acquired characters as a subsidiary aid ([OUP, 2022](https://academic.oup.com/ije/article/45/6/2206/2617243)). Weismann's argument, once considered decisive, is now seen as not addressing use and disuse ([Wikipedia, "Lamarckism"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lamarckism)). These points show that Darwin's heredity ideas were reassessed, not that the gemmule mechanism itself has a modern counterpart.

## Conclusion

Darwin's explanation of trait differences combined an open account of origins with a more developed account of sorting and inheritance. The source of individual variation remained, in his own words, governed by unknown laws, and his admissions of ignorance did not diminish across editions ([Origin of Species Ch. V (1859), Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/On_the_Origin_of_Species_(1859)/Chapter_V)). The explanatory power of his theory lay in selection, divergence and sexual selection, which together explain how small differences grow into varieties and species. The weakness lay in heredity, where blending and pangenesis were later superseded by Mendelian genetics ([graphsearch.epfl.ch](https://graphsearch.epfl.ch/en/concept/665725/concepts?page=1)).

For a modern reader, the lesson is that Darwin's framework has held up better than his mechanisms of heredity. Selection on heritable variation remains the core idea, while the source of variation is now explained by genetic mutation and recombination rather than by conditions of life and gemmules. Historians still dispute how much Darwin relied on inherited use effects and how seriously he took Jenkin's objection, so claims about his "true" position should be made cautiously.
