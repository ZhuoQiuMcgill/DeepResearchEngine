# No law names sociopaths, but conduct-based protections already exist

The question's premise is not supported: laws already protect people from repeated harm by individuals, regardless of diagnosis. "Sociopath" is not a legal category, and no statute in the US, UK or EU names it. Stalking, harassment, coercive-control and protection-order regimes apply to the conduct, whatever the offender's personality ([The Conversation, 2026](https://theconversation.com/why-psychopath-is-a-dangerous-label-when-it-comes-to-criminal-justice-278133); [Psychology Today, September 2026](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/so-sue-me/202609/the-new-state-laws-defining-abuse-without-a-single-bruise)). The narrower claim does hold: no law targets people with antisocial traits as such. The reasons are both legal and scientific. Law punishes what people do rather than what they are, and psychopathy does not remove criminal responsibility in current US practice ([Morse, Penn Law chapter](https://scholarship.law.upenn.edu/faculty_chapters/106/)). Trait-based detention also faces contested prediction, a disputed "untreatability" assumption, and strict European human-rights limits. Attempts to build such laws, notably England's DSPD programme, were shelved or narrowed. The real gaps are at the edges: manipulation short of a crime, people who have not yet offended, and coercive control outside intimate relationships.

## Existing protections are conduct-based and uneven

Most protection is triggered by behaviour, and the reach differs sharply by jurisdiction. US federal stalking covers a course of conduct with intent to harass or intimidate, but **coercive control is not a federal crime**, and **Hawaii is the only state to have criminalised it**, as a petty misdemeanor ([Womenslaw.org](https://www.womenslaw.org/laws/federal/statutes/18-usc-ss-2261a-stalking); [Coparenting Guide, 2026](https://coparentingguide.org/coercive-control-custody/); [Psychology Today, September 2026](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/so-sue-me/202609/the-new-state-laws-defining-abuse-without-a-single-bruise)). Sources disagree on how many other states have coercive-control laws: one lists **eleven** states ([Narcissistic Abuse Rehab, May 2026](https://www.narcissisticabuserehab.com/coercive-control-legislation-around-the-world/)), while another names **four** explicit states, California, Connecticut, Hawaii and Washington ([Coparenting Guide](https://coparentingguide.org/coercive-control-custody/)). No official register resolves the conflict, so the four-state figure is the safer count of explicit statutes.

England and Wales is more developed. Section 76 of the Serious Crime Act 2015 makes controlling or coercive behaviour an offence, with a **maximum of five years**, but only within **intimate or family relationships** ([Policing.uk](https://policing.uk/offences/controlling-coercive-behaviour)). The **stalking protection order** does not need a conviction: a court must be satisfied of acts associated with stalking, a risk to another person, and necessity ([legislation.gov.uk, Stalking Protection Act 2019, s.2](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2019/9/section/2/enacted)). The EU Directive 2024/1385 criminalises cyberstalking and cyberharassment and allows emergency barring and protection orders, but commentators say it "does not address the full spectrum" of violence against women ([EUR-Lex summary](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/EN/legal-content/summary/preventing-and-combating-violence-against-women-and-domestic-violence-throughout-the-european-union.html); [EUR-Lex recital text](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=OJ%3AL_202401385)). It must be implemented by **June 2027** ([European Parliament briefing, 2024](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/RegData/etudes/BRIE/2024/767146/EPRS_BRI(2024)767146_EN.pdf)).

## Why no sociopath-specific law has passed

The main legal reason is that criminal law punishes actions, not character. Morse argues that the criminal law should punish for actions and that punishing for character alone would be unjust ([Illinois Law Review](https://illinoislawreview.org/wp-content/ilr-content/articles/2004/2/Morse.pdf)). Current US law holds that neither psychopathy nor ASPD excuses crime, and they are not sufficient mental abnormalities for involuntary civil commitment ([Morse, Penn repository](https://repository.law.upenn.edu/Documents/Detail/499606)). The English law changed the same way: the **Mental Health Act 2007 removed "psychopathic disorder"** as one of the four categories of mental disorder ([legislation.gov.uk, Mental Health Act 2007](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2007/12/pdfs/ukpga_20070012_en.pdf)).

The scientific basis for trait-based restriction is contested rather than absent. A 2023 umbrella review pooled 17 effect sizes from about 77,000 participants and found a **pooled correlation of r = .284** between psychopathy and dangerousness, while its authors note the relationship is contested ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36608488/); [ScienceDirect abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0272735822001258)). That is a real but partial signal, not a basis for individual prediction. A 2026 review of violence in non-affective psychosis found that **higher PCL-R scores** were among few predictors, and neither survived Bonferroni correction ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41747436/)). The "untreatability" assumption is also disputed. Scholars argue it has discouraged commitment, and that outpatient commitment is less controversial than inpatient commitment ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0160252711001117)). Treatment evidence is mixed: one review found little evidence that targeting traits reduces recidivism, though changes in criminogenic needs are linked to lower recidivism ([ResearchGate, Olver review](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/333499403_Treatment_of_psychopathic_offenders_Evidence_issues_and_controversies)).

Stigma and cost have also weighed against new laws. Commentators warn that the label can distort sentencing and produce overconfident predictions, and that in England judges should not lengthen sentences because of a psychopathy description, although the label can still enter through expert reports ([Phys.org, June 2026](https://phys.org/news/2026-06-psychopath-dangerous-criminal-justice.html)).

## Proposals were shelved, and courts limit trait-based detention

Proposals have been tried and mostly stalled. England's DSPD programme ran from 2001 to 2013, and a Cambridge chapter reports that **attempts to enshrine it in law all failed** ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dangerous_and_severe_personality_disorder); [Cambridge, Mind, State and Society](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/mind-state-and-society/origins-of-the-dangerous-and-severe-personality-disorder-programme-in-england/7DE2EBB0314A3D0E314D576B83505A8D)). Its cost is disputed: Channel 4 News reported about **£200m**, while a later review gave about **£500m** ([Channel 4 News](https://www.channel4.com/news/dangerous-offenders-scheme-to-be-axed); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/275895870_The_lessons_and_legacy_of_the_programme_for_dangerous_and_severe_personality_disorders_Lessons_and_legacy_of_the_DSPD_programme)). The Butler Committee's 1975 proposal and a 1989 Law Commission draft bill were also ignored ([Wikipedia, Butler Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Butler_Committee)). The Law Commission's 2013 insanity proposals did not treat antisocial personality disorder as a qualifying condition ([Strathclyde student law review](https://www.strath.ac.uk/media/1newwebsite/departmentsubject/law/documents/studentlawreview/fourthedition/Nicolson.pdf)).

The clearest US action was narrower. **Oklahoma** redefined the insanity plea to exclude people with antisocial personality disorder, signed in May 2016, and NPR reports that "a number of states" did something similar ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/sections/health-shots/2016/08/03/486669552/does-a-psychopath-who-kills-get-to-use-the-insanity-defense)). New York Senate Bill S7794 would have added antisocial personality disorder and psychopathy to the sex-offender commitment definition. It passed committee 11–0 in June 2016, but its final status is not confirmed by the sources ([NY State Senate](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/bills/2015/2015-s7794)).

Courts accept personality-based commitment only under strict conditions. In *Kansas v. Hendricks* (1997), the Supreme Court upheld civil commitment for a "mental abnormality" or "personality disorder" linked to predatory sexual violence ([Justia](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/521/346)). In *Kansas v. Crane*, the Court said an absolute lack-of-control requirement would risk barring commitment of highly dangerous people ([Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/00-957.ZO.html)). European courts are more protective. In *W.A. v. Switzerland* (2021), the ECHR found a violation where a man with a difficult-to-treat personality disorder was held in preventive detention in an unsuitable institution ([ECHR press release](https://hudoc.echr.coe.int/app/conversion/pdf/?library=ECHR&id=003-7167836-9726000&filename=Judgment+W.A.+v.+Switzerland+-+Violations+in+preventive+detention+of+mentally+ill+serious+offender.pdf)). In *K.A. v. Austria* (19 May 2026), the Court found no violation, but it stressed that a severe, objectively established disorder remains indispensable and that a narcissistic personality structure alone is not enough ([ECHRCaseLaw](https://www.echrcaselaw.com/en/echr-decisions/k-a-v-austria-forensic-therapeutic-confinement-domestic-violence-and-the-autonomous-meaning-of-mental-disorder-under-article-5-echr/)). The English Mental Health Act 2025 received Royal Assent on 18 December 2025, but one secondary source says only sections 51 and 52 had commenced by August 2026 ([CivilHelp](https://civilhelp.co.uk/mental-health-law/mental-health-act-reform-status)). That commencement status rests on a single source and should be checked.

## Conclusion

The question assumes a legal vacuum that does not exist. Protection from repeated harm is already available through conduct-based offences and civil orders, and the most useful reform question is where those tools stop. The sharpest gaps are coercive control outside intimate relationships, most US states' lack of criminal coercive-control offences, and the protection of people who have not yet offended. Closing these would target behaviour, which is where existing law already places its focus.

A law aimed at sociopaths as a class faces three obstacles. It would conflict with the principle that punishment follows conduct, it would rest on a prediction and treatment science that is partial and contested, and it would meet strict constraints under European human-rights law. The history of DSPD, the 2007 removal of "psychopathic disorder", and the stalled bills show that these obstacles have so far outweighed the pressure for reform.
