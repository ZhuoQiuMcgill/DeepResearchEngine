# Income Shocks and Medical Crises Drive Most Filings

Most people who file for personal bankruptcy in the US are hit by an income shock, a serious illness or a family breakdown, and these causes often overlap. Surveys of filers consistently put **income loss in roughly two-thirds to four-fifths of cases**, with medical problems close behind ([debt.org](https://www.debt.org/bankruptcy/statistics/)). The size of the medical share is the most contested point. Survey-based estimates range from **46% to 67%** of filings, while a DOJ study and a rebuttal reject a large medical role. Overspending has one supporting study, from a single state in 2003, and recent evidence does not show it as the main cause. Filings are rising again: US court counts reached **608,511** cases in the 12 months to June 2026, up 12.2% ([usbankruptcyhelp.com, citing AOUSC](https://www.usbankruptcyhelp.com/current-bankruptcy-filing-statistics)). The evidence is mostly US-based and dated, and no source measures the causes of 2026 filings directly.

## Surveys of filers name income loss and family breakdown first

Income loss is the most frequently cited trigger. In the Consumer Bankruptcy Project (CBP) survey, **78% of filers cited a decline in income and 65% cited medical issues** ([debt.org](https://www.debt.org/bankruptcy/statistics/)), though these figures come from a secondary summary. Among families with children in the CBP sample, **70% said they filed after losing a job, a pay cut or an unexpected income drop**, with a sudden family medical condition second and divorce third ([Federal Reserve Bank of Boston](https://www.bostonfed.org/-/media/Documents/cb/PDF/Bankruptcies.pdf)). Warren and Tyagi report that **87% of these families cited one or more of three reasons: job loss, medical problems, or divorce or separation** ([Caucus 99 summary of The Two-Income Trap](https://caucus99percent.com/content/resilience-two-income-trap-reflections-time-and-money)). A Maine Law Review note on a nationwide survey of recent filers ranked medical bills first at 26% agreement, with loss of job second ([Maine Law Review](https://mainelawreview.org/2015/04/08/medical-debt-as-a-cause-of-consumer-bankruptcy/)). Because these surveys let respondents give several reasons, the shares do not sum to 100%, and the ranking depends on the year and on whether a question asks for the primary or any contributing reason.

Income loss can also be a consequence rather than a separate cause. A Canadian insolvency practitioner's 2019 study of 5,800 Ontario personal insolvencies reports that **8 in 10 filers were employed at the time of filing**, and that triggers are often catastrophic life events ([Hoyes Michalos](https://www.hoyes.com/press/joe-debtor/causes-of-insolvency/)). That finding fits a picture in which a sudden shock, rather than unemployment alone, pushes people with existing debt into filing. The filer profile in the CBP data is middle-income and indebted, with a **median age of 49** and single women making up **about 33%** of filers, though these figures are also from secondary summaries ([debt.org](https://www.debt.org/bankruptcy/statistics/)).

The overspending explanation rests on a narrower base. A UC Davis study of all Delaware personal filings in 2003 concluded that simple overspending drove most filings, and it found bankrupt households had higher mortgage and auto loans relative to income ([UC Davis](https://www.ucdavis.edu/news/reckless-spending-not-illness-or-job-loss-causes-most-bankruptcy)). The study covers one state in one year, and its author's suggestion that some people overspend deliberately is an inference, not a measured result. A 2026 vendor survey of 1,421 self-reported filers points to cost-of-living pressure and tariffs, but its overspending figures are internally inconsistent, so it cannot settle the question ([StudyFinds](https://studyfinds.com/most-americans-months-away-from-financial-collapse-bankruptcy-survey/)). The better reading is that choices and shocks interact, and the data cannot separate them.

## Medical crises raise filing risk, but their size is disputed

Medical causes are the most contested of the main triggers. Himmelstein-group studies link medical problems to **about 62% of filings** in a 2007 survey of filers ([Manhattan Institute](https://manhattan.institute/article/the-healthcare-bankruptcy-myth)) and to **66.5% of filings** in 2013–2016 ([Medical Xpress](https://medicalxpress.com/news/2019-02-medical-bankruptcy-two-thirds-filers-cite.html)). A DOJ study of more than 5,000 cases from 2000–2002 found **54% of bankruptcies involved no medical debt** ([RealClearMarkets](https://www.realclearmarkets.com/articles/2009/07/30/the_medical_bankruptcy_myth_97335.html)), and a Fay rebuttal finds **no statistical link once debt levels are controlled** ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/7269373_Medical_Bankruptcy_Myth_Versus_Fact)). The dispute turns on method: the Himmelstein sample includes only people who filed, so critics argue it cannot show medical debt caused filing ([AEI](https://www.aei.org/research-products/speech/clarifying-the-research-on-medical-bankruptcy/)). The evidence does not decide the headline share, so this report does not adopt one. It does treat medical problems as a frequent contributing factor rather than a rare one, and it treats the overspending view as a separate, unproven alternative.

A 2026 Health Affairs study adds a causal test that the self-report surveys lack. Linking a statewide trauma registry to credit reports for about 12,800 injured patients, it found that **bankruptcy filings rose by 3.2 per 1,000 patients at fifteen months after injury, a 6% relative increase** ([Health Affairs](https://www.healthaffairs.org/doi/10.1377/hlthaff.2025.00847)). An NBER working paper finds that **small and larger medical debts (at or above $500) are not meaningfully predictive of default** ([NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w33644)), and its authors caution that a correlation between bankruptcy and medical debt does not establish causation ([NBER PDF](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w33644/w33644.pdf)). The report follows the Health Affairs direction, because it compares injured patients with matched controls and measures filings directly, while the NBER result concerns credit default in a different population. The modest size of the effect means medical shocks matter for some households rather than explaining most filings. Hospitalisation is also a narrow measure: a separate US study of matched filings estimates that **only 4% of personal bankruptcies are caused by hospitalisations** ([Argyle et al., FDIC-hosted PDF](https://www.fdic.gov/system/files/2024-09/2020-argyle.pdf)).

## Unemployment moves filings in aggregate, less clearly for individuals

The link between unemployment and filings is clearest in aggregate data. A state panel study finds that **a 10% rise in the unemployment rate is associated with a 14.4% rise in personal bankruptcies**, and that personal filings track current unemployment ([SSRN, Platt, Demirkan and Platt](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1524484)). Individual-level studies point the other way: a literature summary notes that studies using individual data often find the bankruptcy decision unrelated to local unemployment increases ([ResearchGate abstract](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/5209498_The_Effect_of_Unemployment_Benefits_Welfare_Benefits_and_Other_Income_on_Personal_Bankruptcy)), and a CBO literature review records that one model found unemployment significant while another did not ([CBO, 2000](https://www.cbo.gov/sites/default/files/cbofiles/ftpdocs/24xx/doc2421/bankruptcy.pdf)). The report follows the aggregate link, because the state panel gives the clearest magnitude and it is consistent with the survey evidence that job loss is a leading trigger. The individual-level evidence is more mixed, so the report does not extend the 14.4% figure to any single household. The pandemic also shows that the link depends on income support: a Harvard Business School working paper reports that **bankruptcy filings fell significantly during COVID-19** ([HBS, Wang, Yang and Iverson](https://www.hbs.edu/ris/download.aspx?name=21-041.pdf)) despite high unemployment. Some evidence also finds that higher unemployment benefits reduce the probability of bankruptcy ([ResearchGate abstract](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/5209498_The_Effect_of_Unemployment_Benefits_Welfare_Benefits_and_Other_Income_on_Personal_Bankruptcy)).

## Filings are rising again, and recovery depends on the outcome

The 2026 rise is real in court counts but not in every series. Epiq reports **293,265 individual filings in the first half of 2026, up 12%** ([Epiq Global](https://www.epiqglobal.com/en-us/resource-center/news/small-business-filings-increase-50-year-over-year-in-first-half-of-2026)), and Epiq's commentary links the rise to higher borrowing costs, household expenses, delinquencies and a softer job market ([IFA Commercial Factor](https://magazine.factoring.org/news/subchapter-v-filings-jump-46-in-2026-according-to-epiq-aacer)). These are general attributions, not case-level findings. The New York Fed's series, which counts consumers with new bankruptcy notations on credit reports, shows about 124,000 in Q1 2026, flat on the prior quarter ([NC Bankruptcy Expert](https://ncbankruptcyexpert.com/2026/07/22/law-review-economics-federal-reserve-bank-new-york-quarterly-report-household-debt-and)), and 135,220 in Q3 ([Wolf Street](https://wolfstreet.com/2026/11/11/household-debts-debt-to-income-ratios-delinquencies-foreclosures-collections-bankruptcies-in-q3-2026/)). The two series conflict, but they measure different things. The report relies on the court counts for the trend, since they record filings directly.

Recovery after filing depends on the outcome of the case. Using random assignment of filings to judges, Dobbie and Song find that **Chapter 13 protection increases annual earnings by $5,562 and lowers five-year foreclosure rates by 19.1 percentage points** ([NBER w20520](https://www.nber.org/papers/w20520)). Dismissed filers, by contrast, experienced large and persistent earnings drops, according to a secondary summary ([TheStreet](https://www.thestreet.com/personal-finance/file-for-bankruptcy-and-get-a-better-job-make-more-money-and-live-longer-12924686)). Credit scores recover faster than credit access does: a Philadelphia Fed working paper finds risk scores rebound quickly, but most filers keep much lower credit limits ([Philadelphia Fed WP 14-25](https://www.philadelphiafed.org/-/media/frbp/assets/working-papers/2014/wp14-25.pdf); [Bentz Holguin Law summary](https://www.bentzholguinlaw.com/can-bankruptcy-actually-improve-your-credit-score-a-closer-look-at-the-data/)). A Federal Reserve Board study finds that filers have more limited unsecured credit access and pay higher rates than comparable non-filers, and that access improves with time since filing ([Federal Reserve Board](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/household-borrowing-after-personal-bankruptcy.htm)).

## Non-court alternatives are used most in England and Wales

Alternatives to court are most visible in England and Wales, where official data cover volumes. In the 2025–26 year, the Insolvency Service recorded **48,850 debt relief orders (DROs)**, up from 45,917 in 2024–25 ([GOV.UK annual report](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/insolvency-service-annual-report-and-accounts-2025-to-2026/insolvency-service-annual-report-and-accounts-2025-2026)). In October 2026 the record showed **696 bankruptcies, 4,064 DROs and 8,427 individual voluntary arrangements (IVAs)** ([GOV.UK, October 2026](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/individual-insolvencies-october-2026)). The DRO debt limit rose from £30,000 to £50,000 in June 2024, and the £90 application fee was removed in April 2024 ([Traders Union](https://tradersunion.com/news/geopolitics-trade/show/2660235-insolvency-service-debt-relief-upgrade/)). An older StepChange guide gives a limit under £15,000 ([StepChange](https://www.stepchange.org/portals/0/documents/info/stepchange-debt-management-plans-guide.pdf)), but it predates the 2024 change, so the report uses the £50,000 limit. A debt management plan (DMP) distributes one affordable payment among eligible non-priority creditors ([Support Fund, StepChange](https://supportfund.co.uk/advice/insights/step-change-debt-help)).

In the US, the evidence on alternatives is thinner. An FTC-cited figure that a nonprofit DMP commonly takes 48 months or more comes from a debt-relief company's summary, not the FTC page itself ([United Debt Relief](https://uniteddebtrelief.com/blog/credit-counseling-bankruptcy-or-debt-settlement-how-to-choose/)). Debt settlement is described as a last resort before bankruptcy, with DMPs usually the better option ([Experian](https://www.experian.com/blogs/ask-experian/alternatives-to-debt-management-plans/)). No official source gives how many US consumers use DMPs, settlement or consolidation loans.

## Conclusion

The main reasons people file are income shocks, medical crises and family breakdown, and the evidence supports this ordering more firmly than any single headline share. The strongest contested question is how large the medical role is. The surveys and the 2026 Health Affairs study point to a real but modest effect, and the DOJ and NBER findings warn against a headline figure that treats every medical bill as a cause. Overspending remains a possible contributor, but no recent study shows it as the majority cause.

For a reader trying to understand filing, the practical lesson is that filings respond to shocks, and that shocks matter most where people have little income buffer or unemployment support. Recovery also depends on whether the court grants protection, so a dismissal or a non-court route can leave people worse off. The data are thin on recent years, on non-US filers and on alternatives outside the UK, so the picture should be updated as newer US data emerge.
