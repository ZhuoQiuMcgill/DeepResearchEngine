# Exclusion and Markets Shaped African American Culture

Economics has shaped African American culture mainly through exclusion. Because Black Americans were barred from public welfare, mainstream insurance and many white-owned businesses, they built their own mutual aid societies, churches, insurers, banks and commercial districts, and those institutions still carry cultural weight. Today culture is also a large commercial asset: **Black-owned employer firms reported $211.8 billion in receipts in 2022**, and Black buying power is widely cited at about $2.1 trillion. Control of that value remains uneven, however. Critics argue that most of the profit from Black music, fashion and style goes to corporations rather than creators, and the **median Black family's net worth was about $37,000 in 2025 against $332,000 for white families**. Debates now centre on whether the gap reflects culture or structure, and on whether race-targeted economic programmes should survive. Several key figures are contested, and those disagreements are flagged below rather than resolved.

## Exclusion built Black institutions from mutual aid to banks

Black communities turned to self-help because the mainstream economy would not serve them. Early mutual aid societies were often church-related and, in the South, **sometimes the only source of financial protection available to Black people** ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/history/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/insurance-companies)). Burial societies went on to found funeral homes, cemeteries, banks, and real estate and insurance companies ([Cambridge Guide to African American History](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-guide-to-african-american-history/societies-mutual-aid/9852F251415D02AC9CACEE6E07FC9F38)). Insurance became the largest and most durable Black business sector before 1960. As white companies raised premiums and cut policy sizes for Black customers, Black fraternal and mutual insurers filled the gap ([Library of Congress blog](https://blogs.loc.gov/inside_adams/2021/02/african-american-insurance-companies/)). **North Carolina Mutual Life**, which grew out of the Grand Fountain of the United Order of True Reformers, is described as the largest Black business before 1960 ([Cambridge Guide to African American History](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-guide-to-african-american-history/societies-mutual-aid/9852F251415D02AC9CACEE6E07FC9F38)). Sources differ on its founders: one gives seven local businessmen and another gives John C. Merrick and six others ([Davies Group](https://davies-group.com/northamerica/knowledge/black-history-month-insurance-industry-trailblazers/); [Kemper](https://cloud.kemper.com/en/resources/insurance-insights/a-legacy-of-protection-how-black-communities-built-financial-security)).

Banks were smaller and more fragile. A Library of Congress guide counts **134 Black-owned banks formed between 1888 and 1934, of which only nine survived the Depression** ([LOC Resource Guide](https://guides.loc.gov/african-americans-in-business/businesses-industries/banking-insurance-finance)). Other sources give lower counts: a Liberty Bank CEO cited by PushBlack Finance gives "more than 130" banks for the same period ([PushBlack Finance](https://www.pushblackfinance.com/news/true-history-black-owned-banks)), and Forbes gives "over 100" ([Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/sites/sonariglinton/2025/04/08/why-buying-black-is-not-enough-the-importance-of-black-owned-banks/)). The sources differ on the definition, so the count should be stated with its scope. The failure of Freedman's Bank, created by Congress in 1865, is also explained differently. The Library of Congress attributes it to **speculation and fraud by white managers** ([LOC Resource Guide](https://guides.loc.gov/african-americans-in-business/businesses-industries/banking-insurance-finance)), while a 2022 White House speech blames an economic downturn and an organised assault on Reconstruction ([White House](https://bidenwhitehouse.archives.gov/briefing-room/speeches-remarks/2022/10/04/remarks-by-vice-president-harris-at-the-freedmans-bank-forum)). A 2026 article disputes whether the bank was Black-owned in the later sense ([KOLUMN](https://www.kolumnmagazine.com/2026/03/18/the-long-ledger-of-black-banking/)). Churches filled part of the credit role as informal banks, funded schools and orphanages, and backed enterprises such as Capital Savings Bank in Washington, D.C., founded in 1888 ([City Startup Labs](https://citystartuplabs.com/celebrating-black-philanthropy-month-a-living-history-of-mutual-aid-and-triumph/); [ChildSavers](https://www.childsavers.org/bpm/); [Main Street Inc.](https://mainstreetinc.com/resources/industry-insights/black-banking-milestones-main-street-honors-black-history-month/)).

Commercial districts followed the same logic. Greenwood in Tulsa is the best-known case, and its size is disputed. The Tulsa City-County Library counts **108 Black-owned businesses in the 1921 directory** ([Tulsa City-County Library](https://www.tulsalibrary.org/black-wall-street)), the Federal Reserve Bank of Kansas City reports **191** ([Federal Reserve Bank of Kansas City](https://www.kansascityfed.org/oklahomacity/oklahoma-economist/oklahoma-economist-the-past-present-and-future-of-black-wall-street/)), and KJRH reports **over 300** ([KJRH](https://www.kjrh.com/news/local-news/tulsas-black-wall-street-the-rise-and-devastating-fall-of-americas-most-prosperous-black-community)). The differences likely reflect different directories, years and definitions. The popular claim of great wealth is also contested: NBC News quotes historian Ellsworth saying that **"the wealth in Greenwood has gotten a bit exaggerated"** ([NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/select/news/tulsa-race-massacre-fact-check-ncna1269045)), and a review of the myth estimates that business owners were probably no more than five percent of the population ([Daily Beast](https://www.thedailybeast.com/shattering-the-hollywood-myths-of-the-tulsa-race-massacre/)). Segregation created a captive market. Restaurants, barbershops, cafés and music venues in Black neighbourhoods thrived because white stores excluded Black customers ([The Henry Ford](https://www.thehenryford.org/collections/explore/articles/black-entrepreneurs-during-the-jim-crow-era); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/271820153_Black_Enterprise_in_the_Retail_Trade_during_the_Early_Twentieth_Century)).

## Migration reshaped work, housing and neighbourhood life

The Great Migration was driven largely by economic pull and push. Historians point to a **wage gap, with factory jobs typically paying about three times rural Southern work** ([OurHistory](https://www.ourhistory.org.uk/the-great-migration/)), together with sharecropping debt and the boll weevil's spread from 1892 ([Lumen Learning](https://courses.lumenlearning.com/wm-ushistory2/chapter/the-great-migration/)). Wartime labour shortages in northern industry were an early driver ([Atlantis Press](https://www.atlantis-press.com/article/126003821.pdf)). Economist Leah Boustan's framework adds the post-WWI manufacturing boom, the 1927 Mississippi flood and the mechanical cotton harvester ([UCLA Newsroom](https://newsroom.ucla.edu/stories/understanding-the-great-migration-through-an-economic-lens)).

The labour-market effects are disputed. UCLA reports that Boustan did not find the widely held claim that Southern migrants displaced Northern Black workers ([UCLA Newsroom](https://newsroom.ucla.edu/stories/understanding-the-great-migration-through-an-economic-lens)). A Springer paper reads Boustan's 2009 work differently, finding that migration **put downward pressure on the wages of Black people already in the North**, and argues that the decline may have been larger than thought ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40176-016-0070-2)). Most studies find positive economic gains for migrants, though one strand disputes that migrants' own economic position improved ([Atlantis Press](https://www.atlantis-press.com/article/126003821.pdf)). Migration also brought **residential segregation and white flight, which led to neighbourhood deterioration** ([Atlantis Press](https://www.atlantis-press.com/article/126003821.pdf)). Work shifted from farm and domestic labour toward factory and manufacturing jobs, and the 1940s brought gains that narrowed the racial wage gap ([92Q](https://92q.com/5920004/the-great-migration-explained/), a popular summary). Cultural effects are discussed in general terms, including music, art and the legacy of northern ghettos ([Economic History Association review](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/product/7CBB899677B5639E61640F42631C7355/core-reader)). The sources do not quantify how specific neighbourhood cultures changed.

## Culture earns large revenue, but control stays elsewhere

Today the economic value of Black culture is large, and most of the debate concerns who keeps it. A 2026 Black Music Action Coalition report, "Stolen by Design," argues that publishing and copyright systems extracted generational wealth from Black creators ([Black Music Action Coalition](https://www.bmacoalition.org/musicindustryreports)). It is an advocacy document, and its data were not verified. In October 2026 Nas published an open letter saying Black music's pioneers were exploited, pointing to longstanding disputes over royalties and ownership involving Ma Rainey, Chaka Khan and Gladys Knight ([The Root](https://www.theroot.com/nas-calls-out-the-exploitation-of-black-music-artists-i-2000131400)). Outside the US, UK Music reports that Black music made up **80% (£24.5 billion) of a £30 billion UK market between 1994 and 2023** ([UK Music](https://www.ukmusic.org/research-reports/black-music-means-business/)), a UK-scope finding that does not describe the US. No recent US industry report measures the share of royalties that Black artists receive, so the most cited US royalty percentages come from unsourced aggregators and were not used.

Sport shows a shift from fees toward equity. A 2026 commentary argues that **"compensation is not ownership,"** because an endorsement cheque raises an athlete's wealth while an equity stake gives a claim on the enterprise ([The Saber Firm](https://www.thesaberfirm.com/post/the-black-athlete-as-an-institution)). Inc. reports athletes increasingly taking equity instead of fees ([Inc.](https://www.inc.com/amie-sheridan/indiana-fever-aliyah-boston-athlete-brand-deals-pay-equity-ownership/91399931)). Black investors have historically concentrated on the entertainment side rather than sports ownership ([Sports Tech Atlanta](https://www.sportstechatlanta.com/sportstechnologyblog/2026/5/4/beyond-the-playing-field-the-black-investors-reshaping-sports-ownership)). Sources differ on the NIL cap: one reports roughly **$20.5 million per institution** for Division I schools ([The Saber Firm](https://www.thesaberfirm.com/post/the-black-athlete-as-an-institution)), while Wikipedia gives **$21.6 million for 2026–27** ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Student_athlete_compensation)). The Saber Firm's figure is the older one, so the 2026–27 figure is the more likely current cap, but the two should be checked against the NCAA before use. Media consumption is also large: Nielsen reports that **Black Americans spent an average of 84 hours per week on digital media, TV and radio in 2024**, up three hours on the previous year ([Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/sites/jabariyoung/2025/01/29/nielsens-annual-report-highlights-these-new-trends-among-black-consumers/)).

Fashion and style follow a similar pattern. Critics say streetwear rooted in hip-hop has been commercialised and gentrified by luxury brands ([Medium](https://ymanawad.medium.com/from-ghetto-to-chic-2b347b6a0051)), and a Strike Magazine piece calls Balenciaga's sweatpants "an exploitation of Black culture with the hopes of securing major profits" ([Strike Magazines](https://www.strikemagazines.com/blog-2-1/cultural-appropriation-is-not-fashion)). A Yahoo News piece takes a more hopeful view, arguing that brands working with original creators can counter appropriation ([Yahoo News](https://www.yahoo.com/news/50-years-since-hip-hop-180001837.html)). These are opinion pieces. No peer-reviewed study measures how much profit flows from streetwear or hip-hop fashion to Black creators, and the sources do not estimate the share that goes to Black-owned firms.

## Black-owned business and buying power: large totals, small control

The latest official Census figures for employer firms cover the 2022 reference year. The Census Bureau reports **194,585 Black-owned employer businesses, $211.8 billion in receipts, 1.6 million employees and about $61.2 billion in payroll**, with 25.6% of firms in health care and social assistance ([US Census Bureau](https://census.gov/newsroom/press-releases/2024/employer-businesses.html)). Employer firms grew **56.9% from 2017 to 2022** ([Brookings](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/driving-prosperity-how-black-owned-businesses-fueled-recent-economic-growth/)). Black-owned firms were about 3% of US firms classifiable by owner race in 2022, against roughly 14% of Americans who are Black ([Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/02/12/a-look-at-black-owned-businesses-in-the-us/)), and they produced only 1% of total business revenue ([NCH Stats](https://nchstats.com/black-owned-businesses-in-us/)). About 71% of Black-owned firms had one to nine employees ([Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/02/12/a-look-at-black-owned-businesses-in-the-us/)). Figures for 2023 (about 200,885 employer firms and $249.0 billion) appear in secondary sources ([Black Executive Brief](https://www.blackexecutivebrief.com/insights-black-business-statistics/); [Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/sites/jabariyoung/2026/09/28/inside-forbes-inaugural-list-of-25-black-owned-business-to-watch/)), but the primary release was not retrieved, so the 2022 figures are the safer basis. Census tables cited here do not break out music, fashion or media firms, so creative-sector ownership cannot be sized from them.

Buying power is a different measure. The Selig Center and Nielsen put Black buying power at about **$2.1 trillion for 2026**, a projection of disposable income rather than a measured annual total ([Nielsen](https://www.nielsen.com/insights/2026/black-influencer-creator-trends/)). Projections differ: the Black Wall Street Chronicle gives $2.1–2.2 trillion for end-2026 ([Black Wall Street](https://www.blackwallstreet.org/spendingpower)), and the National Urban League gives $2.1 trillion ([Ebony](https://www.ebony.com/black-america-national-urban-league-2026-report/)). The gap between spending and wealth is central to the critique. One commentary sets the $2.1 trillion against a **median Black net worth of $44,100** and argues that buying power is largely absorbed by national brands ([Black Executive Brief](https://www.blackexecutivebrief.com/2-1-trillion-and-counting-the-wealth-is-real-the-architecture-to-hold-it-is-not/)). An essay summarising Shaffer's work argues that buying power is not economic power, because money passes through other hands ([Shatter the Standards](https://www.shatterthestandards.com/p/black-buying-power-is-not-black-economic)). Brands respond to culture: Nielsen reports that **67% of Black consumers pay more attention to brands that reflect their culture**, against 46% overall ([Nielsen](https://www.nielsen.com/insights/2026/black-influencer-creator-trends/)). Yet **71% of Black audiences feel misrepresented** ([Essence](https://www.essence.com/news/money-career/why-brands-still-miss-black-consumers/)). These loyalty figures come from Nielsen's own surveys.

## The wealth gap and the fight over race-targeted policy

The wealth gap is far wider than the income gap. The 2025 Survey of Consumer Finances, released in October 2026, reports that **median white family wealth was nine times that of Black families**, the largest ratio since 2016, and that Black median net worth fell 25% from 2022 ([Federal Reserve](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/files/scf26.pdf)). The Tax Policy Center gives the Black median as falling from $49,100 to $36,800 in inflation-adjusted terms ([Tax Policy Center](https://taxpolicycenter.org/fiscal-facts/how-wealth-varies-race-us)). The NCRC describes the same fall as a widening gap, while also saying the 2022–2025 change in the Black median is statistically indistinguishable from zero ([NCRC](https://ncrc.org/black-household-wealth-possibly-declined-by-25-as-racial-wealth-gap-widened-new-data-shows/)). Income figures come from a different survey. Census CPS data show a **median Black household income of $59,980 in 2025, about 62% of the non-Hispanic white median of $96,710** ([JBHE](https://jbhe.com/2026/09/the-racial-income-gap-narrows-but-remains-huge/)). Census ACS data give a lower Black median of $59,022 ([Census Bureau](https://www2.census.gov/library/publications/2026/demo/acsbr-028.pdf)). The SCF shows Black income declining from 2022 to 2025, while CPS shows a 4.8% rise from 2024 to 2025. The periods differ, so both may be accurate.

Homeownership shows a persistent gap. The Census Bureau's Q3 2026 data put the **Black rate at 44.4% against 75.1% for non-Hispanic white householders** ([Census Bureau](https://www.census.gov/housing/hvs/files/currenthvspress.pdf)). The quarterly Black series is volatile, moving from 45.7% in Q3 2025 to 44.0% in Q1 2026 and back to 45.4% in Q2 2026 ([FRED](https://alfred.stlouisfed.org/series?seid=BOAAAHORUSQ156N)), so no clear upward trend is established. Mortgage denial rates are higher for Black applicants: in 2024 the **Black denial rate was 19.00% against 11.27% overall** ([LendingTree](https://www.lendingtree.com/home/mortgage/lendingtree-study-black-homebuyers-more-likely-to-be-denied-mortgages-than-other-homebuyers/)). A Philadelphia Fed working paper finds about 18% of Black applicants denied in 2018–2019 against 8% of white applicants, but argues that much of the gap reflects applicant characteristics, including credit scores ([Philadelphia Fed](https://www.philadelphiafed.org/-/media/frbp/assets/working-papers/2024/wp24-09.pdf)). A Minneapolis Fed paper finds Black applicants still more likely to be denied after controls, though the estimated gap shrinks ([Minneapolis Fed](https://www.minneapolisfed.org/-/media/assets/papers/community-development-working-papers/2023/the-role-of-race-in-mortgage-application-denials.pdf)). Appraisal is another channel: a Review of Finance article reports a negative appraisal gap for 8.6% of Black-owned homes against 6.5% of white-owned homes ([Review of Finance](https://academic.oup.com/rof/advance-article/doi/10.1093/rof/rfaf046/8316104)).

Scholars disagree about whether culture or structure explains these gaps. Herring and Henderson divide explanations into cultural and behavioural versus structural and unequal ownership opportunity, and note that the two are not mutually exclusive ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/294139733_Wealth_Inequality_in_Black_and_White_Cultural_and_Structural_Sources_of_the_Racial_Wealth_Gap)). They reject single-parent households and poor spending as sufficient explanations, attributing the gap instead to income, stock and business ownership, and they argue that the gap persists after controls ([Michigan State IPPSR](https://ippsr.msu.edu/research/wealth-inequality-black-and-white-cultural-and-structural-sources-racial-wealth-gap)). Coleman Hughes, as summarised by Quillette, argues that spending patterns are at least partly responsible, but that is an opinion essay ([Quillette](https://quillette.com/2021/09/07/why-culture-matters-for-racial-disparities/)). An Urban Institute framework argues that observable factors such as income, education and family structure explained **70% of the racial wealth gap, falling to under 20% for Black and Latino families**, which leaves historical and institutional factors as the main remainder ([Urban Institute](https://www.urban.org/sites/default/files/publication/104133/rethinking-economics-in-the-black-lives-matter-era_1.pdf)). The same Urban analysis finds that **intergenerational transfers explain 12 to 16 percent** of the gap, which cuts against the view that inheritance is the main driver ([Urban Institute](https://apps.urban.org/features/wealth-inequality-charts/)). Critics from the Mises Institute argue that barriers such as zoning and licensing are class-based rather than race-based ([Mises Institute](https://mises.org/mises-wire/problems-theories-black-white-wealth-gap)). The sources do not give a decomposition of cultural versus structural shares of the income gap.

These debates shape policy. Race-targeted federal business support narrowed in 2025 and 2026. The Minority Business Development Agency (MBDA) lost its performance data platform and most of its staff in 2025, and its business-centre cooperative agreements were terminated ([GAO](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-26-107718)). Staffing reports differ. Free Beacon reports that the administration fired all remaining MBDA employees in October 2025 ([Free Beacon](https://freebeacon.com/america/audit-of-minority-business-development-agency-finds-mass-oversight-failure/)), and Funding Landscape describes the agency as effectively shut down and reportedly down to one employee ([Funding Landscape](https://fundinglandscape.com/answers/grants-women-minority-owned-businesses-2026)), while USAFacts's May 2026 count lists 19 federal employees ([USAFacts](https://usafacts.org/explainers/what-does-the-us-government-do/subagency/minority-business-development-agency/)). This report follows the USAFacts count and GAO's account of staff placed on administrative leave, because they are the more recent official sources and the more authoritative of those cited. The 19 figure is a count of federal employees and does not, by itself, show what the agency can still deliver.

The SBA's 8(a) programme, which aims to award at least five percent of federal contracting dollars to socially disadvantaged owners ([Inside Government Contracts](https://www.insidegovernmentcontracts.com/2026/08/sba-finalizes-changes-to-the-8a-programs-social-disadvantage-requirements/)), now uses a race-neutral test for disadvantage. The final rule was published on August 11, 2026 and takes effect September 10, 2026 ([KY APEX](https://www.kyapex.com/post/sba-finalizes-new-rule-for-the-8-a-program-what-s-actually-in-it)). It follows the 2023 *Ultima Services* ruling against the race-based presumption. Forbes reports that about 1,000 firms were suspended and only 65 were admitted in fiscal 2025 ([Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/sites/nataliemadeiracofield/2026/01/27/1000-firms-out-65-in-whats-happening-to-the-8a-program/)). One analysis expects the new test to widen the pool for individually owned applicants ([Schwabe](https://www.schwabe.com/publication/court-issues-final-order-in-ultima-services/)), so the direction of the programme remains unsettled. Federal reparations remain at the bill stage: H.R.40 was referred to committee ([Congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-bill/40)). Evanston, Illinois, pays $25,000 to eligible Black residents and descendants and is in federal litigation ([US News/AP](https://www.usnews.com/news/us/articles/2026-06-16/federal-government-seeks-to-halt-the-first-u-s-reparations-program-for-black-people)). Disbursement totals conflict: Fox News reports at least 137 recipients receiving $3.47 million ([Fox News](https://www.foxnews.com/media/chicago-suburb-locals-hope-reparations-program-address-affordability-pressures-black-population-dwindles)), CourtDocket reports more than $5.2 million to over 200 recipients ([CourtDocket](https://courtdocket.org/evanston-reparations-program-eligibility-25000-uses-and-taxes/)), and the plaintiffs' filing counts $6.35 million to 254 individuals ([Fox News](https://www.foxnews.com/media/evanston-reparations-committee-issue-25k-44-residents)). Independent outcome evaluations are scarce. The GAO work addresses compliance, not results. Grant results from the Chicago Black-owned business coalition, which report that 50% of grantees saw revenue rise in late 2021, are the programme's own reporting ([US Black Chambers](https://usblackchambers.org/coalition-to-back-black-businesses-awards-25000-grants-to-20-black-owned-small-businesses/)). An Inspector General audit reportedly found that 96% of reviewed MBDA transactions should not have been approved, but that report comes through a partisan-leaning outlet and the primary audit was not retrieved ([Free Beacon](https://freebeacon.com/america/audit-of-minority-business-development-agency-finds-mass-oversight-failure/)).

Philanthropy and inheritance connect culture to money. African Americans give a larger share of their income than other groups, and Black churches receive a large share of that giving ([NewsOne](https://newsone.com/3838286/giving-tuesday-african-american-philanthropy/)). Washington Post reports that Black donors gave 25% more of their income than white donors, and that **Black families gave 6% of median wealth to charity in 2010, against 2% for white families** ([Washington Post](https://www.washingtonpost.com/business/2020/12/11/blacks-prioritize-philanthropy/)). These figures rest on a 2012 study and the 2010 wealth data. A 2024 Boston College study finds that Black and Hispanic decedents are less likely to have wills or to receive inheritances ([NCBI](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11690255/)). I found no economist-led study measuring how the wealth gap affects church finance or the balance sheets of Black institutions.

## Conclusion

Economics has shaped African American culture in two directions at once. Exclusion created durable institutions, including insurers, mutual aid societies, churches and commercial districts, and these still anchor community life and philanthropy. At the same time, the commercial value of Black culture and spending power is now very large, and the central dispute is who captures it. The strongest evidence suggests that the most valuable forms of ownership, such as masters, publishing rights and equity, remain concentrated outside Black communities, but the sources do not measure that concentration for the US.

The wealth gap and its explanation are the most important open questions. The income gap is smaller and better measured, while the wealth gap has widened in dollar terms, and the sources do not settle whether culture or structure explains it. The most defensible reading is that structural and historical factors, including inheritance and mortgage and appraisal barriers, carry most of the weight, while cultural explanations are argued mainly on spending and financial behaviour. Policy evidence is also thin. Race-targeted business support is being narrowed, and the few local reparations programmes are in court, with little independent evidence of outcomes. Anyone writing on this topic should name the survey, year and definition behind each number, and should treat advocacy and aggregator figures as claims to be checked rather than facts.
