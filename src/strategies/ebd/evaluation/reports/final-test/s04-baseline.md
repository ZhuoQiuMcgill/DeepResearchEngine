# American Men and Money Reshaped the War

The United States changed World War I less by winning a decisive battle than by tipping the balance of manpower, money and shipping in 1918, while its political choices shaped how the war ended. American troops reached France late, but by Armistice Day **2,079,880 had landed in Europe** ([National Maritime Historical Society](https://seahistory.org/we-built-her-to-bring-them-over-there-the-cruiser-and-transport-force-in-the-great-war/)). Before that, US loans, exports and food supplies kept the Allies in the fight, and US destroyers and convoys protected the transports that carried the army. Woodrow Wilson's Fourteen Points became the basis of the armistice request, but the Allies narrowed them, and the US Senate rejected the Treaty of Versailles and League membership. Congress and Harding then ended the state of war in 1921 with a separate peace. Historians still disagree on whether the American military contribution was decisive, marginal, or a real but partial share of a victory already in motion.

## Troops arrived late, but in time to tip 1918

Pershing's insistence on full training delayed the American build-up. [Wikipedia, "American Expeditionary Forces"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Expeditionary_Forces) reports that few troops arrived before January 1918, and that the AEF played only a minor front-line role until late October 1917. The numbers then rose quickly, although sources disagree on the timing. One account says **over one million** American troops were in France by May 1918, about half on the front lines ([Wikipedia, "American Expeditionary Forces"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Expeditionary_Forces)), while another says **over two million** had reached Europe by that month ([Wikipedia, "AEF order of battle"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Expeditionary_Forces_order_of_battle)). The Armistice total is more firmly documented, and by July 1918 American troops were arriving at **about 10,000 per day** ([Naval History and Heritage Command](https://www.history.navy.mil/news-and-events/news/2025/nhm-040125.html)).

Command was contested. Pershing insisted on a unified American army, yet during the German spring offensives he placed his resources at Marshal Foch's disposal ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/John-J-Pershing)). American units also fought under British or Australian command in June 1918 at Cantigny, Château-Thierry, Belleau Wood and Soissons ([Wikipedia, "John J. Pershing"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_J._Pershing)). The official history says the First Army was not created until August 1918 ([US Army, "Organization of the AEF, Volume 1"](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GOVPUB-D114-PURL-gpo113323/pdf/GOVPUB-D114-PURL-gpo113323.pdf)). The independent-army policy was therefore a position repeatedly modified by crisis rather than a fixed plan.

The operations show what American troops could do, though their effects are contested. Cantigny on 28 May 1918, led by the 1st Division, was small but was the first American offensive of the war ([Historyofwar.org](https://www.historyofwar.org/articles/battles_cantigny.html)). Sources disagree on its losses: the Pritzker Military Museum gives **1,603 US casualties** ([Pritzker Military Museum](https://www.pritzkermilitary.org/explore/museum/past-exhibits/lest-we-forget-doughboys-sammies-and-sailors-great-war/cantigny)), while Historyofwar.org gives 100 dead and 1,500 wounded. Belleau Wood was costly, with RealClearDefense reporting **nearly 10,000 Marines killed or wounded** ([RealClearDefense](https://www.realcleardefense.com/articles/2015/06/23/belleau_wood_108119.html)). A Marine Corps Times article argues that Paris was never the German objective, so the traditional "stopped the drive to Paris" story is not supported ([Marine Corps Times](https://www.marinecorpstimes.com/news/your-marine-corps/2021/06/27/the-us-marines-mythic-fight-at-belleau-wood/)).

Saint-Mihiel, from 12 to 16 September 1918, was the first US-led offensive, with the AEF fighting under its own command ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/event/Battle-of-Saint-Mihiel-1918)). The Germans had begun withdrawing two days before the attack ([Library of Congress](https://www.loc.gov/item/today-in-history/september-12/)), so it was a partly pre-empted success. The official history adds that the First Army could probably have pushed further but was committed to the larger Meuse-Argonne operation ([US Army, "Organization of the AEF, Volume 1"](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GOVPUB-D114-PURL-gpo113323/pdf/GOVPUB-D114-PURL-gpo113323.pdf)).

The Meuse-Argonne offensive, from 26 September to 11 November 1918, was the largest US operation, involving **about 1.2 million American personnel** ([Wikipedia mirror, "Meuse–Argonne offensive"](https://military-history.fandom.com/wiki/Meuse%E2%80%93Argonne_offensive)). Its cost was heavy on both sides. The US Army's campaign study says AEF casualties in about six months of combat exceeded **255,000**, including **52,997 battle deaths**, and that Germany had lost nearly a million casualties by mid-July ([US Army Center of Military History](https://history.army.mil/portals/143/Images/Publications/catalog/77-8.pdf)). Other sources give conflicting totals for the offensive itself, such as 117,000 American casualties ([historyofwar.org](https://www.historyofwar.org/articles/battles_meuse_argonne.html)) and 26,277 American deaths ([Wikipedia mirror](https://military-history.fandom.com/wiki/Meuse%E2%80%93Argonne_offensive)). The German side of the record is less clear, and Ludendorff's description of 8 August 1918 as the "black day of the German army" comes from a German-perspective site ([Morthomme.com](https://www.morthomme.com/en/meuse-argonne/)).

## Convoys kept the troopships moving

The navy's most measurable contribution was protecting the transports. Admiral William Sims urged the British War Cabinet to adopt convoys on 25 April 1917, and the first convoy of the war sailed from Gibraltar on 10 May 1917 ([ABMC](https://www.abmc.gov/news-events/news/protecting-allied-ships-during-wwi-convoy-system-comes-gibraltar/)). By June 1917, more than 30 US destroyers were operating in the Western Approaches and Bay of Biscay, integrated into the British system ([Doughboy.org](https://doughboy.org/chasing-the-u-boat-the-u-s-navy-initiates-anti-submarine-warfare-may-june-1917/)). The convoy system **cut shipping losses to less than half their peak**, and U-boat losses rose from about three a month to five to ten ([National Interest](https://nationalinterest.org/blog/reboot/how-world-war-i-gave-rise-american-anti-submarine-warfare-174041)).

The record of the troop convoys is strong. Of **81 troopship convoy groups** sailing between 14 June 1917 and 4 November 1918, only **24** made reported contact with U-boats and engaged ([NHHC, *Ensuring the Lifeline to Victory*](https://www.history.navy.mil/content/dam/nhhc/research/publications/publication-508-pdf/ensuring-lifeline-508.pdf)). The NHHC says the escort of about two million soldiers took place with almost no losses to submarines ([NHHC](https://www.history.navy.mil/news-and-events/news/2025/nhm-040125.html)). Historian V. E. Tarrant puts the loss at **56 lives** for about two million troops ([The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/as-the-us-entered-world-war-i-american-soldiers-depended-on-foreign-weapons-technology-75034)). The exception was the troopship SS Tuscania, torpedoed in February 1918 with about 200 American deaths ([Wikipedia, "Matthew B. Juan"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matthew_B._Juan)).

Credit for the convoy system is shared. A USNI article argues that convoying, led by British junior officers against a reluctant Admiralty, undermined Germany's starvation strategy ([USNI](https://www.usni.org/magazines/naval-history/2026/august/convoy-heresy)). A scholarly article calls the convoy system the US Navy's largest contribution to the war, but notes that full Anglo-American cooperation was not complete until late 1917 ([Santa Clara University, *Historical Perspectives*](https://scholarcommons.scu.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1038&context=historical-perspectives)). The US contribution was therefore essential to moving the army, but the convoy's effect on the U-boat war was largely an Allied achievement.

## Credit and food sustained the Allies before the troops arrived

American money mattered before American soldiers did. [Wikipedia, "Economic history of World War I"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_history_of_World_War_I) reports that Britain financed the Allies until 1916, when it ran out of money and borrowed from the US. Sources vary widely on the size of US lending, largely because they count different things:

| Source | Figure given | What it counts |
|---|---|---|
| [Wikipedia, "Home front during World War I"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Home_front_during_World_War_I) | $5.041bn | US government loans to Allies, in 1913 dollars |
| [FRUS 1932](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1932v01/d529) | About $10bn | US loans to Allies, including the UK |
| [US State Dept](https://1997-2001.state.gov/issues/economic/fs_000301_wardebt.html) | About $12.4bn | Amounts borrowed by WWI allies, at the time |
| [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/event/World-War-I/The-U-S-entry-into-the-war) | $7bn | Loans from 1917 to the war's end |
| [History Skills](https://www.historyskills.com/classroom/modern-history/wwi-american-business/) | More than $7bn before the Armistice, plus $3.75bn after | Federal lending, split at the Armistice |

The differences reflect definitions, not conflicting evidence, so the most defensible statement is that US government lending to the Allies ran at roughly $5bn to $12bn depending on what is counted. Private purchases were also large: by April 1917 American investors had bought **almost $2.5bn of Allied bonds** ([EBSCO](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/military-history-and-science/world-war-i-and-us-economic-growth/)). Exports rose from **$2.4bn in 1913 to $6.2bn in 1917**, mostly to Britain and France ([History Skills](https://www.historyskills.com/classroom/modern-history/wwi-american-business/)).

Food and shipping were the other constraints. The Food Administration's cereal year to 31 August 1919 shipped about **12.5 million tons** of food to France, Italy and the UK, worth about $2.25bn ([FRUS 1920](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1920v01/d228)). Shipping was tight: in March 1918 the Army ordered 1,000,000 tons of troopships and 2,000,000 more tons of cargo ships, which the Fleet Corporation had not planned for ([Hessen, PSU Historical Journal](https://journals.psu.edu/phj/article/download/23568/23337)). The first Hog Island ship was launched on 5 August 1918 ([Wikipedia, "Hog Islander"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hog_Islander)), too late to shape the spring crisis.

The blockade was British-led, and the sources describe it mainly that way. German imports were cut by **80 per cent**, and by 1918 Germany was cut off from about 20 per cent of its pre-war food supply ([Offer analysis](https://www.habselstree.org.uk/boys/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2024/06/OP65StJohnFoodSupplyandWorldWarOne.pdf)). Livestock reached all-time lows by 1917–18 ([Inquiries Journal](http://www.inquiriesjournal.com/articles/899/4/the-british-blockade-during-world-war-i-the-weapon-of-deprivation)), and the blockade lasted until July 1919 ([Everyday Lives in War](https://everydaylivesinwar.herts.ac.uk/2015/04/food-and-the-first-world-war-in-germany/)). Historians disagree on its weight. The Offer analysis argues it did not break Germany's capacity to wage war and that morale at home and in the navy broke first ([Offer analysis](https://www.habselstree.org.uk/boys/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2024/06/OP65StJohnFoodSupplyandWorldWarOne.pdf)). The American economic role was therefore largely a matter of keeping Allied armies and populations supplied while the blockade and the army's arrival did their work.

The financial shift was lasting. Hugh Rockoff's figures show US investment abroad of $5.0bn and foreign investment in the US of $7.2bn in 1914, moving to $9.7bn and $3.3bn by 1919, a net creditor position of $6.4bn ([EH.net](https://eh.net/encyclopedia/u-s-economy-in-world-war-i/)). Encyclopedia.com instead describes the US as a net debtor of $3.2bn in 1914 ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/defense/energy-government-and-defense-magazines/financing-world-war-i)), so the starting point is disputed even though the reversal is not. Britain's debt to the US was **$3.696bn** and France's **$1.97bn**, according to an IMF chapter ([IMF](https://www.elibrary.imf.org/display/book/9781513511795/ch001.xml)). The US insisted on repayment, and the Young Plan page notes that France's inter-Allied debts to the US blocked its access to American capital markets ([Wikipedia, "Young Plan"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Young_Plan)).

## Wilson's terms shaped the armistice, then the Senate rejected the peace

Entry itself has a clear sequence. Germany announced on 31 January 1917 that it would resume unrestricted submarine warfare ([History.com](https://www.history.com/articles/u-s-entry-into-world-war-i-1)). British intelligence decrypted the Zimmermann telegram, which the British passed to Wilson on 24 February, and the text appeared in US newspapers on 1 March ([Office of the Historian](https://history.state.gov/milestones/1914-1920/wwi); [History.com](https://www.history.com/articles/the-secret-history-of-the-zimmermann-telegram)). Wilson asked Congress for war on 2 April, and the Senate voted 82 to 6 on 4 April ([History.com](https://www.history.com/articles/u-s-entry-into-world-war-i-1)). Sources disagree on when Zimmermann acknowledged the text, citing 3 March ([Wikipedia, "Zimmermann telegram"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zimmermann_telegram)) or 29 March ([Wikipedia, "Arthur Zimmermann"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Zimmermann)). The telegram most likely hardened a decision already driven by maritime grievances, which is an inference from the dated sequence.

Wilson's Fourteen Points, presented to Congress on 8 January 1918, became the basis of German hopes ([Office of the Historian](https://history.state.gov/milestones/1914-1920/fourteen-points)). Germany requested an armistice on 5 or 6 October 1918, accepting the Points as the basis for peace ([Oxford Public International Law](https://opil.ouplaw.com/page/730)). Lansing's note of 5 November accepted that basis with two reservations, on the freedom of the seas and compensation for civilian damage ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/event/World-War-I/The-end-of-the-German-war)). The armistice of 11 November, largely written by Foch, was described as punitive ([FirstWorldWar.com](https://www.firstworldwar.com/source/armisticeterms.htm)), and on 17 November Lansing noted that its terms did not match the Points ([NHHC](https://www.history.navy.mil/research/publications/documentary-histories/wwi/november-1918/secretary-of-state-r/_jcr_content.html)). The US therefore acted as a mediator on Wilsonian terms, while the Allies held the military power that shaped the signed text.

At Paris, Wilson's central goal was secured in text. The League was included in the Covenant, which formed the first twenty-six articles of the Treaty of Versailles signed on 28 June 1919 ([CFR](https://www.cfr.org/ten-best-ten-worst-us-foreign-policy-decisions/senate-rejection-of-the-treaty-of-versailles)). Wilson compromised on many other points to hold the League together ([CFR](https://www.cfr.org/articles/the-senate-rejects-the-treaty-of-versailles)). The Senate did not follow. Wilson had not included senators among the negotiators, and the Senate's Republican majority demanded reservations ([Senate.gov](https://www.senate.gov/about/powers-procedures/treaties/senate-rejects-treaty-of-versailles.htm)). The Senate defeated ratification on **19 November 1919, by 38 to 53** ([American Journal of International Law](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-journal-of-international-law/article/treaty-of-peace-with-germany-in-the-united-states-senate/8BDEE0A805E6762C8B7680BE7C0E54CF)), and rejected it again on 19 March 1920 ([American Journal of International Law](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-journal-of-international-law/article/treaty-of-peace-with-germany-in-the-united-states-senate/8BDEE0A805E6762C8B7680BE7C0E54CF)). Article 10 was the central objection ([Teaching American History](https://teachingamericanhistory.org/blog/wilsons-failure-the-treaty-of-versailles/)).

The US ended the war legally by a separate route. Congress passed the Knox–Porter Resolution on 1 July 1921, and Harding signed it on 2 July ([US House of Representatives History](https://history.house.gov/Historical-Highlights/1901-1950/1921_06_30_Knox-Porter/)). The separate treaty with Germany was signed on 25 August 1921 and ratified on 21 October 1921 ([Wikipedia, "U.S.–German Peace Treaty (1921)"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S.%E2%80%93German_Peace_Treaty_(1921))). It gave the US the rights and reparations of Versailles while omitting the League ([Office of the Historian](https://history.state.gov/milestones/1914-1920/paris-peace)). The US therefore kept the economic and territorial gains of the settlement and rejected its security architecture. One source says the Senate approved separate peace treaties earlier, which conflicts with the October 1921 dates, so the Senate record should be checked.

## Historians disagree over whether the US was decisive

The sharpest debate concerns decisiveness. Geoffrey Wawro argues that US military intervention was decisive in 1918 ([H-Net review](https://www.h-net.org/reviews/showrev.php?id=59111)), and writes that "Germany would have won World War I had the U.S. Army not intervened in France in 1918" ([Time](https://time.com/5406235/everything-you-know-about-how-world-war-i-ended-is-wrong/)). The opposing view, summarised in an encyclopedia entry, is that scholars including Gary Sheffield, Robin Prior, Trevor Wilson, Tim Travers and Ian Beckett see the American military contribution as negligible ([International Encyclopedia of the First World War](https://encyclopedia.1914-1918-online.net/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/ams/959456/pdf//1914-1918-Online-united_states_of_america-2014-10-08.pdf)). The same entry presents Mark Grotelueschen's *The AEF Way of War* as the middle-ground view most scholars embrace, which treats the AEF as a real but partial contribution.

The debate also covers whether entry shortened the war. John Milton Cooper argues that Wilson "shortened the war by months" and saved perhaps hundreds of thousands of lives ([Peace History](https://peacehistory-usfp.org/ww1-hist/)). Michael Kazin argues that the Allies' defeat was far from a foregone conclusion without US entry ([History.com](https://www.history.com/articles/history-faceoff-should-the-u-s-have-entered-world-war-i)). Critics argue that entry prolonged the war by removing British and French incentives to negotiate ([History.com](https://www.history.com/articles/history-faceoff-should-the-u-s-have-entered-world-war-i)). Justus Doenecke argues that the decision belonged to Wilson but was shaped by public and congressional opinion ([Project MUSE](https://muse.jhu.edu/book/1963)).

On the economic side, the historiography has shifted. A 2007 article argues that historians have largely abandoned the economic interpretation of American intervention, but that the wartime export boom should be reconsidered ([Cambridge, *International Organization*](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/international-organization/article/abs/revisionism-reconsidered-exports-and-american-intervention-in-world-war-i/AABEA9496046F8CBF79AFFF9AF014E7B)). The main disagreement is counterfactual: whether the Allies could have continued fighting in 1917–18 without US credit and troops.

## Conclusion

The clearest American effect was to supply the Allies with what they lacked by 1917–18: credit, food, shipping and fresh divisions. The convoy system and the troop lift were well documented successes, and the numbers show that the 1918 campaigns drew on a large American army. The military decisiveness of that army remains contested, and the evidence supports a middle position better than either extreme: American arrivals made the Allied offensive of 1918 possible to sustain and finish, though the sources do not quantify how far they changed the timing of the victory.

The peace shows the limits of American influence. Wilson shaped the armistice request and secured the League in the treaty text, but the Allies controlled the terms, and the Senate rejected the settlement. The US then ended the war by separate treaty and kept many economic provisions while abandoning the League. The lasting consequence was a US that was now the largest creditor, but one unwilling to join the political order its money helped to build.
