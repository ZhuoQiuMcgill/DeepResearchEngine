# Principle and Merit Drive Opposition to Preferences

Opposition to affirmative action rests mainly on a principled objection that government and institutions should not sort people by race or sex, and on a merit argument that places are owed to the most qualified. Empirical claims about harm, such as mismatch, stigma and cascade effects, are real but contested and mostly unsettled. Public opinion shows that the answer depends on wording: **50% of American adults disapproved of selective colleges considering race or ethnicity, against 33% who approved, in Pew's spring 2023 survey** ([Pew, Topic: Affirmative action in college admissions](https://www.pewresearch.org/fact-sheet/topic-affirmative-action-in-college-admissions/)). Broad questions about "affirmative action" still draw majority support, with **61% favouring affirmative-action programs for racial minorities in Gallup's 2018 asking** ([Gallup, Affirmative Action and Public Opinion](https://news.gallup.com/opinion/polling-matters/317006/affirmative-action-public-opinion.aspx)). Legally, the opposition has largely won at the top court: the Court held 6-2 in *SFFA v. Harvard* that Harvard's and UNC's programs violate the Equal Protection Clause ([Ballotpedia](https://ballotpedia.org/Students_for_Fair_Admissions,_Inc._v._President_&_Fellows_of_Harvard)). The most important point for understanding the debate is that opponents' strongest claim is about principle, which empirical findings cannot settle, while their empirical claims remain disputed.

## The principled objection treats any racial sorting as wrong

The core case against preferences is that a government may not classify citizens by race even for benign ends. Justice Harlan's *Plessy* dissent held that the Constitution "is color-blind, and neither knows nor tolerates classes among citizens" ([Justia, Plessy v. Ferguson](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/163/537/)). Chief Justice Roberts applied the same logic to modern cases, ending his 2007 *Parents Involved* opinion with the line that "the way to stop discrimination on the basis of race is to stop discriminating on the basis of race" ([SCOTUSblog](https://www.scotusblog.com/2022/02/justice-breyer-and-parents-involved)). In *SFFA*, the majority said citizens must be treated as individuals rather than "simply as components of a racial, religious, sexual, or national class" ([Johnson, Annals of the AAPSS (2024)](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/00027162251334504)). Justice Thomas's concurrence adds a stigma argument: large racial preferences "stamp [blacks and Hispanics] with a badge of inferiority" ([Supreme Court slip opinion, SFFA (Thomas, J., concurring)](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/22pdf/20-1199_hgdj.pdf)).

Philosophers draw the same line. Louis Pojman separated "weak" affirmative action, which aims at equal opportunity to compete, from "strong" affirmative action, which gives preferential treatment, and concluded that the first is morally required while the second is wrong ([Philosophy Documentation Center, *International Journal of Applied Philosophy*](https://www.pdcnet.org/ijap/content/ijap_1998_0012_0001_0097_0116)). A 2020s paper distinguishes two strands of colour-blind advocacy: consequentialist objections (preferences are divisive, stigmatizing, and inefficient) and principled objections to racial preferences as such ([arXiv 2006.15988](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.15988)). The principled strand is the one that does not depend on evidence about outcomes. Merit arguments, by contrast, are often instrumental, and the libertarian sources frame them as efficiency claims ([Wikipedia, Libertarian perspectives on affirmative action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Libertarian_perspectives_on_affirmative_action)).

Opponents also contest the justifications that supporters offer. The *SFFA* majority found Harvard's and UNC's diversity goals, though "commendable," too "amorphous" to measure under strict scrutiny ([NACAC, preliminary guidance on SFFA](https://www.nacacnet.org/wp-content/uploads/EdC-SFFA-Case-Preliminary-Summary-Analysis-7-6-FINAL-XMT.pdf)). It described the admissions results as approaching "outright racial balancing," which it called "patently unconstitutional" ([Johnson (2024)](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/00027162251334504)). On remedies, Roberts' view that "[e]liminating racial discrimination means eliminating all of it" ([Wikipedia, Constitutional colorblindness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitutional_colorblindness)) and the *SFFA* point that Harvard's view of when race-based admissions would end "doesn't have a date on it" ([Supreme Court slip opinion](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/22pdf/20-1199_hgdj.pdf)) frame remedies as temporary and individual. The compensation argument has a secondary-source answer: Pojman is reported to treat it as an attempt to impose responsibility for slavery on people with no connection to it ([IvyPanda summary of Pojman](https://ivypanda.com/essays/the-case-against-affirmative-action-case-analysis/)), though this has not been checked against the paper itself.

## The empirical harm claims are real but unsettled

Opponents' empirical case rests mainly on the mismatch hypothesis, which holds that students admitted with preferences to more selective schools struggle and switch out of demanding fields. The evidence is split by design, as the table shows.

| Study | Design and population | Finding |
|---|---|---|
| Rothstein & Yoon, on Sander's law-school data | Re-analysis of the same data | Sander's analysis "can be expected to substantially overstate" the effect, because it treats law school grades as an intermediate outcome ([NBER Working Paper 14275](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w14275/w14275.pdf)) |
| Bleemer, QJE | Difference-in-differences on UC applicants, 1994–2002 | Ending affirmative action led URM applicants to cascade into lower-quality colleges and reduced their degree attainment; Bleemer says this is inconsistent with mismatch ([CSHE working paper](https://cshe.berkeley.edu/publications/affirmative-action-mismatch-and-economic-mobility-after-california%E2%80%99s-proposition-209)) |
| Arcidiacono, Aucejo & Hotz, AER 2016 | UC campuses | Less-prepared minority students at higher-ranked campuses had lower science persistence and took longer to graduate ([Duke working paper](https://public.econ.duke.edu/~psarcidi/stem.pdf)) |
| Arcidiacono et al., Quantitative Economics 2011 | Duke private information test | Mismatch is possible only if the school holds private performance information; Duke does, but this "is not sufficient to establish that mismatch occurred" ([NBER Working Paper 14885](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w14885/w14885.pdf)) |

The strongest critique of Sander's law-school findings comes from Ho, who argued that because every school in the sample used affirmative action, comparing white and black students cannot isolate its causal effect. She called this "akin to assigning estrogen to a group of women and a placebo to a group of men" ([Lawyers' Committee for Civil Rights](https://www.lawyerscommittee.org/mismatch-a-dangerously-misleading-hypothesis/)). Sander replied that Ho's adjacent-tier matching is flawed ([Sander, "Mismeasuring the Mismatch," Yale Law Journal](https://yalelawjournal.org/pdf/150_7dq1wfs3.pdf)). The dispute therefore turns on identification and on how school selectivity is measured, and the two sides have not converged. The graduation evidence is also contested. Hinrichs' IPEDS-based work reports that four-year graduation rates at selective public universities did not change significantly after bans, though six-year rates were affected ([Springer chapter summarising Hinrichs 2014](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-031-92236-7_8)). Arcidiacono and coauthors' Prop 209 study reports that minority graduation rates rose after the ban, with a negative effect on Hispanic six-year rates of 0.5 percentage points ([Duke PDF](https://public.econ.duke.edu/~psarcidi/prop209.pdf)). Bleemer's student-level work points the other way. Because the sources conflict, the honest reading is that no study yet shows preferences cut graduation or earnings across designs.

Stigma is a related claim. Experimental work finds that beneficiaries can be seen as less competent, but the effect is conditional. Unzueta et al. found that quota beliefs raised self-perceived competence for non-beneficiary White women but had no effect for those who saw themselves as beneficiaries ([Academia.edu summary of the paper](https://www.academia.edu/9027122/How_believing_in_affirmative_action_quotas_affects_White_women_s_self_image)). A 2026 incentivised experiment with Indian students found caste-based beneficiaries were perceived as less competent, but these perceptions did not translate into material penalties ([IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/p/dur/durham/2026_05.html)). Opponents' stigma claim therefore has laboratory support but no large longitudinal US evidence.

## Enrollment shifts after 2023 and the Asian American penalty

Enrollment data show where the effects of the 2023 ruling are most visible, though not yet outcomes. Brookings reports that **only two of 29 elite institutions reporting fall 2025 data kept Black enrollment at or above 10%, compared with nine in 2022–23** ([THE FEED](https://feed.georgetown.edu/access-affordability/report-end-of-race-conscious-admissions-had-complex-cascading-effects/); [Brookings](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/the-complex-ramifications-of-students-for-fair-admissions-v-harvard/)). A separate analysis finds the average Black share at 63 selective institutions "mostly steady" against the 2010–2015 baseline, and it says this conflicts with the Brookings reading ([Econ Journal Watch](https://econjwatch.org/articles/after-sffa-v-harvard-predictions-that-didnt-come-true)). A January 2026 report reads the same data as a shift, not a shrinkage: Black enrollment fell at highly selective colleges but held across four-year institutions as less selective schools enrolled more ([The College Fix](https://www.thecollegefix.com/black-enrollment-shifts-not-shrinks-after-affirmative-action-ban-report/)). The most striking figure is from a study of about 3,000 institutions, which reports that **Black first-year enrollment at the top fifty selective schools fell 27% in the first year after SFFA**, and that Harvard's Black first-year share fell from 18% in 2023 to 11.5% in 2025 ([NYBooks](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/2026/03/26/diversity-by-other-means-fall-of-affirmative-action/)). Because these are descriptive counts, they cannot show whether the cascade improved or harmed students.

The Asian American penalty is the most contested non-beneficiary claim. Arcidiacono, Kinsler and Ransom estimate that a typical Asian American applicant would have a higher admit rate without the penalty ([Duke PDF](https://public.econ.duke.edu/~psarcidi/realpenalty.pdf)). Arcidiacono served as an expert witness for SFFA, the plaintiff in the Harvard case ([Duke PDF, footnote](https://public.econ.duke.edu/~psarcidi/racialpref.pdf)), and Harvard's expert rebuttal argues the penalty is partly muted by controls and by the inclusion of other groups ([SFFA exhibit B](https://studentsforfairadmissions.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/06/Doc-415-2-Arcidiacono-Rebuttal-Report.pdf)). Bleemer's UC study finds that affirmative action did not raise Asian enrollment relative to other applicants ([Bleemer online appendix](https://zacharybleemer.com/wp-content/uploads/Papers/QJE%20Affirmative%20Action%20Online%20Appendix.pdf)), which is a separate question from the penalty.

Race-neutral substitutes are a central part of the opponents' argument, since they claim that alternatives can do the same work. The evidence is mixed. Only **29% of Black and 32% of Hispanic top-10% graduates enrolled at selective Texas flagships despite guaranteed admission** ([Journalist's Resource](https://journalistsresource.org/education/race-neutral-alternatives-affirmative-action-college-diversity/)). Bleemer reports that top-percent policies and holistic review had negligible effects on lower-income enrollment ([Bleemer research summary](https://zacharybleemer.com/)). Texas studies disagree on how much the plan restored enrollment, and the Manhattan Institute brief summarising that dispute is a secondary, conservative-leaning source ([Manhattan Institute](https://manhattan.institute/article/why-percent-plans-for-university-admissions-aren-t-a-colorblind-success)).

## Courts, ballot measures and organised litigation drive the legal position

The legal path runs from *Bakke* to *SFFA*. In *Bakke* (1978), the Court barred a rigid racial quota at UC Davis but left room for race as one factor ([Oyez, Regents v. Bakke](https://www.oyez.org/cases/1979/76-811)). *Gratz* (2003) struck down Michigan's undergraduate points system, with Chief Justice Rehnquist describing automatic points as the functional equivalent of a quota ([NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/id/wbna3071007)). *Grutter* (2003) upheld the Michigan Law School's race-conscious admissions and expected such preferences to be unnecessary after about 25 years ([Wikipedia, Grutter v. Bollinger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grutter_v._Bollinger)). Sources disagree on what *SFFA* did to *Grutter*: Wikipedia says it "effectively overruled" the case ([Wikipedia, Grutter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grutter_v._Bollinger)), while CRS says the Court did not explicitly overrule it but left open how much room remains ([CRS, LSB10893](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/LSB10893)). The CRS reading is the more careful one, since it describes the holding, not the commentary. Sotomayor's dissent argued that the majority made indifference to race the only permissible route ([Wikisource, Sotomayor opinion](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Students_for_Fair_Admissions_v._President_and_Fellows_of_Harvard_College/Opinion_of_Justice_Sotomayor)).

Ballot measures were a parallel route. California's Proposition 209 passed with **54.55% of the vote** in 1996 and was led by Ward Connerly's campaign ([Wikipedia, Proposition 209](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1996_California_Proposition_209)). Its supporters argued that special interests had "hijacked the civil rights movement" ([CA SOS archive, pro argument](https://vigarchive.sos.ca.gov/1996/general/pamphlet/209yesarg.htm)), while opponents argued its real purpose was to end outreach, tutoring and mentoring ([CA SOS archive, rebuttal](https://vigarchive.sos.ca.gov/1996/general/pamphlet/209yesrbt.htm)). Michigan's Proposal 2 passed 58-42 in 2006 ([Affirmative Action Debate timeline](https://affirmativeactiondebate.wordpress.com/timeline-2/)). Arizona's 2010 ban passed 59–41 and was described by Sander as the last state ban adopted ([Sander, UChicago Law Review blog](https://lawreviewblog.uchicago.edu/2020/10/30/aa-sander)). In 2026, Arizona Prop 142, which would have extended the ban to race-conscious and DEI practices, failed with 40.1% yes ([MultiState](https://www.multistate.us/elections/ballot-measure?id=21186)), while Wisconsin's amendment barring preferential treatment by government entities had 56.5% yes with about 95% of votes counted ([Election Law Blog](https://electionlawblog.org/2026/160233/)). The Wisconsin figure is provisional. California's ACA 7 would narrow the 1996 ban so that within public education it applies only to higher-education admissions, and it is set for November 2028 ([Ballotpedia News](https://news.ballotpedia.org/2026/08/31/first-measure-certified-in-california-for-2028-makes-changes-to-1996-affirmative-action-ban/)).

Federal executive action has moved opposition into contracting. EO 14173 (January 2025) required agencies to include certifications in contracts and grants ([Berkshire Associates](https://www.berkshireassociates.com/blog/fourth-circuit-vacates-preliminary-injunction-against-dei-executive-orders)). The Fourth Circuit vacated the preliminary injunction on February 6, 2026, but sources describe the disposition differently: Pillsbury calls it a permanent vacatur ([Pillsbury](https://www.pillsburylaw.com/en/news-and-insights/fourth-circuit-anti-dei-executive-orders-likely-not-facially-unconstitutional.html)), and Jackson Lewis calls it a vacatur and remand ([Jackson Lewis](https://www.jacksonlewis.com/insights/fourth-circuit-vacates-preliminary-injunction-against-trump-dei-eos)). EO 14398 (March 26, 2026) bars DEI practices by federal contractors ([Wikipedia, EO 14398](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Executive_Order_14398)). On October 14, 2026, a Maryland court declined to block it, reading "disparate treatment based on race or ethnicity" as intentional discrimination already barred by federal law ([Nixon Peabody](https://www.nixonpeabody.com/insights/alerts/2026/10/26/court-declines-to-block-federal-contractor-dei-clause-reads-prohibition-narrowly)). Advocates for the plaintiffs describe that ruling as confirming the order reaches only already-illegal discrimination ([Inside Higher Ed](https://www.insidehighered.com/news/quick-takes/2026/10/19/judge-rules-trumps-anti-dei-order-contractors-can-stand)). That is the plaintiffs' reading, not the court's, and the case remains open.

The organised opposition is concentrated. Edward Blum's SFFA and the American Alliance for Equal Rights (AAER) have brought suits across education and private contracting. Blum said that "dividing people by race" is inconsistent with civil rights law ([Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/privacy-and-data-security/aba-settles-with-blum-group-that-alleged-racial-discrimination)). AAER's challenge to a minority supplier certification program rests on 42 U.S.C. § 1981 ([AAER](https://americanallianceforequalrights.org/american-alliance-for-equal-rights-files-federal-lawsuit-challenging-race-based-minority-supplier-certification-program/)). The ABA scholarship case settled in April 2026 with no damages or relief to the group ([Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/privacy-and-data-security/aba-settles-with-blum-group-that-alleged-racial-discrimination)). On the Arizona ballot, the Goldwater Institute argued that DEI offices spread race-based preferential treatment across state institutions ([Goldwater Institute](https://www.goldwaterinstitute.org/2026ballot/)). The strategy has shifted from universities toward private contracting and nonprofit programmes, which makes it harder for courts to treat the disputes as settled by *SFFA*.

## What the public says and why

Public opinion is more stable than the wording suggests. In Pew's 2023 survey, **74% of Republicans and Republican leaners disapproved of considering race in admissions, against 29% of Democrats** ([Pew, Topic: Affirmative action in college admissions](https://www.pewresearch.org/fact-sheet/topic-affirmative-action-in-college-admissions/)). Pew's December 2022 item found **82% said colleges should not consider race or ethnicity when choosing students** ([Pew short read, June 16, 2023](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2023/06/16/americans-and-affirmative-action-how-the-public-sees-the-consideration-of-race-in-college-admissions-hiring/)). Those figures are from a question that avoided the phrase "affirmative action."

Opposition is strongest among Republicans, and race and age shape it. Pew found **47% of Black adults approved of considering race, the only group more likely to approve than disapprove**, against 29% of White adults ([Pew short read, June 16, 2023](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2023/06/16/americans-and-affirmative-action-how-the-public-sees-the-consideration-of-race-in-college-admissions-hiring/)). A secondary report of the same survey gives White and Asian disapproval at 57% and 52% ([Washington Examiner](https://www.washingtonexaminer.com/news/1798658/half-of-people-disapprove-of-affirmative-action-as-supreme-court-opinions-loom-poll/)). Among adults under 30, opinion is split at 40% approve and 40% disapprove ([Pew, Demographic and partisan views](https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2023/06/08/demographic-and-partisan-views-about-race-and-ethnicity-in-college-admissions/)). Gallup's 2024 data show that 62% of Black adults aged 18–39 called the Supreme Court's ruling a good thing, against 44% of those over 40 ([NewsNation, citing Gallup](https://www.newsnationnow.com/race-in-america/black-americans-affirmative-action-ruling-gallup/)). Gender differences are documented mainly for sex-based preferences: in Gallup's data, women favour programmes for women more than men do, at 72% against 62% ([Gallup, Higher Support for Gender Affirmative Action Than Race](https://news.gallup.com/poll/184772/higher-support-gender-affirmative-action-race.aspx)). The sources do not give a regional breakdown from a major national survey, and no source tests whether class-based preferences draw different reactions.

The stated reason is mostly merit. Across four Gallup askings over 13 years, **67–70% chose the option that applicants should be admitted "solely on merit"** ([Gallup, The Harvard Affirmative Action Case and Public Opinion](https://news.gallup.com/opinion/polling-matters/243965/harvard-affirmative-action-case-public-opinion.aspx)). In hiring, **74% in Pew's 2019 survey said companies should consider only qualifications, even if that yields less diversity**, with Republicans at 90% and Democrats at 62% ([Pew short read, June 16, 2023](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2023/06/16/americans-and-affirmative-action-how-the-public-sees-the-consideration-of-race-in-college-admissions-hiring/)). Older polls add quotas and reverse discrimination: a 1995 Roper item found **63% opposed requiring businesses to hire a set number of minorities and women** ([UnderstandingPrejudice.org](https://www.understandingprejudice.org/readroom/articles/affirm.htm)). Analysts describe the deeper driver as principle: "the most fundamental factor behind opposition to affirmative action is one of principle" ([National Affairs](https://www.nationalaffairs.com/publications/detail/assessing-affirmative-action)).

Wording can flip the topline. Rasmussen's 2011 telephone poll found **63% opposed hiring preferences for women and minorities, but only 45% opposed "affirmative action programs" in general** ([Rasmussen Reports](https://www.rasmussenreports.com/public_content/politics/general_politics/july_2011/63_oppose_giving_women_minorities_special_treatment_when_it_comes_to_hiring)). Gallup's 2018 hiring item found 72% opposing "preferential hiring and promotion of blacks," with 43% opposing strongly ([Gallup, Affirmative Action and Public Opinion](https://news.gallup.com/opinion/polling-matters/317006/affirmative-action-public-opinion.aspx)). Over time, AP-NORC found support for preferential hiring rose from 18% in 2014 to 23% in 2018 ([AP-NORC](https://apnorc.org/projects/changing-attitudes-about-racial-inequality/)). This is a small rise, and it sits alongside a broad concept that still draws majority support.

## Conclusion

People oppose affirmative action for reasons that rank differently by audience. The most durable reason is principled: a government may not sort people by race or sex, and preferences treat individuals as group members. This objection does not depend on measured outcomes, so new evidence about mismatch, graduation or enrollment does not dispose of it. Merit and fairness arguments carry the most public weight in polls, and they are strongest among Republicans. The empirical case, especially on mismatch and stigma, is real but unsettled. The best causal studies conflict, and the post-2023 enrollment data are descriptive and contradictory in places, so claims that preferences harm beneficiaries should be held with less confidence than claims about principle.

The policy direction is clear, but its reach is not. Courts have narrowed race-conscious admissions, voters have adopted bans in several states, and federal contracting rules now target DEI practices. The next contest is over private programmes and contracting under § 1981 and over how far preliminary rulings will extend. Those cases, and any new data on post-*SFFA* enrollment and outcomes, will show whether the principled objection can be applied without losing the outreach and equal-opportunity programmes that even some critics support.
