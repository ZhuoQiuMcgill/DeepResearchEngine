# Law Targets Conduct, Not Personality Types

Law has not overlooked people with antisocial traits. It has mostly declined to create a legal category keyed to the personality itself, and it targets the conduct instead. Three problems explain this. "Sociopath" has no diagnostic status, and the closest clinical constructs measure different things. Personality-based tools predict violence for groups far better than for named individuals, and low base rates make false positives common. Human-rights law also limits detention based on a diagnosis. Where personality-linked regimes survive, as with Canada's dangerous-offender designation, they require a finding of past conduct and present risk. The alternative route is conduct-based protection, which is expanding. Coercive-control offences were enacted in New South Wales in 2024, Queensland in 2025 and Canada in 2026, and England and Wales passed the Crime and Policing Act 2026. The main remaining gaps are manipulation outside intimate relationships, deception that falls short of fraud, and workplace psychological abuse. The evidence for the personality-based route is weak, and the evidence for conduct-based protection is stronger.

## "Sociopath" names no diagnosis, and the clinical constructs measure different things

The word "sociopath" does not describe a clinical category. [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-06-psychopath-dangerous-criminal-justice.html) reports that psychopathy is described as a cluster of traits rather than a standalone diagnosis, and that it appears in neither the DSM nor the ICD. The nearest diagnostic concept, antisocial personality disorder (ASPD), requires at least three of seven criteria, and [APA, Psychiatry.org](https://www.psychiatry.org/news-room/apa-blogs/antisocial-personality-disorder-often-overlooked/) notes that a person must be at least 18 and show evidence of conduct disorder before age 15. ICD-11 drops the separate categories and uses severity plus a "dissociality" trait qualifier, which [ICD-diagnostica](https://icd-diagnostica.com/en/classification/dissociality-in-personality-disorder-or-personality-difficulty) describes as a matter of degree rather than a yes-or-no label. Psychopathy as measured by the PCL-R adds affective and interpersonal traits that ASPD does not require.

These definitional differences drive the prevalence figures, which is why any law would have to choose its construct first.

| Construct | How it is defined | Reported prevalence | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Psychopathy, any instrument | Trait cluster, no formal diagnosis | 4.5% of adults in a 2021 meta-analysis | [PMC meta-analysis](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8374040/) |
| Psychopathy, PCL-R | 20-item rating scale, "gold standard" | 1.2% of adults | [PMC meta-analysis](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8374040/) |
| ASPD, men | DSM-5-TR behavioural criteria | 0.6% to 4.3% in an older chapter | [ResearchGate chapter](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/365701809_Chapter_5_-_The_Epidemiology_of_Antisocial_Personality_Disorder) |
| Psychopathy, English and Welsh male prisoners | PCL-R cut-off of 30 | 7.7% (95% CI 5.2–10.9) | [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0160252709000247) |

The prison figures reflect selection into custody and cannot be applied to the general population. The 0.6–4.3% ASPD range comes from an older base, and no recent systematic review in the sources updates it.

## Personality labels predict groups well but individuals poorly

The predictive evidence supports group-level statements, not individual sorting. In a meta-analysis of 18 studies, the mean effect size for violence was 0.79 ([Salekin et al.](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/A-review-and-meta-analysis-of-the-psychopathy-and-Salekin-Rogers/b5abf35b80216d6be3c4ac200efc8a75a5d8e749)). A 2025 meta-analysis puts the effect size for institutional violence at d = 0.49, with poor predictive value for sexual offending ([Taylor & Francis](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00223891.2025.2469268)). These figures measure different outcomes and are not directly comparable, but both point to moderate validity. A further review concludes that the link between PCL-R scores and violence is only moderate at group level and small in institutional settings ([Taylor & Francis](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00223891.2021.1955693)). Across nine risk tools, the PCL-R performed comparably to others, and some tools did better ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/46094266_The_Efficacy_of_Violence_Prediction_A_Meta-Analytic_Comparison_of_Nine_Risk_Assessment_Tools)). One meta-analysis states there is little evidence that actuarial estimates can determine whether a specific patient will reoffend or be violent ([PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9643469/)).

Base rates make this worse. Because psychopathy is rare, misclassifying a low scorer as high is more common than the reverse, and lowering the cut-off increases false positives ([JAAPL](https://jaapl.org/content/jaapl/29/1/89.full.pdf)). Applying the PCL-R outside research samples, where prevalence is lower, raises the false-positive rate further ([Academia.edu](https://academia.edu/2899861/Ethical_Complications_with_the_Utilization_of_the_Psychopathy_Checklist_Revised_PCL-R_in_Custodial_Assessment)). Reliability also suffers in adversarial settings. Prosecution-retained experts gave defendants higher PCL-R scores than defence-retained experts (d = 1.08), and paired scores across these experts were poorly reliable ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/229777949_A_Review_and_Meta-Analysis_of_the_Psychopathy_Checklist_and_Psychopathy_Checklist-Revised_Predictive_Validity_of_Dangerousness)). The sources found no predictive accuracy for fraud, and no source gives the share of people with these traits who go on to cause serious harm.

## Personality-linked detention survives only when tied to conduct and risk

Where personality-based restraint has been upheld, it is anchored in conduct. In Kansas v. Hendricks, the US Supreme Court read the Kansas Act as requiring a finding of dangerousness linked to a "mental abnormality" or "personality disorder" ([Kansas v. Hendricks, onecle](https://law.onecle.com/ussc/521/521us346.html)), and the Court said the civil label would be displaced only on the clearest proof of punitive purpose or effect ([openjurist](https://openjurist.org/521/us/346)). In Canada, the Supreme Court in R. v. Lyons treated dangerous-offender designation as part of sentencing, based on past antisocial behaviour, with experts free to address psychopathy ([SCC, Lyons](https://decisions.scc-csc.ca/scc-csc/scc-csc/en/item/248/index.do?site_preference=normal&iframe=true)). An offender with antisocial personality disorder was held not to be a dangerous offender because his offences did not fit the pattern required by the statute ([TheCourt.ca](https://www.yorku.ca/osgoode/thecourt/2015/02/17/a-decrease-of-judicial-discretion-in-action-r-v-szostak/)). In R. v. Boutilier, the Court upheld the regime 8–1, although a BC judge had found it overbroad because a treatable offender could be designated for life ([Gary Botting](https://garybotting.com/publications/article/dr--gary-botting-scc-ruling-on-dangerous-offenders-to-have-massive-impact)).

Earlier personality-type laws failed. Minnesota enacted a "psychopathic personality" statute in 1939 ([WSIPP](https://www.wsipp.wa.gov/ReportFile/1534/Wsipp_Sexual-Predator-Commitment-Laws_Full-Report.pdf)). Sexual psychopath laws spread across about half of US states from 1938 and peaked in the 1960s, and most were later repealed. By 1990 about half of states had abolished their 1960s procedures, partly because not all violent sex offenders respond to the same therapy and because sex offenders were not regarded as mentally ill ([Mercer Law Review](https://digitalcommons.law.mercer.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1512&context=jour_mlr)). California repealed its statute in 1982 ([Seattle U Law Review](https://digitalcommons.law.seattleu.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1359&context=sulr)). The sources do not confirm whether Minnesota's statute remains in force in 2026, although WSIPP reports it was upheld by the Minnesota Supreme Court in 1994.

The English IPP sentence shows the cost of a premise that fails in practice. The European Court of Human Rights held in James, Wells and Lee v UK that IPP detention after the tariff breached Article 5(1) where prisoners lacked access to rehabilitation, while finding the sentence was not itself unlawful ([Garden Court North Chambers](https://gcnchambers.co.uk/echr-rules-ipp-sentences-breach-human-rights-art-51/)). The Court found the purpose was public protection rather than rehabilitation, even though the scheme assumed rehabilitative resources ([Oxford Human Rights Hub](https://ohrh.law.ox.ac.uk/james-wells-and-lee-v-uk-indefinite-detention-and-arbitrary-deprivations-of-liberty/)).

## Human-rights law restricts diagnosis-based detention

Under the ECHR, detaining a person "of unsound mind" requires that the person be reliably shown by objective medical expertise to be of unsound mind, that the disorder warrants confinement, and that detention lasts only while the disorder persists ([UK Law Reference, Winterwerp](https://uklawreference.com/cases/winterwerp-v-netherlands)). Deviation from prevailing norms cannot by itself justify detention ([ECHR Lawyer](https://echrlawyer.org/echr-case-law/winterwerp-v-the-netherlands/)). In K.A. v Austria, the Court said "unsound mind" has an autonomous meaning that need not match ICD or DSM categories, but it did not hold that every dominance-oriented personality structure is enough ([ECHRCaseLaw](https://www.echrcaselaw.com/en/echr-decisions/k-a-v-austria-forensic-therapeutic-confinement-domestic-violence-and-the-autonomous-meaning-of-mental-disorder-under-article-5-echr/)). A doctoral thesis notes that the Court has doubted whether dissocial personality alone is a true mental disorder for Article 5(1)(e) ([Yılmaz](https://library.oapen.org/bitstream/handle/20.500.12657/98501/1/Sentencing%20Mentally%20Disordered%20Offenders.pdf)).

The UN disability committee takes a stricter line. Its Article 14 guidelines state that detention on the basis of actual or perceived impairment is prohibited without exceptions ([CRPD Guidelines](https://covenantswatch.org.tw/wp-content/uploads/2015/12/CRPD_Guidelines_art.14_eng.pdf)). They also say schemes allowing detention on impairment, alongside dangerousness, are incompatible with Article 14 ([EHRC written evidence](https://committees.parliament.uk/writtenevidence/88557/pdf/)). The OHCHR states that detention based on perceived danger linked to disability is contrary to Article 14 ([OHCHR](https://www.ohchr.org/EN/NewsEvents/Pages/DisplayNews.aspx?NewsID=15183)). These positions conflict with impairment-plus-risk regimes, and the sources do not show how UK, Canadian or Australian courts have responded. The CRPD guidelines are not binding treaty text.

Advocates of a psychopathy-based approach exist but are few. One legal scholar argues that psychopathy should be recognised as a mental illness and that dangerous psychopaths can be preventively detained ([Levy, LSU](https://digitalcommons.law.lsu.edu/faculty_scholarship/15/)). That is a single-author position, and the sources found no clinical body recommending a personality diagnosis as a legal trigger for protective measures.

## Conduct-based protections are expanding, with gaps outside relationships

Existing law reaches much of the harmful behaviour, but it is organised by relationship type. New Zealand is not in the sources, so the focus is England and Wales, Australia, Canada, the US and the EU. In Australia, **New South Wales made coercive control a crime from 1 July 2024** against current or former intimate partners ([NSW Government](https://www.nsw.gov.au/coercive-control-the-law)), and **Queensland's offence has applied since 26 May 2025** and extends to family members and informal carers ([SBS](https://www.sbs.com.au/news/article/coercive-control-is-now-illegal-in-this-state-heres-what-it-means/4478i0naj)). Conviction rates are reported as relatively low ([ABC News](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2025-05-26/coercive-control-laws-domestic-violence/105295122)), and the AIHW says evidence on criminal justice approaches is limited ([AIHW](https://www.aihw.gov.au/family-domestic-and-sexual-violence/understanding-fdsv/coercive-control)). Canada's Bill C-16 received Royal Assent on 18 June 2026 and adds an intimate-partner coercion offence ([Canada.ca](https://www.canada.ca/en/department-justice/news/2026/06/canada-makes-femicide-first-degree-murder-as-all-three-major-criminal-code-reforms-become-law.html)). Commencement is contested: one source says most provisions took effect on 18 July 2026, another says the coercive-control offence is delayed for up to two years for training ([Legal AI Insights](https://legalaiinsights.com/risk-digest/canada-coercive-control-offence)), and a third says it was not in force as of 28 October 2026 ([Kazandjilaw](https://www.kazandjilaw.com/coercive-control-offence-in-canada/)). The most recent source is the one that states non-commencement, and the proclamation should be checked. The EU's Directive 2024/1385 covers psychological and economic violence, must be transposed by 14 June 2027 ([EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/EN/legal-content/summary/preventing-and-combating-violence-against-women-and-domestic-violence-throughout-the-european-union.html)), and is scoped to gender-based and domestic violence.

England and Wales has strengthened stalking tools. The Crime and Policing Act 2026 received Royal Assent on 29 April 2026 and lets courts make stalking protection orders on conviction, with breach as an offence ([legislation.gov.uk](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2026/20/section/129#section-129-1)). Stalking protection orders have been reported as "very rarely used", though the sources found only the headline of that report ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stalking_Protection_Act_2019)). Romance fraud shows where the gaps lie. Banks must reimburse eligible victims up to £85,000, but the rules exclude cryptocurrency and international transfers, and the FCA believes reported losses are only a fraction of the true figure ([Key Law](https://keylaw.co.uk/fca-urges-stronger-protection-for-victims-of-romance-fraud/)). One academic argues the law responds to romance fraud through fraud offences, which arguably leaves parts of the behaviour unpunished, and calls for a new offence where sexual activity is procured by deception ([Lancaster EPrints](https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/id/eprint/153573/)). The UK Fraud Strategy 2026–2029 focuses on civil penalties and a victim charter, not on the manipulation that precedes fraud ([K&L Gates](https://www.klgates.com/UK-Governments-Fraud-Strategy-Framework-2026-to-2029-5-6-2026)).

Workplace protection in Australia is weak in practice. In the first 15 months, 874 stop-bullying applications were lodged and only four produced an order ([Marsh](https://www.marsh.com/en-gb/services/workers-compensation/insights/workplace-bullying-update-in-and-out-of-the-fair-work-commission.html)). A later review found all but 12 matters withdrawn or resolved, with one order made ([Keypoint Law](https://www.keypointlaw.com.au/keynotes/fair-work-commission-anti-bullying-jurisdiction-has-fair-work-commission-stopped-bullies/)). The two sources describe different stages, so the figures are not directly comparable. Orders cannot award money ([Etheringtons](https://etheringtons.com.au/workplace-bullying-fair-work-commission/)). The sources found no England and Wales or EU offence covering psychological abuse by a non-partner, and no elder-abuse-specific criminal offence in the five jurisdictions.

US coverage is uneven and its count is disputed. One source lists eleven states with enacted coercive-control legislation as of May 2026 ([Narcissistic Abuse Rehab](https://www.narcissisticabuserehab.com/coercive-control-legislation-around-the-world/)), while another says most of these operate through civil remedies rather than standalone offences ([Psychology Today](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/so-sue-me/202609/the-new-state-laws-defining-abuse-without-a-single-bruise)). Colorado's HB26-1309 was signed in May 2026 ([Evara Consulting](https://evaraconsulting.substack.com/p/coercive-control-is-becoming-a-crime)). South Carolina's bill died in committee ([STAR Network](https://starnetwork.org/coercive-control-new-hampshire-colorado/)). A 2026 federal bill would create a public database of people convicted of violent crimes ([Bickerton Law](https://bickerton-law.com/2026/05/08/congress-considering-law-that-would-create-a-violent-criminal-offender-registry/)).

## Conclusion

The question assumes a gap that is real but narrower than it appears. The law already protects people from most of the conduct associated with antisocial traits, including stalking, threats, fraud, family violence and, increasingly, coercive control. What it does not do is treat the personality itself as the trigger. The reasons are substantive, not merely historical. The label has no stable diagnostic meaning, the best tools predict groups with moderate accuracy, and false positives multiply when the trait is rare. Human-rights law requires objective evidence of a true disorder and a link to confinement, and the UN disability committee would go further and bar impairment-based detention. The historical record of psychopathy-based and sexual-psychopath statutes, largely repealed, and the IPP experience both suggest that personality-based regimes fail when treatment is absent.

The practical implication is that reform effort is better directed at conduct and at the gaps that conduct-based law leaves. Manipulation outside intimate relationships, romance deception that stops short of fraud, and workplace psychological abuse are the areas where the evidence points to real exposure. Several questions remain open: whether Canada's offence is in force, whether Victoria's and South Australia's laws have commenced, and whether the stalking orders and coercive-control offences produce convictions. The sources do not establish how English courts have applied the coercive-control offence under the Serious Crime Act 2015, or how US courts treat the CRPD guidelines.
