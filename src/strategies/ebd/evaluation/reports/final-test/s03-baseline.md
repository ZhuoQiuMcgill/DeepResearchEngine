# Engineering Dropout Stems From Early Academic Shocks and Belonging Gaps

Engineering students leave for a mix of academic, social and personal reasons, and no single cause dominates. Most departures happen early: an ASEE paper reports that much attrition occurs in the first two years, with only about 15% after the second year ([ASEE](https://peer.asee.org/factors-impacting-retention-and-success-of-undergraduate-engineering-students.pdf)). Course difficulty, poor teaching and advising, and weak belonging are the most frequently reported reasons, and in several US studies belonging ranks highest ([Leaving Engineering, multi-year study](https://www.academia.edu/90118915/Leaving_Engineering_A_Multi_Year_Single_Institution_Study)). Students often leave despite grades similar to those of students who stay, which suggests that perceived difficulty, mismatched expectations and weak connection matter as much as ability. Mental health, financial strain and paid work add pressure, especially for students with fewer resources. Headline rates are hard to compare: a **41% leaving rate for US bachelor's entrants** and a **35% non-completion rate in Germany** use different definitions and cohorts ([NCES](https://nces.ed.gov/pubs2014/2014001rev.pdf); [DZHW](https://edocs.tib.eu/files/e01fb24/1881460169.pdf)). The evidence on what institutions can do is promising but mostly observational.

## Academic shocks in the first years drive many departures

The academic explanations are the most consistently reported. In a review of 50 engineering attrition studies, **28 cited high-school preparation, 27 cited classroom or academic climate, 23 cited grades and conceptual understanding, and 14 cited inadequate teaching and advising**. Only **4 named inadequate mathematics preparation specifically**, and ten found that leavers did not have greater conceptual difficulty than stayers ([Geisinger & Raman, IJEE 2013](https://www.ijee.ie/articles/Vol29-4/14_ijee2746ns.pdf)). That gap between perceived and measured difficulty is important: students who leave often do so because results fall below their expectations, not because they lack the ability to finish.

Gateway courses are the sharpest version of this pattern. A Mexican study found that Analytical Geometry, Calculus I, Calculus II and General Physics I carried high dropout risk across the programmes it examined ([Proofs: Student Performance](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/350358794_Proofs_Student_Performance_in_First-Year_Math_and_Physics_Courses_as_Predictor_of_Student_Dropout_in_Engineering_Programs)). A Brazilian study of 4,848 students identified the same four courses as high-risk in early semesters ([ResearchGate, Factors Affecting Engineering Students Dropout](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/330523818_Factors_Affecting_Engineering_Students_Dropout_A_Case_Study)). In an Indian context, difficulty in thermodynamics, multivariate calculus, mechanics and electric circuits was a main cause of sophomore attrition ([Identification of the engineering gateway subjects](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/330934398_Identification_of_the_engineering_gateway_subjects_in_the_second-year_engineering_common_curriculum)).

Early accumulated failure may matter more than any single course. An unreviewed Argentine preprint using administrative data for 16,868 students estimates that low early academic capital, defined as passing at most one subject in early semesters, has a causal effect about twice as large as first-time repetition of the gateway Algebra course ([arXiv 2604.22772](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.22772)). Mathematics preparation also has a measurable signal: at a UK-type system, diagnostic test results, use of a mathematics learning centre, and A-level statistics were significant factors, and students who did not enjoy mathematics were more likely to leave or switch ([Is mathematics a barrier for engineering?](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/0020739X.2023.2256319)). Taken together, the evidence points to a bottleneck in mathematics and physics during the first year or two, but it is hard to separate preparation from performance once a student is in the programme.

Grading is the least studied academic lever. Eleven studies in the 2013 review reported that low course grades drove students away regardless of conceptual understanding ([Geisinger & Raman](https://www.ijee.ie/articles/Vol29-4/14_ijee2746ns.pdf)), but no source found isolates the effect of grading policy, such as curving or pass-fail options, on engineering attrition.

## Belonging and identity shape who stays, and gaps fall unevenly

Belonging is the non-academic factor that appears most often. One review reports that academic factors and lack of belonging both contribute to decisions to leave, and that intention to complete an engineering major declines sharply at least two semesters before students leave ([Engineering Student Retention and Attrition Literature Review](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/315972539_Engineering_Student_Retention_and_Attrition_Literature_Review)). Within a multi-year study, a one-point rise on a five-point belonging scale was associated with a **37.9% drop** in choosing a technical major after leaving ([ResearchGate version](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/242073587_Leaving_Engineering_A_Multi-Year_Single_Institution_Study)). These are associations from self-reports, so it is unclear whether low belonging causes departure or reflects early disengagement.

The reasons differ by group, and much of the evidence on gender comes from the workforce rather than from students. A diary study of more than 40 undergraduate engineering students found that women often felt marginalised in group work, and that negative team dynamics made the field less appealing ([MIT News](https://news.mit.edu/2016/why-do-women-leave-engineering-0615)). Among women who dropped out of undergraduate engineering, many did not find practice-oriented subjects or collaborative projects in the first course, reported lower self-efficacy than men, and sometimes described a chilly classroom climate ([I am done with this!, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9415617/)). By contrast, a national study of 1,464 women who left engineering jobs found that work-family imbalance (16%), loss of interest (12%) and lack of advancement (11%) were the top reasons ([Women's Reasons for Leaving the Engineering Field](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5491652/)). These workforce reasons should not be transferred directly to students.

For Black, Latino and Indigenous students, the evidence centres on climate and foundational courses. Interviews with Black and Latino male students found unwelcoming climates, lack of same-race peers and inadequate preparation as barriers, and attrition was linked to poor experiences in foundational courses, lack of caring faculty and reconsidering the major ([Academic and social barriers, ASEE](https://peer.asee.org/academic-and-social-barriers-to-black-and-latino-male-collegians-success-in-engineering-and-related-stem-fields.pdf)). Stereotype threat and related belonging threats are cited as motives for attrition, with introductory programming singled out ([Belonging in Engineering for BLI students, NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10444688)). These are mostly small qualitative US samples, so national attrition figures by race within engineering were not found.

First-generation students show mixed evidence. One qualitative study found that first-generation engineering students reported significantly lower intent to persist, lower engineering identity and higher stress ([First generation college students in engineering](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/224360873_First_generation_college_students_in_engineering_A_qualitative_investigation_of_barriers_to_academic_plans)), and students from first-generation or low-income backgrounds are more likely to switch to a non-STEM major after one poor grade or a withdrawal from a foundational course ([Enduring commitment, ASEE](https://peer.asee.org/enduring-commitment-exploring-why-low-income-and-first-generation-students-choose-to-stay-in-engineering.pdf)). However, a 2024 study at a Hispanic-Serving Institution and an HBCU found that persistence was not related to first-generation status or financial need, and at the HBCU only SAT scores predicted persistence ([First-year engineering student persistence](https://doi.org/10.1080/2331186X.2024.2413810)).

## Money, paid work, family and mental health compound the strain

Financial and family pressures act mainly through time and stress. A Spanish study found that students who had a job or family responsibilities were more likely to drop out, and that dropout was more common among older students ([University Dropout in Engineering, Psicothema](https://www.psicothema.com/pdf/4707.pdf)). In a Brazilian study, many engineering students had to work to support themselves or their families, and students without financial aid were more exposed to economic pressure ([Predicting graduation and dropout rates](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s44217-025-01092-3)). A study of engineering undergraduates found that financial help reduced stress and let students focus on their studies, while long working hours were a source of stress ([Implications of Financial Support](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/382730164_Implications_of_Financial_Support_for_the_Academic_Efficacy_and_Mental_Health_and_Wellbeing_of_Engineering_Undergraduates)). In a German bachelor's dataset, around **25% of engineering, mathematics and science students** named financial problems as the most crucial dropout motive ([Behr, European Journal of Education 2021](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/ejed.12433)).

The financial evidence is contested. A survey of 189 Chilean engineering students found that socio-economic conditions and self-regulation were significant protective predictors of dropout intention ([Factors Associated with Dropout Intention](https://investigadores.uta.cl/en/publications/factors-associated-with-dropout-intention-in-engineering-educatio/)), and the 2024 two-institution study cited above found no link to financial need. A reasonable reading is that money matters most for students with little margin for error, and that its effect depends on whether aid reduces stress.

Mental health is widely linked to intentions to leave, but the causal path is not established. A 2025 study reports high rates of mental health distress among engineering undergraduates and low treatment use, with mental health cited as a motive for leaving ([Mental health and treatment use, JEE](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/jee.20629)). A civil engineering case study linked poor mental health to dropout intentions ([Mental health-related problems and dropout](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/360844034_Exploring_the_relationship_between_mental_health-related_problems_and_undergraduate_student_dropout_A_case_study_within_a_civil_engineering_program)). Yet in the Chilean survey, mental health was not statistically significant after controlling for other predictors ([Factors Associated with Dropout Intention, MDPI DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/bs16050717)). A Cal Poly brief states that **half of surveyed engineering students screened positive for a major mental health condition** and that unmet needs contribute to attrition, but its sample and method are not shown in the retrieved text ([Cal Poly research brief](https://ceng.calpoly.edu/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/Engineering-Student-Mental-Health-Research-Brief-for-Deans-2.pdf)). Stress is also normalised in engineering culture ([Engineering Student Retention and Attrition Literature Review](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/315972539_Engineering_Student_Retention_and_Attrition_Literature_Review)), which may reduce help-seeking, although no source tests that link directly.

## Reported rates depend heavily on how dropout is defined

The headline figures use different definitions, so they cannot be compared directly. The table below sets out the main measures.

| Source | What counts as leaving | Engineering figure |
|---|---|---|
| [NCES, US 2003–04 entrants to 2009](https://nces.ed.gov/pubs2014/2014001rev.pdf) | Leaving the field by switching to non-STEM or leaving postsecondary education | **41%** of bachelor's entrants in engineering/technologies; 62% for associate's |
| [DZHW, Germany 2020 graduates](https://edocs.tib.eu/files/e01fb24/1881460169.pdf) | Leaving the system without a first degree, including students who switched fields first | **35%** |
| [HESA, UK 2016/17 entrants](https://www.hesa.ac.uk/news/07-03-2019/experimental-uk-performance-indicators) | Non-continuation after year one (not a completion rate) | **4.2%** for top-entry engineering & technology students |
| [Purdue/Ohland, nine US institutions](https://www.purdue.edu/uns/x/2009b/090804OhlandEngineering.html) | Eight-semester retention across institutions | Retention ranged **37% to 66%**; engineering did not dropout more than other majors |

The NCES figure combines engineering with science technologies for small-sample reasons, and it excludes switches within STEM. The German measure is a cohort comparison, and extended study changes the reported rates. The **"around 50%" figure** that circulates in some US sources is not reproduced by national data. An ASEE 2025 paper states it without a primary source ([ASEE 2025 paper](https://nemo.asee.org/public/conferences/365/papers/47763/view)), and a small UC Davis study (N=7) reports a similar pattern that cannot be generalised ([UC Davis FYC paper](https://fycjournal.ucdavis.edu/sites/g/files/dgvnsk16091/files/media/documents/Investigating%20High%20Rates%20of%20Attrition.pdf)). Treat it as unverified.

German trends also depend on the analysis. DZHW reported engineering non-completion at universities falling from 48% to 36% in a 2014 analysis ([DZHW Forum Hochschule 4/2014](https://www.dzhw.eu/pdf/pub_fh/fh-201404.pdf)), then 32% in 2017 ([DZHW Forum Hochschule 1/2017](https://www.dzhw.eu/pdf/pub_fh/fh-201701.pdf)). A later brief gives 34% and 35% for engineering and 30% for universities of applied sciences, though its text is partly garbled, so exact category mapping could not be confirmed ([DZHW-Brief 05|2022](https://www.dzhw.eu/pdf/pub_brief/dzhw_brief_05_2022.pdf)). In the US, the NCES data show large institutional gaps for all STEM entrants, with **57% dropout at for-profit four-year institutions** versus about 20% at public institutions ([NCES](https://nces.ed.gov/pubs2014/2014001rev.pdf)). The NCES study is descriptive, and it did not collect motivation or institutional climate variables, so its reasons are inferred from grades rather than stated by students.

## Institutional responses show promise but weak causal evidence

Advising, first-year courses and active learning consistently appear as retention-associated practices, but the evidence is mostly observational. A study of 1,228 first-year students, not restricted to engineering, found that strengths-based advising was associated with higher first-year retention and four-year graduation ([Intrusive advising](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/359969919_Effectiveness_of_intrusive_advising_of_engineering_first-year_students_using_tailored_freshman_seminars)). A mechanical engineering advising pilot reported 76% first-cohort retention compared with 28% for the prior cohort, but this is a single pre/post comparison ([ASEE pilot strategy](https://peer.asee.org/pilot-strategy-to-enhance-mechanical-engineering-student-retention-through-active-academic-advising-and-early-engagement.pdf)). An Alaska first-year course reported retention rising from 79.5% to 87.4%, but the authors state this was not significant at the 5% level (p = 0.08) ([Improving student success and retention](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/283758397_Improving_student_success_and_retention_rates_in_engineering_An_innovative_approach_for_first-year_courses)).

The strongest systematic evidence is for small-group learning. A meta-analysis in science, mathematics, engineering and technology found increased persistence, and states that an effect of 0.46 on persistence corresponds to a **22% reduction in attrition** from SMET courses ([Felder et al. meta-analysis](https://petersj.people.charleston.edu/CCLI/PDF/Efficacy_of_Student-active_Learning_Pedagogies.pdf)). That meta-analysis dates from the late 1990s. A 2010 review found that most engineering attrition studies examined causes or predicted at-risk students, rather than evaluating interventions ([Who leaves and who stays?](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.11120/ened.2010.05020026)), and no randomised trial of advising or active learning on engineering attrition was found.

## Conclusion

The most defensible answer is that engineering students leave when early academic setbacks collide with weak belonging, financial or personal strain, and poor advising. Attrition is concentrated in the first two years, so the most critical window for action is early. Gateway mathematics and physics courses are repeatedly named as bottlenecks, but accumulated early failure and perceived difficulty may matter as much as any single course.

Institutions should treat belonging and advising as first-order concerns rather than extras. The evidence for them is promising but observational, so the sensible next step is to test interventions with comparison groups and to measure stated reasons alongside grades. Readers comparing rates should first check definitions: an NCES, DZHW or HESA figure answers a different question. Until national, engineering-specific, reason-based data are available for a recent cohort, the most reliable picture combines a 35–41% leaving rate with a consistent set of early academic and belonging causes.
