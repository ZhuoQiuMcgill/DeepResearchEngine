# Academic failure and early-year struggle drive engineering exits

Engineering students leave mainly because they struggle academically in the first year, and the most consistent signal is weak mathematics performance. Belonging, motivation and expectations come next, and money, paid work and mental health are secondary and contested. Roughly a third of German engineering bachelor's students at universities leave without a first degree, per the DZHW series ([DZHW Brief 03/2020](https://www.dzhw.eu/pdf/pub_brief/dzhw_brief_03_2020.pdf)). For the US, a secondary estimate puts engineering dropout at 40–50%, and about half of that attrition falls in the first year ([ASEE PEER](https://peer.asee.org/student-dropout-prediction-in-regional-universities-using-automated-machine-learning); [Pittsburgh-based ASEE paper](https://peer.asee.org/engineering-attrition-student-characteristics-and-educational-initiatives.pdf)). The evidence is strongest for the German and US systems and thin for the UK and India, where no engineering-specific recent dropout figure was found. Most reason studies are single-institution, self-report or small-sample work, so the rankings below describe patterns, not measured national causes.

## Academic performance and first-year mathematics lead the reasons

The most frequently reported reason is academic: in a review of 50 engineering attrition studies, classroom climate and grades each appeared in more than 20 supporting studies ([Geisinger & Raman, IJEE 2013](https://www.ijee.ie/articles/Vol29-4/14_ijee2746ns.pdf)). A survey of 624 students at eight Spanish universities found that difficulty, poor academic performance and poor relationships with professors were the main reasons for leaving ([Factors Affecting Engineering Students Dropout](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/330523818_Factors_Affecting_Engineering_Students_Dropout_A_Case_Study)). German data point the same way: a 2017 DZHW report gives performance problems as the most important reason for leaving, at **89% of engineering dropouts at universities and 86% at universities of applied sciences** ([DZHW Forum Hochschule 1/2017](https://www.dzhw.eu/pdf/pub_fh/fh-201701.pdf)). Longitudinal data sharpen the mathematics point. At the University of Louisville, completing the four-course mathematics sequence was associated with a **93% graduation rate**, against a **68%** first-year retention metric ([IEEE](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/9028542/)). A 3,889-student Spanish study found that entrance exam scores, failure rates and exam absenteeism were strongly associated with first-year dropout, while gender was not significant ([IEEE Xplore](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/11150407/)).

Not everyone agrees that poor preparation is the root cause. The same review found that **ten of the fifty studies** reported leavers did not have greater conceptual difficulties than stayers, and it notes that contradictory GPA findings remain unexplained ([Geisinger & Raman, IJEE 2013](https://www.ijee.ie/articles/Vol29-4/14_ijee2746ns.pdf)). A low grade may therefore reflect disengagement as much as weak ability, and the evidence does not settle which is primary.

## Belonging, expectations and interest explain the rest

Motivation and belonging appear often enough to be treated as a second cause. A multi-year single-institution US study rated lack of belonging as a stronger influence on leaving than academic factors, though that finding comes from a secondary summary ([Leaving Engineering](https://www.academia.edu/2360757/Leaving_Engineering_A_Multi_Year_Single_Institution_Study)). At UCL, perceptions of future careers, motivation, expectations and confidence were the main contributors to staying or leaving, based on 128 surveys and 15 interviews ([Staying or leaving, European Journal of Engineering Education](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/03043797.2020.1711707)). The German framework treats motivation and unmet expectations as a separate cause that runs alongside performance and financial reasons ([DZHW slides](https://www.dzhw.eu/pdf/pub_vt/21/2017-02-18_vortrag_studienabbruch_isleib.pdf)). Many leavers entered with vague ideas of what engineers do, and some left because another field looked more appealing ([Geisinger & Raman, IJEE 2013](https://www.ijee.ie/articles/Vol29-4/14_ijee2746ns.pdf)). Across studies the two sides differ in method. Academic claims rest on predictive grades and course sequences, while motivation claims rest on interviews and leaver surveys, which are open to self-report bias.

## Money, paid work and mental health are contested

Financial and work-related reasons appear more often in self-reports than in measured outcomes, so their causal weight is uncertain. About one-fifth of all German dropouts cited financial problems as decisive, without an engineering breakdown ([DZHW Forum Hochschule](https://www.dzhw.eu/pdf/pub_fh/fh-201002.pdf)). A Brazilian engineering school found that **71%** of surveyed students agreed that students who work alongside study are more prone to dropout, but these are perception responses, not dropout outcomes ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s44217-025-01092-3)).

The two sources on paid work conflict. A survival-analysis study of term-time employment finds that full-time work alongside full-time study lowers completion, and that working more than about **20 hours a week** carries the same dropout risk as full-time work ([Working While Studying, ERIC](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1082237)). A related paper argues that the higher exit rate among non-working students reflects academic difficulty pushing students to stop working, rather than work causing dropout ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/272122514_Working_while_studying_the_impact_of_term-time_employment_on_dropout_rates)). A separate analysis of student employment reports that employment had no influence on dropout and that academic performance is the main predictor, though it is not specific to engineering ([Student Employment as a Possible Factor of Dropout](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/340876609_Student_Employment_as_a_Possible_Factor_of_Dropout)). This report follows the ERIC finding that heavy work hours matter, because it tracks students over time, but treats the causal link as unproven. The engineering-specific evidence does not decide the question.

Mental health is linked to leaving in a civil engineering case study at a private university, where dropout rose after COVID-19 mainly because of mental health problems ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9160475/)). A US engineering-deans brief reports that **50%** of surveyed engineering students screened positive for a major mental health condition, but its sampling is not described ([Cal Poly brief](https://ceng.calpoly.edu/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/Engineering-Student-Mental-Health-Research-Brief-for-Deans-2.pdf)). No large longitudinal engineering study linking diagnosed mental health to leaving was found.

## How many leave, when, and who leaves most

Rates depend heavily on how each system counts leavers, so the figures below are not directly comparable.

| System | Measure | Reported figure | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Germany, universities | Engineering bachelor dropout (DZHW) | Mechanical 35%, civil 32% (graduating year 2020) | [DZHW Brief 03/2020](https://www.dzhw.eu/pdf/pub_brief/dzhw_brief_03_2020.pdf) |
| Germany, universities | Including delayed graduates | 35% at universities; 20% at universities of applied sciences | [DZHW-Brief 05\|2022](https://www.dzhw.eu/pdf/pub_brief/dzhw_brief_05_2022.pdf) |
| Germany | Federal Statistical Office, six semesters | About 15% (as reported by a student-advice magazine) | [stuzubi.de](https://stuzubi.de/online-magazin/studiengaenge-mit-den-meisten-studienabbrecherinnen/) |
| United States | Engineering dropout, secondary estimate | 40–50% | [ASEE PEER](https://peer.asee.org/student-dropout-prediction-in-regional-universities-using-automated-machine-learning) |
| United Kingdom | Engineering continuation rate | 88%, against a 94% all-subject average | [Taylor & Francis](https://doi.org/10.1080/13596748.2026.2647621) |

The German figures conflict. The DZHW series (32–35%) and the 15% Federal Statistical Office figure use different cohorts and methods, and no source reconciles them. The DZHW series is the more useful guide because it is engineering-specific and tracks a defined cohort, whereas the 15% figure comes via a secondary magazine. The UK continuation rate is not a dropout rate and no recent UK engineering-specific non-completion figure was found. No Indian national figure was found at all.

Timing is consistent across systems. Acatech reports that most German engineering dropouts and course changes happen in the first two subject semesters ([acatech](https://www.acatech.de/publikation/studienabbruch-in-den-ingenieurwissenschaften-hochschuluebergreifende-analyse-und-handlungsempfehlungen/download-pdf/?lang=de_excerpt)). Across all German fields, **47% of dropouts** leave in semesters 1–2 ([studienabbruch-und-weiter.de](https://studienabbruch-und-weiter.de/zahlen-daten-fakten-studienabbruch/)). Engineering does not show a sharper early exit than other fields in the sources, because few sources compare the two directly.

Some groups leave more often. One quantified source reports **25%** attrition in STEM for first-generation students against **13%** for continuing-generation students, though it is a secondary citation of one study ([ASEE](https://peer.asee.org/persistence-and-the-pandemic-retention-of-historically-underrepresented-first-year-engineering-students-before-and-after-covid-19.pdf)). Women who left STEM were more likely to switch majors (32%) than men (26%), but that measures switching, not leaving university ([Inside Higher Ed on NCES](https://www.insidehighered.com/news/2013/11/27/study-tracks-attrition-rates-stem-majors)). No national engineering-specific figure by gender or family income was found.

## What helps students stay

The strongest evaluated design is a longitudinal field experiment in which women beginners with female peer mentors showed higher belonging, motivation, confidence and retention in engineering, and the effects persisted one year after mentoring ended ([PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1613117114)). A high-risk curriculum at a private research university, which decoupled first-year physics and calculus and added support, raised first-year STEM retention from **86.1% to 93.6%** and second-year retention from **76.4% to 85.4%** (p < 0.001). That is a treatment-control comparison, and the excerpt does not confirm randomisation ([Frontiers in Education](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2022.1087064/full)). Other programmes report gains with weak designs. An engineering bridge programme stayed close to the college average, and its ASEE version found no significantly higher retention ([ASEE](https://peer.asee.org/implementation-and-effects-of-a-bridge-program-to-increase-student-learning-and-retention-in-engineering-programs.pdf)). Peer-mentoring gains may reflect self-selection, as the authors themselves note ([ASEE](https://peer.asee.org/peer-mentoring-impact-on-mentees-and-comparison-with-non-participants.pdf)). No systematic review of engineering retention interventions was found, and no source asked leavers what would have kept them enrolled.

## Conclusion

The most defensible answer is that engineering students leave because they struggle academically early on, especially in mathematics, and because weak engagement, belonging and expectations make it easier to quit once that struggle starts. Money, paid work and mental health matter for some students, but the evidence for their causal weight is contested, and the paid-work question is not settled for engineers. Germany provides the most reliable national series, and the rate there has stayed in the low-to-mid 30s percent, but no comparable recent trend exists for the US, UK or India.

For institutions, the practical implication is to target the first year, especially mathematics, and to test interventions with comparison groups and follow-up to graduation. The sharpest gap in the evidence is the absence of leavers' own accounts of what would have kept them enrolled, along with national, engineering-specific data on gender, income and mental health.
