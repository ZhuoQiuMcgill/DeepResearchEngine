# Why Ethicists Reject Eugenics: Consent, Equality, Uncertainty

The ethical case against eugenics rests on four linked objections. Coerced sterilisation violates bodily integrity and consent, and the UN has called forced sterilisation of disabled girls and young women "systemic violence" that causes "irreversible harm" ([OHCHR](https://www.ohchr.org/en/press-releases/2017/10/forced-sterilization-young-women-disabilities-must-end-un-rights-expert-says)). Classifying people by disability, race, class or ethnicity demeans them and can entrench inequality. Selection based on weak prediction, and decisions about whose life is worth having, risk harm even when voluntary. The historical record gives these objections weight: Japan's Supreme Court ruled its forced-sterilisation law unconstitutional in July 2024 ([ABC News](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2024-07-03/japan-rules-mandatory-sterilisation-law-unconstitutional/104055364)), and Virginia apologised for its programme in 2002 ([Encyclopedia Virginia](https://encyclopediavirginia.org/entries/buck-v-bell-1927/)). The question's premise, that most people, scholars and institutions consider eugenics unethical today, is not established by the evidence. No source measures views on eugenics as a whole, and the polls concern embryo screening. The better-supported claim is that coercive, state-run eugenics is widely condemned, while voluntary selection of embryos remains contested.

## Coerced sterilisation turns on consent and bodily integrity

The clearest ethical objection concerns non-consensual sterilisation. The UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities calls for banning all medical interventions on people with disabilities without their free and informed consent ([OHCHR, A/73/161](https://www.ohchr.org/en/documents/thematic-reports/a73161-report-special-rapporteur-rights-persons-disabilities)). A 2014 interagency UN statement says no child, including a disabled child, should undergo non-therapeutic sterilisation ([UNAIDS](https://www.unaids.org/sites/default/files/media_asset/201405_sterilization_en.pdf)). The main weakness is that "consent" was often given by others. The UN Special Rapporteur reported sterilisations carried out at the request and with the consent of judges, health professionals, family members or guardians ([OHCHR](https://www.ohchr.org/en/press-releases/2017/10/forced-sterilization-young-women-disabilities-must-end-un-rights-expert-says)). In Japan, about 8,500 people were sterilised with apparent consent, but lawyers say these cases were likely "de facto forced" because of pressure ([Gulf News/AFP](https://gulfnews.com/world/asia/japans-top-court-rules-forced-sterilisation-law-unconstitutional-1.103405777)).

The historical scale is large, though totals differ by source. In the United States, Buck v. Bell upheld compulsory sterilisation 8–1 in 1927 ([Encyclopedia Virginia](https://encyclopediavirginia.org/entries/buck-v-bell-1927/)). Estimates of US victims range from about 36,000 by 1940 ([Institute for Justice](https://ij.org/center-for-judicial-engagement/programs/victims-of-abdication/buck-v-bell-1927/)) to as many as 70,000 across the twentieth century ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/sections/health-shots/2016/03/07/469478098/the-supreme-court-ruling-that-led-to-70-000-forced-sterilizations)). Britannica notes that Buck v. Bell "has never been formally overturned," though later rulings held forced sterilisation unconstitutional ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Buck-v-Bell)). Nazi Germany's 1933 law mandated sterilisation for conditions presumed hereditary ([USHMM](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/eugenics)), with estimates of **360,000 to 400,000** sterilisations ([USHMM](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/forced-sterilization-a-form-of-nazi-persecution); [EBSCO](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/politics-and-government/nazi-eugenics/)). Germany's record of accountability is weak: the law was never formally abolished, and only the hereditary-court rulings were declared Nazi injustice in 1988 ([AUPress](https://read.aupress.ca/read/psychiatry-and-the-legacies-of-eugenics/section/2245b2ec-330e-4933-94d6-c618024254f5)).

Apologies have typically followed decades later and often after litigation. In Alberta, **2,834 people were sterilised** out of 4,785 cases brought before the Eugenics Board ([University of Toronto](https://exhibits.library.utoronto.ca/exhibits/show/stateinthebedroom/coercedsterilization)). Premier Ralph Klein apologised in 1999 but said the operations were legal at the time and did not call them criminal ([CBC](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/alberta-apologizes-for-forced-sterilization-1.169579)). Sweden's totals conflict: a special commission found some **63,000** people were sterilised, about half forcibly ([EDF](https://www.edf-feph.org/sweden-forced-sterilisation/)), while a Borneo Post/AFP report gives a figure of 230,000 victims for 1999 payments that no other source corroborates ([Borneo Post](https://www.pressreader.com/malaysia/the-borneo-post/20170327/282505773429391)). Japan's figures also conflict: AFP-based reports give about 16,500 forced sterilisations ([NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/world/japans-top-court-orders-government-compensate-disabled-people-forcibly-rcna160306)), Al Jazeera gives about 25,000 ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2019/4/24/japans-abe-apologises-to-victims-of-forced-sterilisation)), and a Japanese human rights bulletin claims no forced sterilisations were carried out under the 1948 law itself. That last claim conflicts with the other sources and should be treated as contested.

## Disability and equality objections target classification itself

The second objection holds that classifying people as undesirable is itself a wrong. Early eugenic discourse was pervaded by racist, sexist, ableist, xenophobic and classist assumptions, and "feeble-mindedness" was entangled with ideas of race, class and gender ([SEP, "Eugenics"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/eugenics/)). Disability theorists add an "expressivist" argument: screening followed by selective termination sends the message that it is better not to exist than to have a disability ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/eugenics/)). This argument is contested. Some liberal eugenicists reply that one can value the person while devaluing the disability, and disability scholars question the assumptions about flourishing behind claims that certain conditions are plainly disadvantageous ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/eugenics/)).

The equality objection has a second strand that applies to voluntary choice. Critics argue that taking race to be morally neutral in embryo selection is a veiled way of separating new practices from historical eugenics, because social bias shapes choices ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/eugenics/)). The European Society of Human Genetics warned that without oversight, embryo polygenic testing "could easily lead to discrimination and the stigmatization of certain conditions" ([Medical Xpress](https://medicalxpress.com/news/2022-01-polygenic-scores-pre-implantation-genetic-unproven.html)). The Oviedo Convention names genetic discrimination explicitly ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/238341888_The_Oviedo_Convention_A_European_Legal_Framework_at_the_Intersection_of_Human_Rights_and_Health_Law)). The sources do not show that a specific equality test has been applied to eugenic classification beyond these texts.

## Liberal defenders dispute whether the objections reach voluntary choice

The strongest defence of selection rests on a distinction between old and new practices. Advocates of liberal eugenics agree that past eugenic practices were morally wrong but argue that current individual reproductive technologies differ, since they aim at the child's own good rather than at a public goal ([SEP, "Eugenics"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/eugenics/); [SEP, Fall 2026](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2026/entries/eugenics/)). Savulescu's procreative beneficence holds that parents should choose the child whose life is expected to go best ([SEP, Fall 2016](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2016/entries/eugenics/)). Buchanan and colleagues reject disability-rights objections to genetic testing but warn of "backdoor eugenics," and argue that entrenched reproductive rights make a return to state eugenics unlikely ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC80823/)).

Critics reply that the distinction is weaker than it looks. Sparrow argues that the consequentialist justifications used by liberal eugenicists sit uneasily with their promise to preserve reproductive liberty, and that government incentives or campaigns could still amount to state-sponsored control over reproduction ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/eugenics/)). Critics also argue that rejecting state-mandated eugenics does not solve the problem if social pressure produces the same homogenising effect ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/eugenics/)). Michael Sandel argues that designing children conflicts with "openness to the unbidden" and with treating children as gifts ([SuperSummary](https://www.supersummary.com/the-case-against-perfection/summary/)). The Nuffield Council's report, in a quoted passage, argues that restricting selection by law is the worst option because it opens the door to other restrictions on reproductive decisions, though this is a quoted view rather than the Council's own conclusion ([Nuffield Council](https://cdn.nuffieldbioethics.org/wp-content/uploads/Genetics-and-human-behaviour.pdf)). The debate remains open; the sources describe it but do not settle it.

## Present-day screening and the disputed premise

The objections now apply to a commercial market. Companies such as Genomic Prediction, Orchid, Herasight and Nucleus offer polygenic embryo selection, and at least two already offer testing for intelligence ([Live Science](https://www.livescience.com/health/genetics/new-tech-allows-parents-to-score-ivf-embryos-for-desirable-traits-and-its-in-desperate-need-of-regulation-opinion)). The American Society for Reproductive Medicine concluded that polygenic embryo screening "is not ready for clinical use" and should not be offered as a reproductive service at this time ([ASRM](https://www.asrm.org/news-and-events/asrm-news/press-releasesbulletins/asrm-ethics-and-practice-committees-release-new-report-concluding-polygenic-embryo-screening-is-not-ready-for-clinical-use/)). The European Society of Human Genetics called the practice an "unproven, unethical practice" ([EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/941151)). A 2024 study found polygenic scores gave inconsistent embryo rankings, and repeating one method gave different results ([Medical Xpress](https://medicalxpress.com/news/2024-05-polygenic-scores-inaccurate-highly-inconsistent.html)). Defenders reply that polygenic scores can identify higher-risk siblings and that selection may reduce risk ([Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41431-022-01237-0)). Companies argue that critics rely on population-level results that do not show performance within families ([Herasight](https://www.herasight.com/blog/embryo-screening-myths)). The sources do not include an independent study of within-family accuracy for any trait.

Regulation is patchy. The Oviedo Convention bans heritable genome modification (Article 13) and non-medical sex selection (Article 14) for states parties, but it does not name polygenic trait selection ([WorldLII](https://www.worldlii.org/int/other/COETS/1997/1.html)). The UK bans non-medical sex selection under its 2008 HFE Act ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_Fertilisation_and_Embryology_Act_2008)), while polygenic embryo screening "is largely unregulated in the United States" ([ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2024/05/240514141244.htm)).

The premise is where the evidence is most divided. A JAMA Network Open survey of 1,427 US respondents found **72.0% approved** of polygenic embryo screening overall, but only **36.0%** approved of screening for behavioural traits and **30.3%** for physical traits ([JAMA Network Open](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2818674); [News-Medical](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20240515/Understanding-public-perception-of-polygenic-embryo-screening-for-IVF.aspx); [medRxiv](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2023.10.13.23297022.full.pdf)). A 2026 study of more than 2,000 people in the US and China reported that **61%** were open to testing for non-medical traits, which the authors describe as higher than earlier work suggested ([EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1141221); [NewsBreak](https://www.newsbreak.com/psypost-332799984/4887881691969-how-people-really-pick-ivf-embryos-isn-t-what-they-claim)). Set against the premise that most people consider eugenics unethical, these figures suggest that majorities accept screening for disease and that many accept trait selection as well, even though the surveys were not designed to test attitudes to eugenics. The 2024 survey's authors also note that a majority approved screening while expressing strong concerns about sliding into eugenics ([EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1044330)). The report therefore does not treat A1 as established. It also does not treat the survey results as proof that most people reject the eugenics objection; the evidence does not decide that question.

## Conclusion

The ethical reasons usually given against eugenics are consistent: non-consensual sterilisation violates bodily integrity, classification by disability, race or class demeans people and invites discrimination, and state or expert power over reproduction is dangerous. These objections have strong support for historical coercive programmes, where the record of sterilisations and later apologies is well documented, even if totals differ widely between sources.

The harder question is voluntary selection. Defenders argue that individual choice aims at the child's welfare, and critics reply that the same aims and biases can operate through markets, incentives and social pressure. Present-day evidence on embryo prediction is weak and disputed, and the surveys show substantial public willingness to screen. A careful reader should therefore distinguish three claims: coercive eugenics is condemned, selective reproduction is contested, and the claim that most people, scholars and institutions reject eugenics as such remains unestablished by the evidence reviewed here.
