# How Colonial Trade and Labour Fed American Capitalism

The mainland colonies contributed to capitalist growth mainly through staple exports, shipping and insurance, merchant credit, and bound labour, but the size of each contribution remains disputed. In 1768–1772, Middle Colony commodity exports ran at roughly **8–10% of regional income**, and invisible earnings from shipping and commercial services added about **44% of commodity export value** ([NBER w14334](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w14334/w14334.pdf)). Chesapeake tobacco exports reached about **100 million pounds by 1776** ([Chesapeake Region, Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/history/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/chesapeake-region)), and by 1770–1775 Philadelphia residents owned **76.6% of registered tonnage** ([Appendix to NBER w14334](https://back.nber.org/appendix/w14334/Appendix_ExportEstimates%20_Final.pdf)). Credit mostly flowed from London and Glasgow merchant houses to planters and storekeepers, while colonial paper money and land banks created local credit whose effects historians still dispute. Aggregate growth is the weakest link: estimates of colonial growth range from **about 0.04% to 0.48% a year** ([Estimating Early American GDP](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/249036837_Estimating_Early_American_Gross_Domestic_Product)). Whether colonial society was capitalist at all remains unresolved, so the answer depends heavily on how "capitalism" is defined.

## Staple exports and shipping gave colonial trade its scale

The best-sourced value figures come from 1768–1772 and cover mainly the Middle Colonies and the Chesapeake and Lower South. New England's export earnings in that period were about **11.1% of regional product**, with much of that from shipping services, and salted fish went largely to Mediterranean ports ([Lindert, NBER w19861](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w19861/w19861.pdf)). One economic history of colonial America reports that the northern colonies ran an average annual favourable trade balance of **£198,000** over 1768–1772 ([The Economy of Colonial America](https://dokumen.pub/the-economy-of-colonial-america-9780231881340.html)). No source reviewed gives a consolidated export total for all thirteen colonies, so these regional figures should not be summed.

Staple crops were concentrated and fast-growing. By 1740, **Charleston's rice exports accounted for about 60% of the lower southern colonies' export value** ([Economy of South Carolina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_South_Carolina)). By the early 1770s, rice exports reached **£312,000 a year** and indigo **£113,000 a year**, together more than half the value of Upper South tobacco exports ([The Economy of Colonial America](https://dokumen.pub/the-economy-of-colonial-america-9780231881340.html)). Tobacco expanded even faster in volume while its unit price collapsed: it fell from sixteen pence to five pence a pound in the 1630s and to one penny by 1670 ([Staple Economies, Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/history/news-wires-white-papers-and-books/staple-economies)). Output per worker is reported on conflicting bases. One source gives 720 pounds per farmer for 1619–1629 and 1,710 per worker for 1680–1699 ([Tobacco in the American colonies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tobacco_in_the_American_colonies)), while a thesis gives output per hand doubling from 911 to 1,877 pounds between 1640 and 1699 ([Smoke on the water](https://www.academia.edu/81074468/Smoke_on_the_water_an_historical_archaeological_assessment_of_maritime_sources_of_productivity_change_in_the_early_English_tobacco_trade)). The two series are not reconciled, so the productivity gain is clear in direction but not in size.

The Navigation Acts shaped who captured these profits. They gave English subjects and ships a legal monopoly over colonial trade ([Navigation Acts, Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/history/modern-europe/british-and-irish-history/navigation-acts)), and enumerated products such as sugar, indigo and tobacco could be shipped directly only to England or another English colony ([Navigation Acts, Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/event/Navigation-Acts)). Robert Paul Thomas estimated the gross burden of the system at **$3.1 million in 1770**, while noting that this must be weighed against the benefits of empire membership, and roughly three-quarters of colonial exports had to pass through Britain first ([Navigation Acts, Economic Burden](https://www.encyclopedia.com/history/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/navigation-acts-economic-burden-american-colonies-issue)). Older Whig and Progressive writers such as Bancroft and Beard saw a considerable burden, but colonial ships fared well on New England–West Indies routes, and smuggling and re-export were common. New England merchants, for example, imported Southern tobacco into Boston and re-exported it abroad ([Restoration and the Navigation Acts](https://mises.org/mises-daily/restoration-and-navigation-acts)). The Acts raised colonial costs, but merchants adapted, and no source gives a quantified net effect on merchant profits.

Northern ports grew into an integrated commercial system. Land argues that Philadelphia, New York and Boston formed a "cohesive port complex" from the early eighteenth century ([H-Net review of Colonial Ports, Global Trade](https://www.h-net.org/reviews/showrev.php?id=60322)). Philadelphia doubled New York's shipbuilding output by the 1770s, and Boston remained the region's top shipbuilder ([Colonial Ports, Chapter 2](https://brill.com/display/book/9789004542709/BP000003.xml?language=en)). By the 1760s, New York and Philadelphia had passed Boston in population and economic growth ([National Park Service](https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/the-atlantic-empire-of-peter-faneuil.htm)). Insurance followed trade: John Copson opened an insurance office in Philadelphia in 1721, and by 1759 there were three brokerages in Philadelphia and four in New York ([Insurance in Colonial America](https://commonplace.online/article/insurance-in-colonial-america/)). Benjamin Franklin's Philadelphia Contributionship, begun in 1751, had about two thousand policies worth almost $2 million by 1781 (same source).

## Merchant houses, not joint-stock firms, carried most credit

Joint-stock companies mattered most in early settlement. The Virginia Company of London, chartered in 1606, relied on private investors to pay for transporting colonists and building Jamestown ([Virginia Places](https://www.virginiaplaces.org/government/govcolony.html)). By 1612, with no dividends paid, it struggled to attract investors and turned to share lotteries, which were only marginally successful ([Virginia Places](http://www.virginiaplaces.org/settleland/virginiacompany.html)). Its charter was revoked in 1624, and tobacco made the colony profitable only after the Company had failed ([Historic Jamestowne](https://historicjamestowne.org/history/virginia-company/)). The sources reviewed do not quantify the share of Chesapeake or Charleston trade financed through joint-stock vehicles after 1624.

Trade credit after that rested on private houses. London consignment merchants bought goods on **12–15 months' credit** for Chesapeake correspondents, expecting payment in tobacco or bills before their own suppliers' credit expired ([London Record Society](https://www.british-history.ac.uk/london-record-soc/vol15/vii-xxviii)). Glasgow firms placed factors in Chesapeake stores, and the Union of 1707 gave Scottish merchants legal access to the English colonies ([Glasgow History](https://glasgowhistory.co.uk/tobacco-lords)). In South Carolina, Charleston export merchants usually acted as correspondents for British firms, and most rice shipping was in British hands ([Cowes and the Rice Trade](https://www.iwhistory.org.uk/RM/rice/rice.htm)). Credit reached down to small planters: a Glasgow-based study estimated that Virginia store credit extended to planters or young men totalled about **£2 million**, mostly in accounts under £100 ([Paying for Goods and Services](https://www.encyclopedia.com/history/news-wires-white-papers-and-books/paying-goods-and-services)). Some individuals owed London houses as much as **£11,000** ([Colonial Williamsburg](https://research.colonialwilliamsburg.org/DigitalLibrary/view/index.cfm?amp=&doc=ResearchReports\RR0066.xml&highlight=)).

Debt was therefore a mechanism tying colonial production to British finance. Walsh finds that before 1763 the main cause of large planters' debt was buying capital assets, especially slaves, early in their careers ([Motives of Honor](https://books.google.com/books/about/Motives_of_Honor_Pleasure_and_Profit.html?id=k1rqCQAAQBAJ)). Planters' indebtedness to British and Glasgow merchants was among the grievances that contributed to the Revolution ([Glasgow History](https://glasgowhistory.co.uk/tobacco-lords)). Consolidation on the merchant side was strong: between 1685 and 1775 the number of British firms in the Chesapeake trade fell sharply, and the average firm grew ten to thirtyfold ([A Revolution of Scale in Overseas Trade](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-economic-history/article/abs/revolution-of-scale-in-overseas-trade-british-firms-in-the-chesapeake-trade-16751775/19731D90FF418BAFB17509250DB0AB4B)). Colonial credit flows, bills of exchange and total plantation debt in London are not quantified in the sources.

## Bound labour shifted to slavery, and scholars dispute why

Indentured servitude supplied most Chesapeake labour in the seventeenth century. Of **120,000 emigrants** to the Chesapeake in the 1600s, **90,000 were indentured servants** ([National Humanities Center](https://nationalhumanitiescenter.org/pds/amerbegin/permanence/text6/text6read.htm)). The shift to enslaved labour was rapid: Virginia had about 150 enslaved Africans in 1640 and nearly 3,000 forty years later (same source), and by 1700 most Chesapeake plantations had more enslaved than servant workers ([Tobacco in the American colonies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tobacco_in_the_American_colonies)). Free labour, by contrast, remained in short supply throughout the colonial period ([US Department of Labor](https://www.dol.gov/general/aboutdol/history/chapter1)).

Cost data explain some of the switch, but the picture is mixed. Galenson's average price for male servants with four years to serve was **£8.95**, and an article on Maryland reports that servant prices stayed steady through the eighteenth century ([Indentured Servitude in Eighteenth-Century Maryland](https://hssh.journals.yorku.ca/index.php/hssh/article/download/38469/34885/45705)). Another article argues that servant prices rose as slave prices fell, which made slaves relatively cheaper ([From Servants to Slaves](https://journals.calstate.edu/tthr/article/download/2496/2270/5139)). A Library Company essay argues that the annual cost of servants fell as more of them survived their terms, and that slavery did not replace servants evenly across the region ([Lorena Walsh and the Economic Interpretation](https://www.librarycompany.org/Economics/2011Conference/pdf/PEAES%20--%2011%20conf%20--%20Main%20ppr%203-16-11.pdf)). Upfront outfitting and transport costs of **£6 to £10** equalled about **75 to 130 percent** of median seventeenth-century Chesapeake incomes, though the excerpt's author is not identified ([delanceyplace](https://delanceyplace.com/view-archives.php?p=4651)).

Interpretations diverge sharply. Galenson presents indenture as an efficient market response to labour shortages ([University of Memphis thesis](https://digitalcommons.memphis.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=5123&context=etd)). Walsh argues that elite planters chose slavery deliberately and that most "did follow economically rational plans," although they also exploited the enslaved ([Motives of Honor, ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/260141016_Motives_of_Honor_Pleasure_and_Profit_Plantation_Management_in_the_Colonial_Chesapeake_1607-1763_by_Lorena_S_Walsh)). Coombs and Parent date that deliberate choice to as early as the 1650s ([Motives of Honor, ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/260141016_Motives_of_Honor_Pleasure_and_Profit_Plantation_Management_in_the_Colonial_Chesapeake_1607-1763_by_Lorena_S_Walsh)), and Walsh places the consolidation of slavery earlier than the 1670s ([Project MUSE](https://muse.jhu.edu/article/447896)). Walsh also finds that from the 1730s planters raised work requirements and used psychological and physical coercion, lengthening the workday ([American Historical Review review](https://academic.oup.com/ahr/article-abstract/116/1/170/42309?redirectedFrom=fulltext)). Northern labour followed a similar urban pattern: **New York City was over 40% enslaved by 1700** ([Bay Path University](https://open.baypath.edu/his114/chapter/northern-slavery/)), and Newport had at least 150 ships in the slave trade by 1740 ([American Yawp](https://www.americanyawp.com/text/04-colonial-society/)).

The strongest quantitative productivity claim comes from the antebellum South, not the colonial period. Wikipedia reports a qualified consensus that slave agriculture was efficient, citing a total-factor-productivity estimate that puts gang-system farms **35% ahead** of Northern free-labour farms, though other economic historians rejected that thesis ([Economics of slavery in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economics_of_slavery_in_the_United_States)). No source reviewed compares colonial slave, servant and wage productivity using account-book data, and no colonial wage series was retrieved.

## Land law, courts and paper money made credit tradable

Colonial land was allocated through channels that made it saleable. Virginia's headright system gave a patent, usually for 50 acres, for each person a sponsor brought in, including servants and slaves ([FamilySearch](https://www.familysearch.org/en/wiki/Virginia_Land_and_Property)). Headright claims could be assigned before patenting, and patents often came years later ([Reigel Ridge](https://www.reigelridge.com/Genealogy/Resources/Virginia-colonial-grants.shtml)). Sources differ on when the system ended. FamilySearch gives a range to 1732, while the Library of Virginia states that "treasury rights" from 1699 mostly replaced headrights by 1715 ([Library of Virginia](https://lva-virginia.libguides.com/land-grants)). Proprietary and quitrent grants separated political authority from land revenue. Virginia's quitrent was one shilling per 50 acres a year ([Virginia Places](http://www.virginiaplaces.org/settleland/headright.html)), but enforcement was weak ([Virginia Places, Fairfax Grant](https://www.virginiaplaces.org/settleland/fairfaxgrant.html)). In New England, towns passed from communal to private title over decades; in Cambridge, the first division of common land took place in 1662 ([FEE](https://fee.org/articles/the-puritan-experiment-in-common-ownership/)).

Speculation was extensive. One Virginia proprietary holding was described as "held for speculation," with only about **1% developed for farms**, though the source gives no date ([Virginia Places](http://www.virginiaplaces.org/settleland/headright.html)). Land companies avoided quitrent liability by not completing patents, and official warrants functioned as near-cash ([Virginia Places, Land Companies](https://www.virginiaplaces.org/settleland/landcompanies.html)). Land was also used to create money. Massachusetts formed a land bank in 1716 with £100,000 in notes ([Mises Institute](https://mises.org/library/origins-government-paper-money)). The private Massachusetts Land Bank of 1740 lent against mortgaged real estate, repayable over 20 years at 3% interest, and unwinding it caused financial distress ([October Research](https://www.octoberresearch.com/tuesdays-with-mary-america-on-credit)). The Crown and Parliament declared the 1740 schemes illegal and void ([Historic Ipswich](https://historicipswich.net/2021/07/20/land_bank_scheme/)). The Mises Institute, a libertarian outlet, says wealthy merchants and land speculators were among the most prominent backers of such schemes, and it cites Billias's finding that the poor-agrarian-debtor thesis did not hold ([Mises Institute, journal PDF](https://cdn.mises.org/2_4_8_0.pdf)).

Courts were central to enforcing exchange. Legal historians argue that colonial courts promoted a market society, and that unlike English courts, colonial courts regularly "chancered" bonds, while debt dockets were dominated by book-account cases ([Yale Law Journal](https://yalelawjournal.org/pdf/162_o78uo9t1.pdf)). Parliament's **1732 Debt Recovery Act** applied across the colonies and treated land, houses and slaves as chattels for credit purposes, allowing judicially supervised land auctions that English law did not permit ([Priest, Yale](https://law.yale.edu/sites/default/files/documents/pdf/sela/SELA11_Priest_CV_Eng_20110517.pdf)). Colonial courts put it into practice by expanding the writ of fieri facias ([Harvard Law Review](https://harvardlawreview.org/wp-content/uploads/2006/11/priest.pdf)). Before 1732, colonial legislatures set debt-collection procedures subject to imperial review, and falling tobacco and sugar prices in the late 1720s and 1730s changed that relationship ([Yale Open Law Server](https://openyls.law.yale.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/61c38fd1-3df2-486b-ba80-d2caf314fa9d/content)). Whether courts systematically favoured creditors is plausible but not established by the sources, and colonial probate and inheritance were not covered.

Paper money was the most debated form of colonial credit. E. James Ferguson called the practice of retiring colonial paper through taxes "currency finance." During the Seven Years' War, Pennsylvania, New York and South Carolina issued large amounts of paper with only moderate inflation, which the authors attribute to tax-based redemption ([Journal of Economic History](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-economic-history/article/abs/colonial-monetary-standards-contrasted-evidence-from-the-seven-years-war/1CE28AB2E39E97EE677C40A9CBF39C3C)). The Cleveland Fed similarly argues that inflation stayed subdued because mortgage and tax payments backed the notes, while colonies lost specie when they issued paper ([Federal Reserve Bank of Cleveland](https://www.clevelandfed.org/publications/economic-commentary/2015/ec-201506-paper-money-and-inflation-in-colonial-america)). The older critical account, from the Mises Institute, argues that paper drove specie out by Gresham's law, raised prices and hampered exports ([A History of Money and Banking](https://cdn.mises.org/History%20of%20Money%20and%20Banking%20in%20the%20United%20States%20The%20Colonial%20Era%20to%20World%20War%20II_2.pdf)). Later econometric work supports a more positive view. A study of New England's paper money from 1703 to 1749 finds that money-stock expansions had positive, significant effects on modern-sector activity ([NBER w16190](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w16190/w16190.pdf)). A study of Virginia's regime from 1755 to 1774 finds that a "moneyness" premium rose with circulation ([NBER w21881](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w21881/w21881.pdf)). In Maryland, tobacco-based book credit and bills of exchange drawn on London credits functioned as money ([NBER w13974](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w13974/w13974.pdf)). The Revolution's currency shows the stakes: Congress issued **$200 million** in Continental dollars from 1775 to 1779, **77% of congressional spending**, and one working paper argues that treating it as pure fiat is incomplete ([NBER w17276](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w17276/w17276.pdf)).
