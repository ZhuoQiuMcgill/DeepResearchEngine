# Structured literacy makes phonics explicit and sequenced

Structured literacy and typical practice differ most in how they teach word reading. Structured literacy teaches the sound-spelling system explicitly, in a planned sequence, with decodable texts and a screening-diagnosis-monitoring cycle. Typical practice, usually labelled balanced literacy in U.S. early grades, centres on read-alouds, guided reading from leveled texts and independent reading, with phonics often a minor part and cueing used for unknown words. The premise that most classrooms currently use these typical practices is not supported by the latest evidence. A 2019–2020 survey found about two-thirds of early-grade teachers reporting balanced literacy, but a 2025 national survey published in 2026 found only about half of K–3 teachers describing a structured approach and about one in three describing a balanced one ([K12 Dive](https://www.k12dive.com/news/teachers-knowledge-of-science-of-reading-improves-fordham-reports/818849/); [The 74](https://www.the74million.org/article/report-nearly-one-third-of-teachers-still-use-discredited-reading-methods/)). The best-supported research finding is that explicit, systematic phonics beats unsystematic or no phonics for decoding, while claims about comprehension and long-term gains remain contested. Moving to structured literacy means training of roughly 45 to 135 clock hours, new materials, protected instructional time and, in several places, new policy requirements.

## Balanced literacy's majority is no longer clear-cut

The earlier evidence for balanced literacy's dominance is strong but dated. In a 2019 Education Week survey, [Science News](https://www.sciencenews.org/article/balanced-literacy-phonics-teaching-reading-evidence) reports that 72% of early-elementary and special education teachers said their schools use balanced literacy, while [Education Week](https://www.edweek.org/teaching-learning/data-how-reading-is-really-being-taught/2019/12) reports 68% calling it their philosophy. The two figures measure different things, so the sources disagree on the exact share even within one year. The 2025–26 RAND survey for the Fordham Institute, of more than 1,200 K–3 teachers, uses different categories, so it is not a clean trend line, but it points away from balanced literacy as the majority practice ([Fordham Institute](https://fordhaminstitute.org/national/research/teachers-desk-science-reading-progress-report)). Stated beliefs also outrun classroom moves: [EdWeek](https://www.edweek.org/teaching-learning/how-the-science-of-reading-is-reshaping-teaching-what-the-data-say/2026/04) reports that 68% favour phonics, yet about 4 in 5 teachers say they would first help students break an unfamiliar word into parts, which is not the same as teaching it explicitly.

The gap is widest in high-poverty schools. [EdWeek](https://www.edweek.org/teaching-learning/how-the-science-of-reading-is-reshaping-teaching-what-the-data-say/2026/04) reports that teachers in high-poverty schools were more likely to say they used cueing, and [Chad Aldeman's analysis](https://www.chadaldeman.com/p/the-science-and-culture-of-reading) reports that 43% of high-poverty teachers did not express a clear phonics preference, versus 25% in low-poverty schools. Because "balanced literacy" has no agreed definition, a headcount of balanced teachers does not show what is taught. The 2020 EdWeek report itself notes there is no single definition and recommends observing classrooms ([Education Week](https://epe.brightspotcdn.com/1b/80/706eba6246599174b0199ac1f3b5/ed-week-reading-instruction-survey-report-final-1.24.20.pdf)). No observational study was found that establishes which approach most U.S. classrooms use.

## Structured literacy is defined by sequence and explicitness

The International Dyslexia Association's July 2024 working definition calls structured literacy "a comprehensive, integrated approach to reading and writing instruction" that builds on oral language and explicitly teaches the structure of written language ([IDA fact sheet](https://dyslexiaida.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/8_SL-Fact-Sheet.pdf)). Its domains are phonology, morphology, semantics, syntax and pragmatics, represented in writing as orthography. The IDA says structured literacy is an instructional approach rather than a single programme, and that isolated blocks of instruction "is not SL teaching" ([IDA fact sheet](https://dyslexiaida.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/8_SL-Fact-Sheet.pdf)). A six-element list that includes syllables and sound-symbol association circulates in secondary sources attributed to the IDA ([Really Great Reading](https://www.reallygreatreading.com/blog/what-structured-literacy)), but the 2024 fact sheet does not list those items, so the writer should cite the fact sheet for the definition.

The sequence is the main structural difference. IDA says "systematic" means a planned order of skills that should be adapted to the learner, not fixed ([IDA fact sheet](https://dyslexiaida.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/8_SL-Fact-Sheet.pdf)). Kentucky's guidance asks lessons to build from simple to complex concepts ([Kentucky Department of Education](https://www.education.ky.gov/curriculum/EarlyLiteracy/Pages/structured_literacy.aspx)). Explicit teaching means a direct explanation for each new concept ([Orton-Gillingham.com](https://www.orton-gillingham.com/what-is-structured-literacy/)). A typical lesson includes explicit instruction, modelling, guided practice, cumulative review, reading and spelling application, and ongoing assessment ([Really Great Reading](https://www.reallygreatreading.com/blog/what-structured-literacy)). Texts follow the taught code: [Wilson Language Training](https://www.wilsonlanguage.com/resources/articles/decodable-why-they-matter/) says most decodable text should contain taught phonics elements, with high-frequency irregular words introduced first.

Structured literacy also carries an assessment cycle. Universal screening identifies who needs support, diagnostic assessment specifies what they need, and progress monitoring checks whether the response works ([PLD](https://pld-literacy.org/glossary/universal-screening-or-literacy-screening-check/)). Maryland requires a statewide screener three times a year ([Morgan State NCEED](https://nceed.morgan.edu/reading-screeners-diagnostic-assessments-and-progress-monitoring/)), and Colorado's READ Act sets monitoring intervals by risk level ([Colorado Department of Education](https://www.cde.state.co.us/node/43709)). Typical practice usually lacks this formal cycle, though the sources do not measure its absence directly.

## Typical practice relies on cueing, leveled texts and embedded phonics

The two approaches differ in several dimensions at once, as summarised below.

| Dimension | Structured literacy | Typical balanced literacy |
|---|---|---|
| Word reading | Explicit, sequenced letter-sound teaching ([IDA fact sheet](https://dyslexiaida.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/8_SL-Fact-Sheet.pdf)) | Phonics often minor; cueing to unknown words ([Reading Universe](https://readinguniverse.org/article/explore-teaching-topics/big-picture/the-courage-to-teach-reading-differently)) |
| Texts | Decodable texts matched to taught code ([Scholastic](https://education.scholastic.com/education/solutions/decodables/what-are-decodable-texts.html)) | Leveled and predictable texts ([Reading in Action](https://www.readinginaction.org/science-of-reading-vs-balanced-literacy-what-changed-and-why-it-matters/)) |
| Routines | Lesson sequence built from scope and sequence ([95 Percent Group](https://www.95percentgroup.com/insights/what-is-structured-literacy-an-introduction-for-educators/)) | Read-alouds, shared, guided and independent reading, word study ([Reading in Action](https://www.readinginaction.org/science-of-reading-vs-balanced-literacy-what-changed-and-why-it-matters/)) |
| Unknown words | Decode using taught letter-sound patterns | Meaning, sentence structure and pictures, the "three cues" ([Mississippi Department of Education](https://www.mdek12.org/sites/default/files/Offices/MDE/OAE/OEER/Literacy/Literacy%20Live!%20Leaders/balanced_literacy_v_structured_literacy_slide_deck.pdf)) |

The three-cueing practice is the sharpest point of disagreement. Ontario's inquiry found the approach rested on Goodman's theory of how fluent adults read, and that the ministry could not show a sufficient scientific basis for it ([OHRC Right to Read report](https://www.ohrc.on.ca/sites/default/files/FINAL%20R2R%20REPORT%20DESIGNED%20April%2012.pdf)). Ohio's 2023 law removed three-cueing from the public-school core curriculum, and the Reading Recovery Council of North America filed a suit against the ban; the outcome was not found ([Wikipedia, Reading Recovery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reading_Recovery)). State counts differ: one source says at least 10 other states banned the method, listing them ([Ocpathink](https://ocpathink.org/post/independent-journalism/ban-on-three-cueing-in-reading-nears-final-passage)), while [KOMU](https://www.komu.com/news/state/missouri-lawmakers-ban-controversial-reading-instruction-model-as-primary-method/article_a97b5a7e-4ebd-4c74-b0a2-865accd2e576.html) reports at least 11 by the end of 2024. Advocates say cues are one source of information among several, and Fountas and Pinnell have distanced themselves from the three-cueing label, though no direct defence by them was retrieved ([Right to Read Project](https://righttoreadproject.com/2019/12/18/balanced-literacys-crumbling-foundation-what-we-can-do-about-it/)).

Whether balanced literacy includes direct phonics is disputed. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balanced_literacy) states that direct phonics instruction is included, and the Mississippi deck lists phonics taught in mini-lessons or word study. [Reading Universe](https://readinguniverse.org/article/explore-teaching-topics/big-picture/the-courage-to-teach-reading-differently) says phonics is usually not a focus, and a [Lis for Literacy](https://lisforliteracy.com/what-is-balanced-literacy/) blog calls systematic phonics a supplement. The report treats phonics as present in name but usually secondary in practice, because only 52% of balanced-literacy teachers in a 2019 survey included phonics in their definition ([Wikipedia, History of learning to read](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_learning_to_read)), and no official curriculum defines the term. The evidence does not settle the question for every school.

## Research favours phonics for decoding but not clearly for meaning

The main comparison is a 2025 exploratory analysis by Hansford et al. of 40 structured literacy and 28 balanced literacy studies. Its average effect was 0.43 unweighted and 0.46 weighted for structured literacy, against 0.19 and 0.29 for balanced literacy ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s44217-025-00922-8)). The authors call it exploratory and hypothesis-generating, because program selection was not systematic. Decoding showed the largest structured-literacy advantage (d = 0.44), while reading and comprehension differences were small and not statistically significant, and comprehension numerically favoured balanced literacy ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s44217-025-00922-8)). The paper also finds funnel-plot signs of small-study inflation and notes that many structured-literacy studies were company-sponsored. No large, long-term randomised trial comparing the two approaches was found.

The National Reading Panel's 2000 review is the foundation for the decoding claim. It found that systematic phonics produced greater gains than unsystematic phonics or no phonics and recommended it for routine classroom use ([NICHD](https://www.nichd.nih.gov/newsroom/releases/nrp)). It also found systematic phonics most effective for kindergarten and first-grade students at risk, with an overall effect of d = .44 ([Sparked Literacy summary](https://www.sparkedliteracy.com/post/the-national-reading-panel-on-systematic-phonics-instruction)). Stuebing et al. defend the systematic phonics effects against later re-analyses of the panel's data, calling them practically significant ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3024599/)). Bowers (2020), cited in the 2025 analysis, argues the advantage over whole-language instruction is small and that earlier meta-analyses did not show long-term comprehension gains ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s44217-025-00922-8)). The report follows the decoding finding and treats comprehension and long-term claims as unproven, because the 2025 analysis itself found no significant comprehension difference and the NRP's comprehension finding was limited to particular groups.

Evidence for balanced literacy rests heavily on Reading Recovery. May et al. report large short-term effects, and the regression-discontinuity analysis describes first-grade effects as large and similar to the randomised trial ([ERIC](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED628331.pdf)). The Literacy Council of North America disputes a 2022 regression-discontinuity study that found gains were not sustained through grades 3–4, and disputes that study's method ([myliteracycouncil.org](https://myliteracycouncil.org/revisiting-the-recently-reported-longitudinal-study-of-reading-recovery/)). The report follows the fading-gains reading, because the i3 scale-up fell from 1.17 at mid-first grade to 0.51 by the end of first grade and to 0.16 on the third-grade state test ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/303970638_Reading_Recovery_An_Evaluation_of_the_Four-Year_i3_Scale-Up)). A [Lis for Literacy](https://lisforliteracy.com/what-is-balanced-literacy/) blog also says the i3 gains did not persist, though as a secondary source, and the 2025 Springer paper reads the same regression-discontinuity results as fading.

Evidence for learners with dyslexia is weaker still. The 2025 analysis coded five systematic phonics studies with dyslexic samples and found no balanced-literacy studies with such samples ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s44217-025-00922-8)). For English learners, a 2024 Frontiers study found students with dyslexia gained as much as English-proficient peers, but it was observational and did not compare with balanced literacy ([Frontiers in Education](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2024.1495043/full)).

## Moving to structured literacy costs time, training and policy change

Costs are reported at system level more often than per student. California's 2025 budget set aside **$200 million** for a recommended list of professional development, with formula funding of about **$1,686 per TK-5 teacher** and listed programmes priced from **$199 to $1,400** ([EdSource](https://edsource.org/2026/teachers-training-science-of-reading/765498)). Louisiana's fiscal note estimates **$25.4 million a year** for 400 literacy coaches at $60,000 each ([Louisiana Legislature](https://legis.la.gov/legis/ViewDocument.aspx?d=1287478)). Per-teacher training prices vary: [Glean Education](https://gleaneducation.com/services/), an IDA-approved provider, charges **$1,280** for a 45-hour classroom-teacher course. Training hours follow IDA accreditation, which sets **45 clock hours** for classroom teachers, **90** for interventionists and **135** for specialists ([Illuminate Ed Therapy](https://www.illuminateedtherapy.com/resources/structured-literacy-training-programs-compared)). Districts also face materials costs, with a vendor claiming adoptions can reach hundreds of thousands of dollars ([Pride Reading Program](https://pridereadingprogram.com/science-of-reading-implementation-schools/)); this is a vendor's claim, not an audited figure. No independent per-student figure for a whole school was found.

Time is the other large change. Tasmania's government primary schools must teach at least **one hour** of structured literacy a day ([Tasmanian Liberal Government](https://tas.liberal.org.au/news/2025/07/09/giving-our-children-best-possible-start)), though this is a party policy statement. Victoria mandates **at least 25 minutes** of daily explicit phonics and phonemic awareness for Prep to Grade 2 ([NIFDI](https://www.nifdi.org/resources/hempenstall-blog/925-newspapers-are-showing-considerably-increased-interest-in-literacy-and-phonics.html)). Intervention programmes need more: one certification requires sessions of **45 minutes, four to five days a week** for small groups, and it reports that one district offering only 90 minutes a week does not meet its fidelity standard ([ESC 4](https://esc4.net/certification/dyslexia-interventionist-certification/)). Schools with a 60–90 minute whole-group block can often re-sequence it, but pull-out intervention schedules face larger changes. No independent study has compared instructional minutes with pupil outcomes.

Policy differs by place. In the U.S., **Pennsylvania** requires schools to adopt a structured literacy curriculum in grades K–3 by 2027–28 ([Mon Valley Independent](https://www.monvalleyindependent.com/2026/06/01/all-schools-are-required-to-adopt-structured-literacy-heres-what-some-teachers-already-using-it-say/)), and **Washington** requires districts buying K–4 curricula to follow structured literacy from September 2027 ([Chronicle](https://www.chronline.com/stories/new-law-focuses-on-literacy-in-the-early-grades,398939)). California's training is recommended rather than required ([EdSource](https://edsource.org/2026/teachers-training-science-of-reading/765498)). In Australia, mandatory phonics screening checks operate in several states ([Springer narrative review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s44020-026-00123-2)). In England, the Year 1 phonics screening check is statutory ([DfE administration guidance](https://dera.ioe.ac.uk/id/eprint/42044/1/2026%20phonics%20screening%20check%20administration%20guidance%20-%20GOV.UK.pdf)), and the 2026 expected standard is 31 out of 40 ([DfE statistics](https://explore-education-statistics.service.gov.uk/find-statistics/phonics-screening-check-attainment/2025-26)). England does not use the term "structured literacy" in its policy.

The reported risks are consistent. A 2022 Education Week report says not all teachers understand why the changes are needed, and a literacy-programme manager said training without classroom materials misses the mark ([Education Week](https://www.edweek.org/teaching-learning/why-putting-the-science-of-reading-into-practice-is-so-challenging/2022/07)). A 2025–26 Fordham report finds gaps in training, materials and implementation, especially in high-poverty schools ([Fordham Institute](https://fordhaminstitute.org/national/research/teachers-desk-science-reading-progress-report)). Implementation support mostly focuses on early elementary, and middle-school teachers may not be trained in decoding instruction ([K-12 Dive](https://www.k12dive.com/news/what-you-need-to-know-about-the-science-of-reading/830845/)). Ontario teachers in a 2024 study expected that stopping balanced-literacy word-reading practices would be harder than adopting structured literacy ([Educational Sciences](https://doi.org/10.3390/educsci14070791)). No independent evaluation of pupil progress during a transition was found.

## Conclusion

The most defensible answer to the question is that structured literacy differs from typical practice in what is taught, in the order it is taught and in how explicitly it is taught. The clearest difference is in word reading: structured literacy teaches the code directly, in a planned sequence, with texts that match what has been taught, while typical balanced practice relies more on meaning, pictures and leveled books. The evidence on decoding favours the structured side. The evidence on comprehension, long-term outcomes and the effect of balanced literacy specifically is thinner and disputed.

For schools, the practical implication is that the change is larger than a programme purchase. It requires sustained training, aligned materials, time in the timetable and a screening and monitoring cycle, and the evidence does not show that a quick switch will close gaps. For families, the practical question is whether a child's school is teaching the code explicitly and whether intervention is delivered at an adequate dose. Future research that matters most would be a long-term, controlled comparison of the two approaches for struggling readers and for older learners, areas the current evidence leaves largely unaddressed.
