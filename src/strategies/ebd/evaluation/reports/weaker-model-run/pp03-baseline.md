# Proving Vesuvius Destroyed Pompeii in 79

Bottom-line: The destruction of Pompeii is anchored to the AD 79 eruption of Mount Vesuvius, not Mount Etna. Eyewitness testimony and the volcanic-tephra record converge on Vesuvius as the responsible vent, with August 24, 79 CE as the prevailing anchor date and Pompeii buried by pyroclastic flows the following day. Misattribution to Etna persists in popular narratives, but authoritative museum and UNESCO materials explicitly correct this and reinforce Vesuvius as the culprit. The eruption unfolded in two main phases—a Plinian pumice-fall phase followed by pyroclastic-density-current surges—whose interaction with Pompeii’s geography and urban fabric produced the city’s rapid burial. Correct attribution to Vesuvius matters for understanding eruption dynamics, regional hazard context, and how ancient deposits are read in archaeology and education.

## Mount Vesuvius rather than Etna is the correct culprit and August 24, 79 CE is the anchor date

Mount Vesuvius, not Mount Etna, is the volcano responsible for Pompeii’s burial, a conclusion supported by the convergence of Pliny the Younger’s eyewitness letters and the Somma–Vesuvius tephra record. Pliny’s correspondence describes the eruption from Misenum and places the crisis in late August 79 CE, forming the cornerstone of dating and eruption-type in the Roman Bay of Naples context ([Pliny the Younger letters ( UCSD translation )](https://igppweb.ucsd.edu/~gabi/sio15/lectures/volcanoes/pliny.html); [USGS narrative on the 79 CE eruption; Pompeii burial](https://volcano.si.edu/volcano.cfm?vn=211020)). Contemporary syntheses consistently frame the eruption as beginning on August 24, 79 CE, with Pompeii buried by pyroclastic flows on the following day, a date that has become the standard anchor in museums and public-facing science ([USGS narrative on the 79 CE eruption; Pompeii burial](https://volcano.si.edu/volcano.cfm?vn=211020); [UNESCO Pompeii (World Heritage) page](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/829)). In contrast, credible linkages to Etna are not upheld by primary or widely accepted secondary sources; misattributions are typically noted as educational myths or media simplifications rather than evidence-based conclusions ([Pompeii Museum myths and facts](https://pompeii.museum/discover/pompeii-myths-and-facts/); [National Geographic article](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/history/article/mount-vesuvius-eruption-pompeii)).

## The eruption unfolded in a two-phase sequence that produced the burial

The Pompeii catastrophe is best understood as a two-phase event: an opening Plinian phase that produced a sustained pumice fall, followed by a Peléan-type phase with pyroclastic surges and flows that overran the town. This sequence is supported by both eyewitness-derived reconstructions and modern deposit studies, which describe a long Plinian column and pumice fallout lasting many hours, followed by increasingly energetic pyroclastic currents entering Pompeii and surrounding towns; a fourth surge reached the city around 7 a.m. on August 25 ([The Eruption of Vesuvius in AD 79: Reconstruction from Historical and Volcanological Evidence](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.2307/504292); [Pompeii Museum hour-by-hour timeline](https://pompeii.museum/discover/ad-79-eruption-hour-by-hour/)). The Plinian phase deposited notable pumice blankets, while later surges and dense currents (PDCs) produced the lethal burial pattern in urban fabric, including temperatures in the flows estimated at 240–340°C near the city core ([Lethal Thermal Impact at Periphery of Pyroclastic Surges: Evidences at Pompeii](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0011127); [The 79 CE eruption of Vesuvius: multidisciplinary reconstruction](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2006JB004444)). No lava flowed through Pompeii during AD 79, reinforcing the ash/ pumice and PDC sequence as the destruction mechanism ([Pompeii Museum hour-by-hour](https://pompeii.museum/discover/ad-79-eruption-hour-by-hour/)).

## Archaeological and geological evidence together demonstrate ash/pumice burial and pyroclastic flows

Pompeii’s stratigraphy shows a thick pumice-ash fall over the town, overlain by multiple pyroclastic-density-current deposits that culminated in the destruction of buildings and entombment of residents. Deposits include a white pumice fall (EU2f) and grey pumice (EU3f), followed by stratified ash/pumice units (EU3pf) emplaced by dilute PDCs, and then lithic-rich units (EU4) from a major PDC associated with column collapse; later, more dilute flows continued to overprint older layers ([agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2006JB004444)). The most destructive flow (EU4pf) could entrain heavy clasts and overlie earlier fall deposits, helping explain the complex burial pattern inside Pompeii ([agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2006JB004444)). Archaeological work features plaster casts of victims—over 1,000 recovered with around 104 casts documented in recent inventories—demonstrating rapid burial and preservation of perimortem postures in ash and pumice ([Pompeii History: The Casts](https://pompeii.museum/discover/); [Pompeii Sites: The Casts](https://pompeiisites.org/en/pompeii-map/analysis/the-casts/)). These lines of evidence align with a two-stage destruction mechanism that involved initial fallout followed by deadly pyroclastic currents, a model supported by both historical reconstructions and modern stratigraphy ([The Casts; Pompeii Archaeological Park communications](https://pompeii.museum/discover/pompeii-myths-and-facts/); [The Eruption of Vesuvius in AD 79: Reconstruction](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.2307/504292)).

## Misconceptions about Etna and how researchers address them

Misattribution to Etna persists in some popular outlets, but researchers and public-facing institutions correct this by foregrounding Vesuvius and the AD 79 timeline. Pompeii’s official site and museum materials explicitly address myths, reiterating the Vesuvian origin of the catastrophe and linking it to August 79 CE; UNESCO and major science outlets likewise emphasize Vesuvius as the responsible volcano ([Pompeii Myths vs Facts](https://pompeii.museum/discover/pompeii-myths-and-facts/); [UNESCO World Heritage Centre; Archaeological Areas of Pompeii](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/829); [National Geographic coverage](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/history/article/mount-vesuvius-eruption-pompeii)). When Etna is mentioned, it appears in broader discussions of ancient volcanology or as a comparative reference, not as the cause of Pompeii’s burial; the misattribution is thus contextual and not evidentiary ([National Geographic article](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/history/article/mount-vesuvius-eruption-pompeii); [Pompeii Museum myths and facts](https://pompeii.museum/discover/pompeii-myths-and-facts/)). Public education efforts—continuing museum outreach, UNESCO materials, and park communications—seek to reduce this misconception by foregrounding primary sources (Pliny’s letters), the tephra record, and the established excavation-based narrative of Vesuvius’s AD 79 eruption.

## Conclusion

The weight of historical accounts, archaeological data, and volcanological synthesis supports a Vesuvius-driven catastrophe in AD 79, not Etna. The eruption began with a prolonged Plinian pumice fall and was followed by multiple pyroclastic currents that overran Pompeii, with deposition and victim preservation reflecting rapid, high-temperature surges interacting with urban architecture. While popular media sometimes misattributes the destruction to Etna, authoritative museum, UNESCO, and scholarly sources consistently attribute Pompeii’s burial to Mount Vesuvius and the August 24, 79 CE timeline. The correction preserves the integrity of volcanology education, clarifies the regional hazard context for the Bay of Naples, and reinforces the link between Pliny’s eyewitness account and the modern stratigraphic interpretation. Recognizing the correct vent and sequence strengthens both public understanding and the scientific framework used to study ancient eruptions and their societal impacts.
