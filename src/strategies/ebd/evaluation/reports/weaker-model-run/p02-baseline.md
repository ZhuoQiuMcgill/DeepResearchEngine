# Disentangle insulin discovery and diabetes care

Insulin’s discovery and its early clinical use arose from a four-person Toronto team in 1921–1923, not Alexander Fleming. The first published description of the active pancreatic factor and its anti-diabetic effect appeared in Banting and Best’s February 1922 report, and the first successful human injections occurred January 23, 1922 (Leonard Thompson) [pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17582843/) [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7773348/). James Collip’s purification work in 1923 produced a safer, more refined extract suitable for broad clinical use; this purification is detailed in The Purification of Insulin and some of its Properties (Biochemical Journal, 1923) [portlandpress.com](https://portlandpress.com/biochemj/article-pdf/17/3/376/772062/bj0170376.pdf). The Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1923 largely recognized Banting and Macleod for the discovery of insulin, with Best and Collip not named as laureates in that award but later discussed in historical analyses [nobelprize.org](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/medicine/1923/banting/lecture/). Before insulin, mortality for youth-onset diabetes was extremely high; Joslin/Boston data show a dramatic decline in mortality after bovine insulin became available, from about 824 per 1,000 (1897–1914) to 61 per 1,000 (1922–1926) [niddk.nih.gov](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/-/media/Files/Strategic-Plans/Diabetes-in-America-3rd-Edition/DIA_Ch35.pdf?dkrd=www2.niddk.nih.gov). The Leonard Thompson case marked the clinical turning point and demonstrated feasibility and life-saving potential [fisherdigitus.library.utoronto.ca](https://fisherdigitus.library.utoronto.ca/exhibits/show/insulin100/early-patients/leonard-thompson). Over the medium term, insulin therapy rapidly progressed from crude animal extracts to purified preparations, enabling broader clinical use and eventually leading to the standardization of dosing units [bmj.com](https://www.bmj.com/content/bmj/1/3236/8.full.pdf). While Paulescu’s earlier 1921 pancrein work is acknowledged in some histories as a precursor, the mainstream discovery narrative centers on the Toronto team; this attribution is debated in modern scholarship [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8562980/) [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8919497). Throughout, Fleming is not cited as having a role in insulin research; his enduring legacy resides in penicillin, and credible histories do not attribute insulin work to him [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4520913) [sciencehistory.org](https://www.sciencehistory.org/education/scientific-biographies/alexander-fleming/).

## Did Alexander Fleming have any involvement in insulin research?

Alexander Fleming did not participate in insulin research or the early diabetes-care program. His celebrated contribution lies with penicillin, including Nobel recognition for antibiotics; no credible primary sources or histories assign insulin work to him [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4520913) [sciencehistory.org](https://www.sciencehistory.org/education/scientific-biographies/alexander-fleming/). The insulin discovery story is consistently framed as a Canadian, multi-person collaboration centered on Banting, Best, MacLeod, and Collip, with Paulescu as a historical precursor acknowledged by some scholars but not the primary discoverer in the mainstream account [nobelprize.org](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/medicine/1923/banting/lecture/) [Bliss, 1982](https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/D/bo258994839.html) [Rethinking the “discovery” of insulin, 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8562980/). The literature consistently places Fleming outside the insulin lineage, reaffirming that the insulin breakthrough followed a distinct research program in Toronto [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4520913).

## How did the immediate impact of insulin reshape prognosis and management when it first arrived?

Before insulin, prognosis for Type 1 diabetes was grim, with life expectancy often measured in months to a few years; contemporary syntheses describe diabetes as a rapidly fatal disease without insulin [nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41574-021-00542-w). After insulin’s introduction, population-level mortality fell dramatically; Joslin Clinic data show a striking reduction in deaths for children in the first decade of life from roughly 824 per 1,000 (1897–1914) to about 61 per 1,000 (1922–1926), a sixfold improvement associated with insulin availability [niddk.nih.gov](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/-/media/Files/Strategic-Plans/Diabetes-in-America-3rd-Edition/DIA_Ch35.pdf?dkrd=www2.niddk.nih.gov). The Leonard Thompson case (early 1922) established the practical feasibility of insulin therapy and signaled a turning point from fatal prognosis to life-saving treatment [fisherdigitus.library.utoronto.ca](https://fisherdigitus.library.utoronto.ca/exhibits/show/insulin100/early-patients/leonard-thompson). Early Toronto reports documented rapid metabolic stabilization; insulin could reduce hyperglycemia and acetone ketosis, with ketone bodies often disappearing within 24–48 hours of starting therapy, and improvements in energy, mood, appetite, and functional ability observed within days to weeks [bmj.com](https://www.bmj.com/content/bmj/1/3236/8.full.pdf). These gains occurred alongside continued dietary management and monitoring, highlighting that insulin complemented, rather than replaced, nutritional therapy in the 1920s [bmj.com](https://www.bmj.com/content/bmj/1/3236/8.full.pdf) [fisherdigitus.library.utoronto.ca](https://fisherdigitus.library.utoronto.ca/exhibits/show/insulin100/early-patients/leonard-thompson). While early access was limited by supply and cost, the rapid diffusion of insulin clinics in the 1920s–1930s demonstrates a swift shift in practice patterns as clinicians learned dosing and monitoring strategies, with broader adoption unfolding in the mid to late 1920s [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9852216/).

## How did insulin therapy evolve in formulations, delivery, monitoring, and overall management from 1920s to 2026?

Initial insulin therapy moved from crude, animal-derived extracts to purified preparations and standardized dosing units, enabling safer, more predictable treatment; this foundational transition set the stage for later innovation in formulations and devices. The first human administration (Leonard Thompson) in January 1922 marks the clinical start, followed by purification (Collip, 1923) that enabled broader use and safer dosing [pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17582843/) [portlandpress.com](https://portlandpress.com/biochemj/article-pdf/17/3/376/772062/bj0170376.pdf) [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7773348/). The 1923 Nobel Prize recognized Banting and MacLeod; Best and Collip’s roles are discussed in later scholarship that emphasizes four-way attribution in the invention and refinement process [nobelprize.org](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/medicine/1923/banting/lecture/) [Bliss, 1982](https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/D/bo258994839.html) [Rethinking the “discovery” of insulin, 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8562980/). Early insulin units were defined by animal-potency assays, and vials commonly contained roughly 4–5 units per mL in 1923, with later standardization moving toward 10 U/mL and other concentrations as manufacturing matured [bmj.com](https://www.bmj.com/content/bmj/1/3236/8.full.pdf) [diabetesjournals.org](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/41/12/2448/36513/John-A-Galloway-MD-FACP-A-Pharmaceutical-Physician). Dosing evolved from urine glucose/ketone-based monitoring to blood glucose testing; by the mid- to late-1920s, subcutaneous, meal-timed dosing with dietary planning and regular laboratory checks became common [bmj.com](https://www.bmj.com/content/bmj/1/3236/8.full.pdf) [jamanetwork.com](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/article-abstract/234300). The 1980s–1990s brought recombinant human insulin (Humulin; first marketed in 1982) and eventually rapid-acting and long-acting analogs (lispro 1996; glargine 2000) to improve pharmacokinetic predictability and patient flexibility [pubs.acs.org](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/cen-v060n045.p005) [accessdata.fda.gov](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/nda/pre96/020563Orig1s000rev.pdf). Insulin delivery diversified with pens in the mid-1980s and commercial pumps in the early 1980s, culminating in modern sensor-augmented and automated insulin-delivery systems that support tighter glycemic control [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9846406/) [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6205949/). Foundational trials like the DCCT established that intensive therapy reduces microvascular complications, shaping contemporary targets and fueling the push toward CGMs and automated insulin delivery in type 1 diabetes and, where appropriate, type 2 diabetes [nejm.org](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJM199309303291401) [diabetesjournals.org](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article-pdf/doi/10.2337/dci26-0122/894606/dci260122.pdf) [ADA Standards of Care 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12690173/). The modern landscape—recombinant human insulin, analogs, pens, pumps, CGMs, and closed-loop systems—reflects a long arc from unpredictable animal extracts to data-driven, highly individualized care; however, cost and access continue to shape real-world adoption, with ongoing device durability, payer coverage, and regional disparities driving policy responses [who.int](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240103092) [aspe.hhs.gov](https://aspe.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/documents/755e2d35906b9914df90eae64ab5cb0c/Insulin_Affordability_ASPE_Report_Final-Corrected.pdf) [novonordisk-us.com](https://www.novonordisk-us.com/media/news-archive/news-details.html?id=915073). ADA 2026 also explicitly endorses CGMs and automated delivery as standard components of care where appropriate, reflecting these technologies’ central role in contemporary diabetes management [diabetesjournals.org](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article-pdf/doi/10.2337/dci26-0122/894606/dci260122.pdf). The ongoing public-health context—global access, affordability, and supply chain stability—remains a crucial backdrop to this technical evolution [who.int](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240103092) [IDF Diabetes Atlas, 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11057359/).

## 1) What is the current consensus on responsibility for insulin discovery?

The contemporary consensus emphasizes a team-based attribution—Banting, Best, Collip, and MacLeod—supported by primary documents, with Paulescu acknowledged as a precursor in some analyses but not the mainstream discoverer; the 1923 Nobel Prize credited Banting and MacLeod, while later scholarship argues for fuller attribution that includes Best and Collip [nobelprize.org](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/medicine/1923/banting/lecture/) [Bliss, 1982](https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/D/bo258994839.html) [Rethinking the “discovery” of insulin, 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8562980/) [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8919497). Across major sources, Fleming is consistently described as uninvolved in insulin work; his renowned discovery lies in penicillin, not insulin [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4520913) [sciencehistory.org](https://www.sciencehistory.org/education/scientific-biographies/alexander-fleming/).

## 2) What delivery and monitoring innovations shaped insulin care in the 20th century?

Insulin’s clinical arc moved from animal-derived extracts to purified, standardized preparations; this shift allowed broader and safer use and laid the groundwork for later innovations in recombinant insulin and analogs. The first human use occurred in 1922, with Collip’s purification following in 1923, and the Nobel Prize recognizing Banting and MacLeod in 1923; early units were defined by animal-potency assays, with 4–5 units/mL in 1923 vials and later moving toward standardized concentrations such as 10 U/mL as manufacturing matured [pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17582843/) [portlandpress.com](https://portlandpress.com/biochemj/article-pdf/17/3/376/772062/bj0170376.pdf) [bmj.com](https://www.bmj.com/content/bmj/1/3236/8.full.pdf) [diabetesjournals.org](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/41/12/2448/36513/John-A-Galloway-MD-FACP-A-Pharmaceutical-Physician). The era also saw the turn toward clinician-dosed monitoring based on urine glucose/ketone testing and, later, blood glucose measurements as a standard part of dosage decisions [bmj.com](https://www.bmj.com/content/bmj/1/3236/8.full.pdf). The 1980s–1990s introduced recombinant human insulin (Humulin, first marketed 1982) and then rapid-acting and long-acting analogs (lispro 1996; glargine 2000) to improve pharmacokinetic predictability and patient flexibility [pubs.acs.org](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/cen-v060n045.p005) [accessdata.fda.gov](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/nda/pre96/020563Orig1s000rev.pdf). Delivery devices followed: insulin pens emerged in the mid-1980s and commercial pumps in the early 1980s, culminating in sensor-augmented and automated systems in the 2010s and 2020s [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9846406/) [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6205949/). The DCCT trial's demonstration that intensive insulin therapy reduces microvascular complications anchored the modern emphasis on precision and data-driven management, a trajectory that culminates in today’s CGM- and AID-enabled care [nejm.org](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJM199309303291401) [diabetesjournals.org](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article-pdf/doi/10.2337/dci26-0122/894606/dci260122.pdf).

## 3) How did global access and policy shape insulin use and outcomes?

Global access to insulin and related technologies remains highly uneven, with rising diabetes prevalence worldwide and persistent affordability and supply-chain challenges in LMICs. The IDF Diabetes Atlas (2021) projects 537 million people with diabetes, rising to 643 million by 2030 and 783 million by 2045, underscoring regional disparities and undiagnosed disease burdens [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11057359/). WHO’s 2023–2024 diabetes fact sheets and the Global Diabetes Compact (launched 2021) focus on improving access to essential medicines and strengthening primary care for diabetes worldwide [who.int](https://www.who.int/News-Room/Fact-Sheets/Detail/Diabetes). In the United States, affordability is shaped by policy and market dynamics, with ASPE (2026) detailing insulin cost-sharing and the effects of caps and rebates, and KFF summarizing state caps and payer-related trends [aspe.hhs.gov](https://aspe.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/documents/755e2d35906b9914df90eae64ab5cb0c/Insulin_Affordability_ASPE_Report_Final-Corrected.pdf) [kff.org](https://www.kff.org/health-costs/exploring-insulin-cost-sharing-caps-for-patients-with-private-coverage/). Industry actions—such as Novo Nordisk’s 2024 price reductions for U.S. insulins—also influence affordability, though payer coverage and rebates determine actual patient costs [novonordisk-us.com](https://www.novonordisk-us.com/media/news-archive/news-details.html?id=915073). The Inflation Reduction Act and related Medicare cost-sharing reforms further affect insulin access for older Americans with government coverage, illustrating the intertwined policy-and-pricing landscape shaping real-world use [uscode.house.gov](https://uscode.house.gov/us/usc/119-120/t42/s1395w-102/). Across these dynamics, ADA 2026 Standards of Care explicitly prioritize CGMs and automated insulin delivery where appropriate, signaling a shift toward technology-enabled care but also highlighting cost and access as ongoing considerations [diabetesjournals.org](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article-pdf/doi/10.2337/dci26-0122/894606/dci260122.pdf) [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12690173/).

## 4) What are the remaining challenges and policy responses shaping insulin today?

Despite proven therapeutic benefits, insulin access remains unequal, with cost, coverage design, and device-related expenses (sensors, infusion sets, pumps) driving adherence gaps and health disparities [kff.org](https://www.kff.org/health-costs/exploring-insulin-cost-sharing-caps-for-patients-with-private-coverage/) [who.int]. Global initiatives like the WHO Global Diabetes Compact seek to improve access to essential insulin and monitoring technologies, but country-level implementation varies and depends on health-system capacity and financing [who.int]. Industry actions, such as price reductions and negotiated pricing, interact with payer strategies to reduce out-of-pocket costs, yet patient costs remain a barrier for many privately insured individuals and those in low-resource settings [novonordisk-us.com]. In policy terms, cost-sharing caps, rebates, and emerging biosimilars may improve affordability but require ongoing vigilance to ensure access translates into sustained use and better outcomes across populations [aspe.hhs.gov] [kff.org]. The ADA’s 2026 standards underscore the balance between advancing technology-enabled care and ensuring equitable access, particularly for CGMs and automated insulin-delivery systems that carry significant upfront and ongoing costs [diabetesjournals.org] [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov].

## 5) What does the present-day standard of care look like, and how has it evolved since insulin’s discovery?

The standard of care has shifted from a life-saving but fragile intervention to a precision-based, technology-enabled paradigm. The DCCT established that intensive insulin therapy reduces microvascular complications and established a goal-oriented approach to glycemic control; contemporary guidelines from ADA/EASD emphasize early adoption of CGMs and automated insulin delivery, with continued use of multiple delivery modes (MDI and CSII) tailored to patient needs [nejm.org](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJM199309303291401) [diabetesjournals.org](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article-pdf/doi/10.2337/dci26-0122/894606/dci260122.pdf) [ADA Standards of Care 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12690173/). The industry-and-policy landscape continues to shape access, including global pricing pressures, public-insurance reforms, and differential adoption across regions; yet the overall trajectory is toward safer, more effective and individualized therapies with real-time data to guide decisions [who.int] [aspe.hhs.gov] [kff.org]. The long arc—from early animal extracts to recombinant and analog formulations, to pens and pumps, to CGMs and automated delivery—has dramatically improved time in range and quality of life for many patients, while highlighting persistent inequities in global access and affordability that policy and industry must continue to address.

Conclusion
- Insulin’s discovery is best understood as a collaborative breakthrough by Banting, Best, MacLeod, and Collip, with Fleming’s work firmly unrelated to insulin and instead iconic for penicillin. The discovery catalyzed a rapid reduction in mortality for youth-onset diabetes and opened a therapeutic era that evolved from crude animal extracts to purified biologics, recombinant human insulin, and rapid- and long-acting analogs, alongside devices that enable precise dosing and continuous monitoring. This progression—coupled with large-scale public-health efforts and policy interventions—delivered transformative improvements in prognosis, daily living, and life expectancy for people with diabetes, even as access and affordability remain critical barriers in many regions. While the science proved incredibly enduring, ongoing work is needed to ensure equitable access to the technologies that define modern glucose management and to sustain gains through policy action, price transparency, and continued innovation.
