# Debunk Fleming insulin myth, transform care

The central finding is that Alexander Fleming did not discover insulin; the discovery and early clinical use are widely attributed to Banting, Best, Collip, and Macleod at the University of Toronto in 1921–1922, with Nobel recognition in 1923. Fleming is historically associated with penicillin, not insulin, and major histories consistently name the Toronto team for insulin while noting debates about credit among its four members. The misattribution to Fleming appears mainly in popular accounts rather than authoritative histories or Nobel records. The discovery translated rapidly into clinical practice, beginning with the first human use in January 1922 and culminating in mass production and broad adoption by the mid-1920s, transforming diabetes from rapidly fatal to a manageable condition for many patients. Yet even as insulin’s benefits became clear, global access remained uneven, shaped by price, supply, regulatory pathways, and health-system capacity, prompting ongoing policy efforts toward universal access. In short, the insulin story is a cautionary tale about credit and collaboration as much as it is about scientific breakthroughs and health-system change.

Discovery and attribution of insulin

Insulin is credited to Frederick Banting and Charles Best, with John J. Macleod and James B. Collip providing essential refinements, at the University of Toronto in 1921–1922; the first purified insulin and its early clinical use followed, leading to the 1923 Nobel Prize awarded to Banting and Macleod. ([fda.gov](https://www.fda.gov/about-fda/fda-history-exhibits/100-years-insulin)) The Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for 1923 was awarded to Banting and Macleod “for the discovery of insulin,” with Best and Collip not named as Nobel laureates in 1923; this attribution remains a focal point of historical discussion. ([nobelprize.org](https://www.nobelprizes.org/prizes/medicine/1923/banting/lecture/)) Major histories and archival materials from the University of Toronto emphasize the four-person collaboration and ongoing debates about how credit should be allocated among Banting, Best, Macleod, and Collip. ([press.uchicago.edu](https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/D/bo258994839.html)) UNESCO/Memory of the World materials recount the Toronto team’s work, the testing on patients, and the Nobel Prize outcome as canonical attribution, while noting later debates about credit. ([unesco.org](https://www.unesco.org/en/memory-world/discovery-insulin-and-its-worldwide-impact)) Inferences from primary sources and major reviews converge on a four-person Toronto collaboration as the core narrative, with the Nobel Committee emphasizing Banting and Macleod; Best and Collip contributed crucially to purification and early clinical deployment. ([nobelprize.org](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/lists/all-nobel-laureates-in-physiology-or-medicine/all/)) The evidence does not support attributing insulin’s discovery to Fleming; the misattribution appears in some popular accounts but is not supported by authoritative histories or Nobel records. ([nobelprize.org](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/medicine/1923/banting/lecture/)) The record shows that Fleming is historically associated with penicillin (discovered in 1928) rather than insulin; this distinction is clarified in established bioscience histories. ([nobelprize.org](https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/fleming-lecture.pdf)) Gaps remain in resolving every contributor’s precise share of credit, though primary materials (Nobel records, CMAJ 1922 article) support the four-person collaboration as the core narrative. ([fisherdigitus.library.utoronto.ca](https://fisherdigitus.library.utoronto.ca/exhibits/show/insulin100/first-clinical-trials))

Early impact on diabetes treatment

Before insulin, diabetes care relied on drastic dietary restriction and non-curative measures; insulin introduced a pharmacologic, exogenous means to control glucose, reversing key derangements and transforming prognosis and daily care. The pre-insulin era emphasized carbohydrate restriction and malnutrition-focused regimens, with opium and other palliative practices used in some settings. ([fda.gov](https://www.fda.gov/about-fda/fda-history-exhibits/100-years-insulin)) The first human insulin treatment produced rapid metabolic improvements (downshift in hyperglycemia and ketosis) after purification, with the initial dose causing an injection-site abscess before safer preparations became available. ([jci.org](https://www.jci.org/articles/view/142239)) Purification by James Collip markedly reduced toxicity from crude extracts and enabled safe testing in humans, a turning point in making insulin usable clinically. ([fda.gov](https://www.fda.gov/about-fda/fda-history-exhibits/100-years-insulin)) Early dosing regimens involved relatively large daily total units and a few injections per day, with injections traditionally given before meals and sometimes accompanied by post-injection carbohydrate measures like milk to reduce hypoglycemia. ([bmj.com](https://www.bmj.com/content/bmj/1/3412/895.full.pdf)) Treatments progressed to “series” regimens that extended intervals between injections as glycosuria and ketonuria improved, and dosing strategies evolved with an eye to safety and metabolic control. ([bmj.com](https://www.bmj.com/content/bmj/1/3412/895.full.pdf)) Evidence shows that insulin quickly shifted care from dietary restriction and palliative approaches to a targeted pharmacologic therapy capable of reversing key diabetic derangements, enabling earlier, more aggressive management and daily life adjustments. Guidance from early reports and later reviews underscores how purification and dosing strategies were essential for safe bedside use. ([fda.gov](https://www.fda.gov/about-fda/fda-history-exhibits/100-years-insulin)) Gaps exist in globally representative early dosing data beyond illustrative regimens; most sources highlight specific examples or milestones rather than a continent-wide atlas. ([bmj.com](https://www.bmj.com/content/bmj/1/3412/895.full.pdf))

Global adoption, access, and policy

Insulin’s availability and access have evolved globally, but substantial regional gaps persist due to pricing, supply constraints, regulatory pathways, and health-system capacity. International bodies (WHO, IDF) document ongoing barriers, while major regulatory milestones (from the 1920s to the 1980s–1990s and into the biosimilar era) shaped where and how insulin is produced, approved, and priced worldwide. Advocacy groups and sustained policy efforts continue to press for universal and affordable access. ([who.int](https://www.who.int/news/item/12-11-2021-new-who-report-maps-barriers-to-insulin-availability-and-suggests-actions-to-promote-universal-access)) Global findings identify high prices, limited human-insulin supply, and market concentration as persistent barriers; WHO outlines concrete actions to improve availability and affordability, including quality-assured insulin and delivery devices. ([who.int](https://www.who.int/news/item/25-09-2020-changing-the-game-to-improve-availability-and-affordability-of-quality-insured-insulin-and-associated-devices)) In 2019–2020, WHO launched a prequalification programme to quality-inspect and prequalify insulin to expand supply in low- and middle-income countries, a major regulatory step toward broader access. ([who.int](https://www.who.int/news/item/13-11-2019-who-launches-first-ever-insulin-prequalification-programme-to-expand-access-to-life-saving-treatment-for-diabetes)) The 2021 WHO report maps barriers and proposes actions toward universal access, reinforcing that gaps remain despite policy momentum. ([who.int](https://www.who.int/news/item/12-11-2021-new-who-report-maps-barriers-to-insulin-availability-and-suggests-actions-to-promote-universal-access)) The IDF Atlas (2025) documents country-level availability and supply challenges, illustrating persistent regional disparities in insulin access and diabetes supplies. ([diabetesatlas.org](https://diabetesatlas.org/media/uploads/sites/3/2025/02/IDF-Atlas-2nd-Edition.pdf)) The rise of recombinant human insulin (Humulin, 1982) and rapid-acting analogs (lispro, 1996) expanded production and diversification, while biosimilars emerged in the late 2010s and early 2020s, with regulatory milestones detailed in FDA histories. ([fda.gov](https://www.fda.gov/about-fda/fda-history-exhibits/100-years-insulin)) Pricing and affordability have been uneven; US studies document substantial price increases in the 2000s and 2010s, with some later reductions in net costs via programs, but overall affordability remains a multi-faceted policy issue. ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4886177/)) The ADA and IDF emphasize ongoing affordability challenges and advocacy for caps, coverage, and biosimilar competition as central policy levers; other analyses highlight payer, formulary, and supply-chain dynamics shaping access. ([diabetes.org](https://diabetes.org/tools-resources/affordable-insulin)) In short, insulin’s global availability has improved substantially since its discovery, but widespread universal access remains an unfinished policy goal, requiring coordinated regulation, pricing strategies, and supply-chain resilience. ([who.int](https://www.who.int/news/item/12-11-2021-new-who-report-maps-barriers-to-insulin-availability-and-suggests-actions-to-promote-universal-access))

Lessons for today

Historical lessons emphasize collaborative, multidisciplinary teams and transparent attribution, illustrating how bench discoveries translate into bedside care and how policy can shape access to life-saving therapies. Bliss’s canonical account on insulin frames the collaboration among Banting, Best, Collip, and Macleod as a model of team science, informing contemporary discussions about credit, collaboration, and translation of research into practice. ([press.uchicago.edu](https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/D/bo258994839.html)) Contemporary reviews and institutional histories reinforce that Nobel recognition can reflect selective attribution even when the broader team’s contributions are substantial and ongoing debates persist about fair credit. ([deptmedicine.utoronto.ca](https://deptmedicine.utoronto.ca/sites/default/files/insulin_discovery_jci_4th_jan_2021.pdf)) The modern policy agenda echoes the historical arc: sustaining affordability, expanding access through biosimilars and price-cap strategies, and integrating new technologies (analogs, pumps, CGMs) into equitable care. ([diabetesjournals.org](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/doi/10.2337/dc26-0300/172127/State-Insulin-Out-of-Pocket-Cap-Policies-and)) Stakeholders—including the ADA, IDF, and JDRF—continue to push for universal access, price transparency, and robust supply chains as essential conditions for health equity. ([diabetes.org](https://diabetes.org/tools-resources/affordable-insulin)) These insights imply that policy design should anticipate both scientific innovation and cost barriers, ensuring that advances translate into durable health gains for diverse populations. ([commonwealthfund.org](https://www.commonwealthfund.org/publications/issue-briefs/2020/sep/not-so-sweet-insulin-affordability-over-time)) Gaps remain in consistently applying these lessons across countries, given divergent health systems and market structures; ongoing cross-national analysis and targeted policy action are needed to move toward universal access. ([who.int](https://www.who.int/news/item/12-11-2021-new-who-report-maps-barriers-to-insulin-availability-and-suggests-actions-to-promote-universal-access))

Conclusion

The insulin story demonstrates how an accurate attribution and equitable translation of a breakthrough matter as much as the science itself. Correcting the historical record about insulin’s discovery foregrounds Banting, Best, Macleod, and Collip, while clarifying Fleming’s role in penicillin helps prevent misperceptions that can muddy contemporary discussions about credit, collaboration, and accountability in science. Beyond attribution, the history underscores that life-saving therapies require robust health systems, affordable pricing, and resilient supply chains to deliver real-world benefits—an agenda that remains urgent in the ongoing push toward universal, affordable insulin access and equitable diabetes care worldwide.
